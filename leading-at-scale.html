Leading at Scale

I have spent eighteen years representing a utility before the General Assembly, the administration and state agencies. I lead without a title every day, and I build systems that make a team faster rather than relying on effort alone. I am ready to lead a larger team and support a larger organization.

This page makes that case: how my experience translates, the systems I build, how I would lead a larger team, and honest answers to the hardest questions.

Systems, not heroics

The strongest proof that someone can lead a bigger team is that they already build things that make other people faster.

Issue and expert routing workbook

Problem. Bills were routed to internal experts by memory and an outdated list, so some sat for days.

System. A workbook that maps every utility topic to the right leader, department or cross-functional team, with suggested tags colleagues confirm from the bill summary.

Why it scales. A new team member can route a bill correctly on day one. Speed no longer depends on who has been around longest.

Bill dashboards and trackers

Problem. Session information lived in inboxes and spreadsheets.

System. Dashboards for assigned bills and the full tracked list, with stage, position and context in one place.

Why it scales. Leadership sees the same picture the team sees, without a meeting.

A multi-year reliability campaign

Problem. A complex campaign to keep generating capacity available involved many teams and many audiences.

System. A single tracker as the source of truth, a weekly cross-functional call, district-level fiscal analysis and a legislator power map.

Why it scales. Many teams move as one. That is the director's job at scale.

A foresight concept tested and right-sized

Problem. I believed a formal legislative forecasting function could help.

System. I tested it with a dataset of several hundred bills across six sessions, then recommended two small process changes instead of a new function.

Why it scales. Judgment about what not to build saves an organization money and attention.

How I would lead: the five Cs

1

Clarity

Everyone knows the three priorities, who owns what, and what success looks like this session.

2

Cadence

A predictable weekly rhythm, so information flows without heroics.

3

Capability

Each person grows through real reps, and the bench is deep enough to survive turnover.

4

Credibility

Members trust our facts because they are right, every time, in public and in private.

5

Compliance

A simple system the whole team follows, built with compliance, not around it.

The hard questions

Say your answer out loud before you open the suggested one. A timer runs while you speak.

▸Your supervisory experience is from earlier in your career.

Suggested answer. Adjust it to what is true for you.

That's fair, and I'd rather address it directly. I led a team of seven for seven years, managed a board, outside counsel and accountants, and handled hiring and performance. Then I spent four years as a senior HR consultant advising leaders through realignments, discipline, terminations and succession planning, so I know the people side of management in depth. Since then I've led through influence every day, directing contract lobbyists, running cross-functional groups and serving as my department's lead on data center, storage and decarbonization bills. Leading without authority is harder than leading with it. I'm ready to do it with a team that reports to me.
▸You've been an individual contributor. Can you let go and delegate?

Suggested answer. Adjust it to what is true for you.

I know the trap of staying the team's best lobbyist, and I'm deliberately working on it by handing off whole outcomes, not tasks, with a clear finish line. The tools I've built, like the routing workbook, exist so work doesn't depend on me. A director's job is to make the team faster, and that's the job I want.
▸A larger organization is more complex. How will you handle the matrix?

Suggested answer. Adjust it to what is true for you.

I already work across a multi-company enterprise, with sister utilities and a transmission company, and I coordinate subject matter experts across all of them. The approach scales: learn each leader's priorities first, agree on decision rights, and set a cadence everyone can rely on. More stakeholders means the system matters more, and systems are what I build.
▸Your relationships are concentrated in certain regions and caucuses.

Suggested answer. Adjust it to what is true for you.

I've worked with members from every part of the state over ten General Assemblies, including members I've known for more than fifteen years. Where I have less depth, I'll invest first. My first 30 days would include meetings with every energy committee chair, minority spokesperson and the members I know least.
▸What would your team say is your weakness?

Suggested answer. Adjust it to what is true for you.

They'd probably say I take on too much myself during session. It's a real habit, and I've been working on it by deciding at the start of each week what only I can do and handing off the rest with deadlines. As a director that discipline isn't optional, and I'm committed to it.

The 60-second case

I've represented a utility in Springfield for eighteen years, across ten General Assemblies and four governors, from EIMA through CRGA. Today I'm my department's lead on data center, battery storage and decarbonization bills, which means leading economic development, clean energy and rates teams that don't report to me. I direct contract lobbyists, brief executives and coordinate the company's subject matter experts. I also build the systems that make a team faster, like a routing workbook that gets each bill to the right expert quickly. Earlier in my career I ran a program for 25,000 union members, led a team of seven, and then advised leaders on people decisions as an HR consultant. I'm ready to bring all of that to leading a larger team.
