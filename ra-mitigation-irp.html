Executive summary

Illinois has issued two separate products that share one set of E3 models. The RA Mitigation Plan asks a narrow question under the Environmental Protection Act. Should 2030 CEJA emission deadlines be delayed to keep the lights on? The Integrated Resource Plan asks a broad one under the Public Utilities Act. What will Illinois need through 2047, and what should the ICC direct to get it?

Both reach the same core finding. Data center growth is outrunning new accredited capacity in PJM and MISO, and LRZ 4, the central and southern Illinois zone served mostly by Ameren Illinois, is short first. The 2025 RA Study showed a 0.31 GW surplus for LRZ 4 in 2030. The new analysis shows a 3.91 GW shortfall before imports, and the rest of MISO may have nothing to spare.

The Mitigation Plan responds with one targeted action. It delays the 2030 CEJA deadlines for 11 gas and oil peakers, including Venice and Tilton in the Ameren zone, to May 31, 2033. The IRP responds with a framework. It finds that wind, solar, storage, load flexibility and retaining existing gas all help, and it leaves specific mandates to agency testimony in an ICC docket.

For Ameren Illinois, three points matter most.

  1. The Mitigation Plan helps but only partially. It covers 567 MW of the 2,097 MW ORG fleet. Goose Creek, Kinmundy, Pinckneyville and Raccoon Creek carry 2040 CEJA dates and are outside its scope.
  2. The IRP is the bigger arena. Its record shows gas retention saves about $14.2 billion statewide. Its order, expected around May 2027, can modify the Mitigation Plan and direct utility program changes effective January 1, 2028.
  3. Both clocks run through the 2027 spring session. Mitigation Plan comments are due about January 12, 2027. One consistent fact base should drive comments, ICC testimony and legislative outreach.

3. Energy versus capacity

Energy is the electricity actually used, like miles driven. Capacity is the promise that a plant will be ready when needed, like keeping a spare car in the garage. Grid operators require enough capacity to cover the peak hour plus a margin, and they pay for it through auctions. Many of the plants in these reports matter for capacity, not energy. They sit idle most of the year, but the system needs them on the few hours that matter most.

5. Illinois's clean energy commitments

CEJA (2021) set a goal of 100% clean energy by 2050, expanded the Renewable Portfolio Standard and required fossil plants to cut emissions to zero on deadlines between 2030 and 2045. It also built in the resource adequacy study and Mitigation Plan as a safety valve. CRGA (2026) added statewide integrated resource planning so reliability, cost, emissions and equity are weighed together instead of one at a time. These two laws are why there are two plans.

The central tension

Illinois wants to cut emissions on a fixed schedule and keep power reliable and affordable while demand grows. The Mitigation Plan and the IRP are the state's attempt to do all three at once. Most debates on this page come down to how to balance them.

Two products, one engine

The same E3 modeling platform feeds both products, but each one answers to a different statute, a different author group and a different ICC decision.

Why there are two

CEJA (2021) created a two step safety valve in Section 9.15(o) of the Environmental Protection Act. Step one was the 2025 RA Study. Because that study found a shortfall reasonably likely, step two requires IPA and IEPA to write a Mitigation Plan. Separately, CRGA (effective June 1, 2026) created a permanent statewide IRP in Sections 16-201 and 16-202 of the Public Utilities Act.

Why it matters

The Mitigation Plan can only change emission deadlines. It cannot launch a procurement or a program. The IRP is where the ICC gets authority to direct IPA procurements, utility energy efficiency and Multi Year Integrated Grid Plans. Public Act 104-0477 ties them together by letting the ICC modify an approved Mitigation Plan through its IRP order.

Background

Two laws are behind the two products. The Climate and Equitable Jobs Act (CEJA, 2021) set deadlines for fossil plants to stop emitting carbon, and included a safety valve if those deadlines threatened reliability. The Clean and Reliable Grid Affordability Act (CRGA, effective June 1, 2026) created statewide integrated resource planning, something many states have long done for vertically integrated utilities, but adapted for Illinois's competitive market.

Why it matters

Knowing which law a request falls under tells you who decides, what test applies and what can actually change. A good argument under the wrong statute goes nowhere.

Say it like thisabout 40 seconds

Think of it as one engine with two steering wheels. The state's consultant, E3, built one set of models. The Mitigation Plan comes from the 2021 climate law and can do only one thing, which is delay emission deadlines for specific plants. The IRP comes from this year's grid affordability law, and it gives the Commerce Commission power to direct real programs like energy efficiency, power procurement and utility grid plans. The link that matters is this. When the Commission rules on the IRP, it can go back and change the Mitigation Plan. So the IRP decision, expected around May 2027, may have the final word.

The reliability problem

Load is growing faster than accredited capacity is being added, and Illinois cannot count on its neighbors to make up the difference.

  • Data centers. In the Reference Case, LRZ 4 data center load reaches about 44 TWh by 2050, nearly half of the zone's roughly 90 TWh.
  • Supply that is not arriving. Planned projects keep dropping out of interconnection queues. In LRZ 4, installed capacity expected for 2030 fell 9.8 GW between the 2025 RA Study and the 2026 analysis.
  • Slow replacements. Large gas turbines now take more than five years from order to delivery, and prices rose from about $2,000 to $3,000 per kW in six months, per EPRI research cited in the plan. Without retained units, Illinois would need to add resources at more than three times its historical annual pace.
Import capability is not import availability

LRZ 4 has transmission room to import, but permission to import does not mean surplus capacity exists elsewhere in MISO during a stressed hour. When both RTOs are short, a zone that balances on paper can still come up short.

Learning corner
Background

Grid operators plan so that a shortage serious enough to cut customers happens no more than once in ten years, a standard written as 0.1 LOLE. They translate that into a reliability requirement, which is peak demand plus a reserve margin. Each power source is then counted by how much it can be relied on at the riskiest hours, not by its nameplate size. A solar farm counts for little on a winter evening, and a wind farm counts for little on a still, frigid night. That counting is called accreditation, and MISO and PJM have recently made it stricter.

Why it matters

When people say Illinois is short, they mean accredited capacity is below the requirement. Retiring a plant, adding a data center, or a rule change that lowers accreditation all widen that gap.

Why can LRZ 4 look fine on paper and still be at risk?

Its import limit measures whether power can be delivered, not whether anyone has spare power to sell. If all of MISO is short at the same hour, there is nothing to import.

Why does winter matter more in the Ameren zone?

The model shows the Ameren zone's risk falls in December through February, when demand is high and wind output can drop sharply on very cold nights.

Say it like thisabout 40 seconds

The problem is easy to say. We're adding demand faster than we're adding dependable supply. For our zone, the state's 2025 study showed a small surplus in 2030. The new numbers show a shortfall of almost four gigawatts before imports. Three things changed. Data center demand went up, a lot of planned projects dropped out, and new gas turbines now take more than five years to get. And here's the catch. Being allowed to import power doesn't mean there's power to import. If all of MISO is short on the same cold night, there's nothing extra to buy.

How the agencies decided

Section 9.15(o) requires the agencies to show the need persists, rule out timely alternatives, and limit any delay to what is necessary. They built the decision around five sequential tests.

The agencies stress these tests are a structured reading of the evidence, not a scoring system. Tests 2 and 4 are where debate will focus, on whether alternatives can arrive faster and how to weigh local air impacts that were not modeled.

Learning corner
Background

Section 9.15(o) does not let the agencies extend plants simply because it helps. They must show the shortage persists, consider renewables, storage, demand response and transmission, and limit any delay to the smallest extent and shortest time necessary. The ICC then approves only if the plan meets the shortfall with the least emissions, considers environmental justice communities and supports the lowest total cost over time.

Why it matters

This test is the yardstick every commenter will use. Arguments that fit one of the five tests carry weight. Arguments outside them tend to be set aside.

Key terms
Minimum extent standardThe rule that any delay must be no larger or longer than needed
Environmental justice (EJ) communityA community the state identifies as carrying a disproportionate pollution burden
CopollutantsPollutants released with CO2, such as nitrogen oxides, sulfur dioxide and particulates
Check yourself
Why was coal left out even though keeping it saves more money?

The extra savings were small, about $1.3 billion more over the full period, while coal emits far more per MW. The minimum extent and least emissions tests point to gas and oil only.

Say it like thisabout 40 seconds

The agencies didn't just ask whether old plants help. The law makes them walk through five tests. Is the shortage real? Can anything else fix it in time? Do the plants actually help? What are the cost and pollution tradeoffs? And what's the smallest action that works? Those answers led to a narrow result. Keep 11 gas and oil peakers, leave coal out, and only until May 2033. Expect critics to push hardest on the second and fourth tests, arguing that batteries and demand response can arrive faster and that local air impacts weren't studied.

Learning corner
Background

CEJA sets deadlines for fossil units to reach zero emissions or retire, ranging from 2030 to 2045 depending on fuel, emissions, location and ownership. The units in this plan are peakers, combustion turbines that start in minutes and run only during the tightest hours. Most run very little, which is why the plan treats them as insurance. The plan does not order anyone to keep a plant open. It only removes the legal deadline for a time. Owners still decide whether to maintain, staff and offer the plant into the market.

Why it matters

An extension is permission, not a guarantee. Whether Venice and Tilton actually stay available depends on owner decisions, market revenue and maintenance spending, which the plan does not estimate.

No. They keep the same role as backstops. The state's modeling shows Ameren zone units needed in about one year in five, usually for under ten hours.

The proposal keeps 11 peaker plants available until May 31, 2033. Nine run on gas and two on oil. Two of them, Venice and Tilton, are in the Ameren zone. The IRP is a planning framework, not a construction plan. It tests 22 cases, picks no single preferred portfolio, and says the agencies will put specific recommendations in ICC testimony.

  2. Illinois can still reach a reliable, clean system with proper planning and a diverse portfolio.

Learning corner
Background

In states where utilities own power plants, an IRP picks what the utility will build. Illinois is different. Since the late 1990s, generation here is owned by independent companies that compete in wholesale markets, and ComEd and Ameren Illinois deliver power rather than generate it. So Illinois's IRP cannot order plants built. Instead it estimates what the market will need and points state tools at the gaps, such as renewable procurements, storage targets, energy efficiency and utility grid programs.

Why it matters

This is why the IRP's power runs through programs and procurements, and why its order can change utility plans. It is also why the report shows ranges across 22 futures rather than a single answer.

Why does the IRP recommend keeping options open on nuclear and long duration storage instead of committing now?

The IRP isn't a construction plan. It's a map of what Illinois may need and the tools the state can use to get there. Across 22 scenarios, a few things show up every time. The most common mistake

A system cost saving is not the same as a bill saving. The reports say this themselves. System costs and capacity estimates measure changes in market payments and investment, not retail rates. How much reaches a bill depends on how costs are split among customer classes and how much each customer uses.

2. Capacity, the part that drives the $160 million

Energy is the electricity you actually use, measured in megawatt hours. Capacity is a promise that a power plant will be available when the grid needs it most, measured in megawatts. Grid operators require utilities and suppliers to buy enough capacity to cover their peak demand plus a safety margin. They buy it in auctions.

MISO (Ameren zone)PJM (ComEd zone)
AuctionPlanning Resource AuctionBase Residual Auction

What a MW-day is. It is the price of holding one megawatt of capacity ready for one day. To get a yearly cost, multiply the price by the megawatts needed and by the number of days.

Why prices jump when supply gets tight. Each auction uses a demand curve. When there is plenty of spare capacity, the price is low and flat. As supply falls toward the reliability requirement, the curve gets steep, so losing even a little capacity raises the price a lot. The price is capped at a level tied to the cost of building a new plant, called the cost of new entry, or CONE.

1
Start with the price change. Retiring about 0.8 GW of nameplate gas capacity, which counts as about 0.6 GW of accredited capacity, raises the MISO summer price by about $148 per MW-day.
2
Multiply by the megawatts customers must hold. The Illinois obligation in MISO is about 11,600 MW.
3
Multiply by the days in the season. Summer is 92 days.

$148 × 11,600 × 92 = about $158 million a year, which the report rounds to $160 million. Spread across all 365 days, that is about $37 per MW-day, the "annualized" figure in the report.

The same math for ComEd. PJM's price rises about $134 per MW-day for the full year. The report puts the ComEd cost at about $1.1 billion a year. Working backward, that implies a ComEd obligation of roughly 22,500 MW. That obligation is our calculation, not a number printed in the report. Together, the two zones come to about $1.3 billion a year.

Why the report says the number could go either way

The $160 million assumes the market starts right at its reliability requirement. If the market already has a little spare capacity, as in the last MISO auction, the same retirement raises the summer price only about $59 per MW-day. If the market is already short and prices are at the cap, retirement cannot push the price higher. Instead, customers pay the capped price for more years until new plants are built. The agencies also call the MISO estimate conservative, because MISO's cap is based on a cheaper plant than current turbine prices support. Note too that the draft is inconsistent here. Its text gives a starting price of $7 per MW-day while Table 5-4 shows $886, and that should be corrected.

4. System cost and what the billions mean

How to read any dollar figure in these reports

Ask four questions. Is it system cost, capacity cost, a rate or a bill? Is it one year or many years added up as NPV? Is it statewide, one zone or one customer class? And what costs were left out?

Learning corner
Background

Power costs come in layers. Wholesale markets set the price of energy hour by hour and the price of capacity by auction. Policy adds costs such as renewable credits. Utilities add delivery charges for wires and poles, which the ICC regulates. A residential rate blends all of these, and a bill multiplies the rate by usage.

Why it matters

Legislators and reporters will ask what this means for bills. The honest answer is that these reports estimate system and capacity costs, which move bills, but they do not calculate household bills. Knowing that distinction keeps a message accurate.

Key terms
Energy marketHour by hour buying and selling of electricity
Capacity marketAuctions that pay plants to be available at peak
Default supplyPower for customers who have not chosen a retail supplier, bought by the IPA for ComEd and Ameren customers
Supply versus deliverySupply is the power itself. Delivery is the utility's cost to move it over wires
Check yourself
If the MISO price rises $148 per MW-day only in summer, why is the yearly cost about $160 million and not five times more?

MISO prices capacity by season, and the $148 applies to the 92 summer days only. 148 × 11,600 MW × 92 is about $158 million.

Does $14.2 billion in savings mean bills drop by that amount?

No. It is a statewide system cost reduction added up over many years as NPV. Bill effects depend on how costs are allocated and how much each customer uses.

Say it like thisabout 40 seconds

There are four different cost numbers in these reports, and they mean different things. System cost is the whole state's power bill over about twenty years, roughly 278 billion dollars. Capacity cost is what customers pay to have plants standing by for the hottest and coldest days. A rate is the price per kilowatt hour, and a bill is the rate times how much you use. The 160 million dollar number is capacity cost. If these plants retire, supply gets tighter, the auction price jumps, and customers pay that higher price on every megawatt they need for the summer.

Background

ICC proceedings follow statutory clocks. Once a plan is filed, the Commission has a fixed window to rule, usually 180 days, which it may extend once. Inside that window, parties file testimony, ask each other questions in discovery and cross examine witnesses at hearing.

Why it matters

Deadlines decide influence. The cheapest point to shape an outcome is the earliest one, comments now and agency testimony this winter, not after an order is written.

Overall script

A complete talk track on the main points, about 2.4 minutes spoken. Use it for a briefing, a legislator meeting or a call.

Main points scriptabout 2.4 minutes

Illinois just released two plans that are easy to confuse, so let me separate them.

The first is the Resource Adequacy Mitigation Plan. It comes from the 2021 climate law, and it has one job, which is to decide whether older plants should get more time before their clean air deadlines. The state is proposing to keep 11 gas and oil peaker plants available until May 31, 2033. Coal is not included.

The second is the Integrated Resource Plan, the state's first. It looks at the whole power system through 2047 and gives the Commerce Commission power to direct programs like energy efficiency, power purchases and utility grid plans.

Both plans say the same thing about the problem. Demand, mostly from data centers, is growing faster than dependable supply. Central and southern Illinois is short first, almost four gigawatts in 2030 before imports, and neighboring states may not have power to spare.

Why keep old plants? Because they're cheap insurance. They run only a few hours in rare years, but if they retire with nothing to replace them, the state estimates customers pay about 1.3 billion dollars more a year for capacity, including about 160 million in the Ameren zone. Over the long run, keeping existing gas saves about 14 billion dollars.

What happens next? Comments on the Mitigation Plan are due around January 12. The Commission decides the IRP around May 2027 and the Mitigation Plan around August 2027, and the IRP decision can change the Mitigation Plan. Program changes start January 1, 2028. The next IRP, due in 2029, decides whether any plant runs past 2033.

For Ameren Illinois, the plan protects Venice and Tilton through 2033, but four of our five ORG plants, about 1,530 megawatts, have 2040 deadlines and sit outside it. Our ask is simple. Support the extension, fix the errors in the record, and make the case for those four plants in the IRP and with the General Assembly, using the state's own numbers.

The bottom line is that keeping the lights on and keeping costs down now depends on building new resources fast and keeping some existing ones a little longer.

Five lines to remember

  1. Two plans. One delays deadlines, one directs programs.
  2. Our zone is short first, almost four gigawatts in 2030 before imports.
  3. Old peakers are insurance. Rarely used, expensive to lose.
  4. Retiring them without replacement costs about 160 million dollars a year in our zone.
  5. The IRP decides first, around May 2027, and can change the Mitigation Plan.
