Oct 13

PJM and MISO face different problems. PJM leans on competitive generation and is worried about capacity costs. MISO has more regulated utilities and is worried about local reliability as thermal plants retire. A fix written for one market may not work in the other. The comparison table is in the reliability section above.

Oct 14

High prices worry both parties. Republican officials may stress more generation and regulatory changes. Democratic officials may stress affordability steps, clean energy, and market reform. These are broad tendencies, not uniform positions. Both sides care about what customers pay.

Oct 14

PJM runs a capacity market. Resources are paid to commit to being available, and those costs flow into supply charges. Data center demand, slow new generation, and retirements have pushed on it.

Oct 14

Governor Shapiro negotiated limits on certain PJM auction prices. A cap holds down near term costs but can weaken the signal to build. Pennsylvania described its agreement as protecting consumers from higher auction costs. Participants wondered whether Illinois could apply similar pressure.

Oct 14

States do not control every part of wholesale pricing. Key market rules generally sit with FERC. A PJM focused bill may not do the same thing in MISO.

Oct 14

Customer frustration is growing. A public event with Rep. Ann Williams and clean energy advocates was cited, where a constituent criticized large corporations. The exchange was not verified. Participants also noted frustration among executive branch officials over how utilities respond. The advice was to explain which parts of a bill come from which cause.

Say it in plain words

Electricity prices have become a worry for both parties. On October 13 we heard that PJM and MISO have different problems. PJM is fighting high capacity costs, while MISO, where Ameren Illinois sits, is more worried about having enough dependable plants as old units retire. On October 14 the discussion turned to price caps. Pennsylvania's governor negotiated limits on certain PJM auction prices. A cap helps customers today, but it can also weaken the signal that tells developers to build. Illinois cannot simply copy that, because FERC oversees many of the market rules and a fix for PJM may not work in MISO. Customers are also frustrated, so we should explain bills piece by piece. Distribution, supply, transmission, capacity, and policy programs each tell a different story, and a single explanation will be incomplete.

Background

Why a capacity market exists

Grid operators need enough resources lined up years ahead. A capacity market pays resources for being available, so owners keep plants running and developers build new ones.

Why price caps are a tradeoff

High prices raise bills but also pay for new supply. A cap protects bills in the short run and can slow new building if developers expect lower revenue. The simulator above shows that tension in a simple way.

Who regulates what

FERC oversees wholesale market rules. The Illinois Commerce Commission oversees retail utility rates and plans. State legislators can pass laws, but they cannot rewrite wholesale rules alone.

Questions to ask
  • Which part of a customer's bill is moving, and why?
  • Would this proposal work the same way in PJM and MISO?
  • What happens to new building if prices are capped?
