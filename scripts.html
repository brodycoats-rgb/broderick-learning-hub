Three lengths

30 seconds, for legislative staff

Data centers are different from ordinary load growth because of their size, speed, and how they behave. They can ask for service in about 18 to 36 months, faster than generation and transmission get built. The question is not whether Illinois wants data centers. It is whether each project is ready, whether the grid can serve it reliably, and whether the project carries the costs and risks it creates. PJM and MISO are taking different paths, and [company position, confirm before use] is to welcome credible projects that pay their own way.

2 minutes, for a sponsor or colleague

I agree with the goal of protecting existing customers from costs created by a project that is delayed, downsized, or canceled. The question is how to make that protection work in Illinois.

Here is the setting. Illinois recruited data centers with incentives. AI scale requests changed the questions. Who pays, how a project proves it is real, how reliability is protected, and what happens if the load never arrives. A lot of bills were introduced this spring. No comprehensive framework passed. On June 5 the Governor paused new incentive agreements beginning July 1.

Three things I would test in any proposal. First, the harm. What exactly are we protecting against, and who bears it? Second, the mechanism. A deposit, a minimum bill, and an exit charge can each cover a different exposure, but they have to fit together so the same cost is not recovered twice. Third, the authority. Our utilities deliver power, many large customers buy supply competitively, and transmission is governed through regional and federal rules.

So my suggestion is that the statute set the customer protection standard and direct the ICC to set tariff, assurance, minimum commitment, and exit terms in a full proceeding. [Confirm company position before saying this externally.] Could we start with the specific harm you want to prevent, then compare mechanisms that reach it?

5 minutes, for a committee briefing
  1. Frame, 45 seconds. The question is not whether Illinois wants data centers. It is whether each project is ready, can be served reliably, and carries its own costs. Name the five separate problems. Energy, capacity, deliverability, disturbance performance, and cost allocation.
  2. Why now, 60 seconds. Service in about 18 to 36 months against longer lead times for supply. The queue is not the forecast. Data centers reached about 4.4 percent of US electricity in 2023 (DOE) with projections of 6.7 to 12 percent by 2028. [Cite the source and year when speaking.]
  3. Two regions, 60 seconds. PJM is about capacity, with 30 of 32 gigawatts of forecast growth tied to data centers. MISO is about technical integration and visibility. Attribute the $23.1 billion figure to the PJM market monitor.
  4. Reliability in plain words, 60 seconds. Sudden load loss is a contingency in reverse. Ride through, ramp limits, telemetry, and staged restoration. Do not cite the 3,800 MW figure until sourced.
  5. Costs and framework, 45 seconds. Readiness ladder before binding forecasts. Cost based tariffs through an evidentiary process. Minimum revenue, collateral, and exit remedies. Statute sets principles, and the ICC and MISO set technical details.
  6. Close, 30 seconds. Welcome credible growth. Separate the layers. Assign cost and default risk to the project. Allow flexibility only when curtailment is real. Principles in statute, details in the record. [Confirm any specific ask with GR leadership.]

Answers to the hard questions

Say the answer aloud first, then open the card. The structure is the same every time. Acknowledge the concern, give one verified fact with its date, name the tradeoff on both sides, and finish with a decision test or a next step.

Are data centers raising rates?

They can create costs and they can add revenue. It depends on the infrastructure required, the tariff terms, the contract commitments, market effects, and whether the project operates as forecast. The policy goal is to assign costs and risks correctly before investment occurs.

Why not ban or slow data centers until the grid is ready?

That is not our position. The question is whether each project is ready, can be served reliably, and carries its own costs. Credible projects should be welcomed and speculative ones should clear readiness milestones first. [Confirm company position.]

Why should data centers get tax incentives?

That is now an accountability question. Illinois should compare verified investment, jobs, property tax, and community benefits against the exemption value and the infrastructure impact, then tie any future incentive to measurable performance. The reported figure is more than $983 million for 27 data centers from 2020 through 2024, which is not the lifetime cost of the program.

Should they bring their own power?

The phrase captures a legitimate concern but is too imprecise for statute. We need to define whether the goal is new energy, dependable capacity, on site generation, storage, flexible load, or full cost responsibility. And it is not a standalone solution. Deliverability, fuel, air permits, and default risk all matter.

If a data center drops off the grid, isn't that good for reliability?

No. Generation was scheduled for that load. If several facilities drop at once, frequency rises and operators must cut output quickly. It is a contingency like losing a generator, in the opposite direction.

Why not curtail data centers first in an emergency?

A flexible or interruptible service option can be designed, but the trigger, notice, compensation, and performance rules must be explicit. A promise to curtail needs equipment, testing, and consequences.

Why not let the legislature set the rate?

The legislature should set the consumer protection goal. The Commission can test evidence, allocate costs, review contracts, and update terms in an open proceeding. Arbitrary multipliers in statute risk being wrong in both directions.

Is PJM's plan the right model for Illinois?

Illinois is split between PJM and MISO and the frameworks differ. We should coordinate them without forcing one region's solution onto the other. ComEd faces PJM's tighter position and Ameren Illinois is in MISO Zone 4.

Didn't 3,800 MW of data centers just trip off in PJM?

There is a multi gigawatt disturbance concern, but I cannot confirm the exact size or date from primary sources. [Do not cite 3,800 MW until PJM, NERC, Dominion, or the filed record is cited.]

How much has PJM lost to data center load?

The PJM market monitor estimates about $23.1 billion in added capacity revenue across three auctions. That is the monitor's analysis and not an uncontested PJM finding.

Who pays if the project is canceled?

Under a weak structure, existing customers or shareholders may carry the risk. Strong tariffs use deposits, minimum bills, collateral, and exit charges so the project that creates the risk carries it.

Is BIPA driving data centers out of Illinois?

BIPA creates compliance and litigation considerations, especially for biometric access systems. Some stakeholders cite it as an investment concern, but location claims should be supported project by project.

Is Ameren trying to slow data centers? [clearance]

The objective is to serve credible growth while protecting existing customers. Reasonable readiness standards and financial commitments can speed serious projects by separating them from speculative requests.

Can you tell the chair Ameren supports this?

I can confirm we discussed the option, but I cannot confirm endorsement. Let me take the specific language through the appropriate review.

How many data centers does Illinois have?

There is no single authoritative public count. Cleanview lists 114, Data Center Map 242, and Aterio 278. They define facilities differently, so I would name the dataset and date and focus on megawatts by status.

Doesn't a deposit on every project just slow investment?

Risk based forms, step downs, and periodic review matter. A deposit that fits the exposure protects customers without tying up more developer capital than needed. Rules that are too restrictive or unpredictable can send credible projects elsewhere. [Company position, confirm.]

Moves for the working group

Know whether the meeting is for learning, option building, negotiation, or a decision. Bring one idea, one question, and one material concern. Keep a first contribution to about 30 to 60 seconds. Once your point is understood, stop.

MomentWhat you can say
Clarify the problemAre we addressing speculative requests, or costs left after cancellation? Those may need different tools.
Build on an ideaThe milestone approach could help. Could we connect each milestone to the spending it triggers?
Offer an alternativeAnother option is to phase the commitment as the project advances. Could we test that against the customer protection goal?
Disagree constructivelyI see the value of consistency. My concern is that one fixed amount may miss the real exposure. Could we compare two project sizes?
Hold your boundaryI am comfortable exploring that. I am not confirming a company position today.
Handle uncertaintyI need our regulatory team to confirm the tariff interaction. I can bring that answer back.
Keep the group movingWe seem to agree on the goal. The open choice is whether the protection is fixed or tied to demonstrated costs.
Catch a missing factBefore we close this, let us separate the two jurisdictions. Could we record this as an option pending confirmation of the Illinois tariff, and not as an Illinois requirement?

After the meeting, record what was agreed, what is open, who owns the next step, and what you personally promised. Separate an idea discussed from a decision made.
