Session Outlook and Court Risk Study Guide

Chambers, calendar, courts and market pressure

Why it matters

Where a bill starts, who chairs the committee and what the courts decide can matter more than the text of the bill. These meetings gave a map of the road ahead.

What the meetings said

  • Association strategy shift. Engaging legislators early, before a bill hardens, worked about 70 percent of the time. In the other 30 percent the bill was too problematic. Uncoordinated outreach from different departments inside one company can lead legislators to assume the company agrees with a bill.
  • Chambers. The House committee structure for energy was called large and unbalanced, and some caucus members were said to lack confidence in leadership. The Senate was described as steadier. Advice was to align companies with the Senate sponsor first.
  • Calendar. Expect little in January, more activity in March as deadlines arrive, and a crowded April.
  • Elections. Participants expected supermajorities to remain largely stable. Individual race talk was left out here.
  • Commission turnover. Several ICC seats were described as expiring or turning over. Names came through the transcript unclearly, so verify.
  • Court risk. The Illinois Supreme Court is deciding whether the state may override local zoning for renewable siting. A ruling is expected before the end of 2026. Counsel also flagged generation tax exemptions as possibly vulnerable under the ban on special legislation.
  • Stadium. A bill that does not name a location is being considered to give members cover. School districts and teachers are concerned about property tax effects.
  • Tier 2 pensions. Reform is expected to wait unless the IRS enforces safe harbor rules.
  • Market pressure. PJM is under pressure from both federal and state leaders over prices, and two governors have been coordinating on affordability and data center load.
  • Storms. Record tornado counts mean restoration speed is now a legislative topic. The framing is that severe weather is the new baseline, not a one time event.

Worked example, why the path matters

Imagine two identical bills. One starts in the Senate with a steady committee and a sponsor who has talked to all the affected companies. The other starts in the House with a crowded committee and no coordinated position. The first is likely to arrive in March ready to move. The second may still be stuck in April when the calendar pushes everything out.

Scripts

Thirty seconds

The spring calendar will be slow early and tight late. If companies want to shape anything, the work happens now, with the Senate sponsor, before the March deadlines.

Two minutes

A few things will shape the session. First, the Senate has been steadier than the House, so a Senate first path is safer. Second, courts may reset the ground rules. A Supreme Court ruling on state siting authority is expected by year end, and it could change how transmission and renewable projects get built and taxed. Third, prices are the political center. Federal and state leaders are both pressing PJM, and they differ on solutions.

Our job is to talk early, stay coordinated, and make sure one voice speaks for each issue. That keeps legislators from guessing who supports what.

Why Senate first

The recommendation was to get the companies together on what they can agree on, work through the Senate sponsor, and do it early. The reasons given or implied were that the Senate has been steadier in leadership and committee structure, and the House has had more turmoil. The other point was process. Different companies pursuing separate efforts last year left little to show for it, and the advice was to keep outside voices out and to hold disciplined communication among a small group of principals.

  • Watch commission seats, since a new commissioner can change how dockets turn out.
  • Watch staff changes. A former energy staffer moved to the electric industry, and one discussion mentioned retirement timing for staff, which signals lost institutional memory.

Supreme Court siting case, in more detail

The Illinois Supreme Court is considering whether the General Assembly can limit the power of counties and municipalities to regulate renewable energy siting. A ruling is expected before the end of 2026. A case near a town in downstate Illinois was named, and the summary flagged the case name and docket number as unknown.

If the state wins
Statewide siting rules stay, and large wind, solar and transmission projects keep a predictable path.
If local governments win
Each county or municipality may set its own rules. Financing and schedules for transmission and renewable projects face disruption.
Second risk, special legislation
Counsel described the state's tax exemptions for generation as shaky under the ban on special legislation. A loss could unwind tax treatment that projects counted on.
Fallback ideas
Regional compacts or agreements with counties were listed as possible alternatives. They are slower and less certain.

For energy readers the lesson is the pattern. A framework bill with no location can pass when a specific one cannot. A data center bill can use the same device, with rules first and sites later.

Tier 2 pensions, why the issue keeps returning

Tier 2 is the pension tier for people hired after 2010. The concern is a federal one. If the plan does not meet a federal safe harbor standard, the IRS could act and the state could face a funding and compliance problem. The meeting view was that the issue will have to be fixed eventually but is likely to wait unless the IRS forces the timing. The governor is expected to drive timing through the Senate president. Without a federal trigger there is little appetite to act in the 2027 spring session. The cost of any fix is large and immediate while the benefit is long term, which is why it keeps getting postponed.

Market pressure from outside Illinois

  • PJM faces criticism from federal officials and from governors of both parties over prices, although the two sides want different cures. One side leans toward more thermal capacity, and the other toward more renewables.
  • Two governors were described as talking about how to manage affordability and data center growth. One had secured price caps in PJM. No comparable cap tool was described in MISO.
  • Staff to a governor were reported as frustrated with utilities. Whether that is a real policy shift or a public show was an open question.
  • A town hall featuring a legislator and an advocacy coalition drew an accusation that the legislator fronted for large corporations, and organizers ended public comment. It shows the tone of public meetings on energy costs.

More questions you may get

Why is nothing expected in January?

New members are sworn in and committees are organized. Real negotiation tends to start in March when deadlines approach.

What if the House blocks a Senate bill?

Then the issue waits or moves in another vehicle. That is why early coalition work and drafted language matter.

Why does the siting ruling matter for energy?

It decides whether the state or counties set the rules for large renewable and transmission projects, which affects schedules and financing.

Is the stadium bill connected to energy?

Not directly. It matters because it competes for floor time and because its drafting approach, a framework without a location, could be reused for other hard votes.

What triggers Tier 2 action?

A federal safe harbor problem. Without that trigger, the view was that the legislature will not act in spring 2027.

What should I watch after the election?

Who chairs the energy committees, who leads each caucus, and whether commission seats turn over.
