A bill is filed

A member files text that changes Illinois law. Most changes add a rule, change an existing rule, or set up a program.

2
Committee

A committee hears the bill and votes whether to send it to the floor. Many bills end here.

3
Second and third reading

Second reading is when amendments are added. Third reading is the final floor vote in that chamber. The text lawmakers vote on is the amended text, which can differ from the summary in our tracker.

4
Other chamber

The bill repeats the process in the other chamber. If it changes, the first chamber must agree.

5
Governor

The Governor signs, vetoes, or changes it with an amendatory veto. The veto session after the election is when lawmakers can answer.

What a yes vote means

A yes vote on a bill makes its text the law once the other steps are done. A yes on a resolution only states a wish. So the useful question about any bill is what changes on the day after it becomes law, and who has to do something different. Every card below answers that in one line, labeled A yes vote does.

Plain language glossary

The terms that come up again and again. Tap a term to see what it means and why it matters in these bills.

Megawatt and megawatt hour

A megawatt (MW) is how fast power is used at one moment, like speed. A megawatt hour (MWh) is the amount used over time, like distance. One MW running for an hour is one MWh. A 5 MW data center running all year uses about 43,800 MWh at full load.

Why it matters Bills set size thresholds in MW and penalties in dollars per MWh.

Large load and hyperscale

A large load is a customer that needs far more power than a typical business, often tens or hundreds of megawatts. Hyperscale describes the very largest data centers, usually run by major cloud companies.

Why it matters Where a bill draws its size line decides who is covered.

Interconnection

The process of connecting a customer or a power plant to the grid. It includes studies to see what upgrades are needed and who pays for them.

Why it matters Our queue is the line of requests waiting for study. Long lines and speculative requests slow everyone.

Tariff

The rulebook and price list the ICC approves for a class of customers. It says what a customer pays and the terms of service.

Why it matters Many data center bills try to change tariff terms.

Rate base and cost recovery

The rate base is the utility equipment the ICC has approved to earn a return on. Cost recovery is how approved costs are collected from customers over time.

Why it matters If a project is cancelled after equipment is built, who pays depends on these rules.

Cost allocation

The way total costs are divided among customer groups. The ideal is that costs follow who caused them.

Why it matters The heart of the large load debate.

Stranded asset

Equipment or a plant that was built in good faith but is no longer used enough to pay for itself, while the bill for it remains.

Why it matters Why we care about customers leaving early and about policies that shorten asset lives.

Security deposit, letter of credit and collateral

Money or a bank promise given up front to cover a customer's obligations if the project fails. A letter of credit is a bank's written guarantee to pay.

Why it matters HB4172 would stop utilities from asking for these from large customers.

Minimum demand charge

A rule that a large customer pays for a minimum amount of service even if it uses less. It protects other customers if the customer shrinks.

Why it matters Common in state and ICC approaches to large loads.

Clean energy certificate

A tradable certificate showing one megawatt hour of clean power was generated somewhere. Buying one lets a company claim that power. Often called a REC.

Why it matters Several bills require buying and retiring these.

Hourly matching and annual matching

Annual matching only requires that over a year the clean power bought equals the power used. Hourly matching requires clean power for every hour of use.

Why it matters Hourly is far stricter and costs more. It favors steady, round the clock supply.

Bring your own energy

A policy idea that a very large customer should supply or pay for its own new power instead of leaning on the existing system.

Why it matters The Governor, the POWER Act sponsors and Republican members all say they want a version of this.

Self direct program

A program that lets a large customer reduce certain charges on its bill by building or buying its own clean energy.

Why it matters The question is always who covers the money not collected.

Additionality

The idea that clean energy counted for a customer should be new supply that would not otherwise exist, not an old plant relabeled.

Why it matters Why bills limit eligible resources by age.

MISO and PJM

The regional grid operators. MISO covers Ameren Illinois' territory and most of central and southern Illinois. PJM covers ComEd's northern Illinois territory. They run the wholesale power markets and plan the regional grid.

Why it matters A rule written for one region can be hard to apply in the other.

Capacity price and resource adequacy

Resource adequacy means having enough power available when demand peaks. Capacity price is what the market pays for being available. When supply is tight, capacity prices rise and customers pay more.

Why it matters Behind every bill about keeping plants open or closing them.

FERC and state jurisdiction

FERC regulates wholesale power and transmission across state lines. States regulate retail service and distribution. A state rule that reaches into wholesale or transmission costs can be challenged.

Why it matters Why some bill language raises a legal flag.

ICC

The Illinois Commerce Commission, the state regulator of utilities. It approves rates, tariffs and many utility plans.

Why it matters Many bills hand it new tasks.

IPA

The Illinois Power Agency. It runs state procurement of renewable energy and now many storage programs.

Why it matters Often named in new funds and programs.

DCEO

The Department of Commerce and Economic Opportunity. It runs state economic development incentives, including data center incentives.

Illinois EPA and Pollution Control Board

The Illinois EPA enforces environmental rules. The Pollution Control Board writes many of them.

CEJA

The Climate and Equitable Jobs Act, Public Act 102-662, signed in 2021. It set deadlines for coal and gas plants to cut emissions or close and created many clean energy programs.

Why it matters Several bills try to delay or roll it back.

CRGA

The Clean and Reliable Grid Affordability Act. A recent law that created the storage standard and related programs and charges.

Why it matters SB4003 would unwind parts of it.

Preemption and home rule

Preemption is when the state overrides local rules. Home rule lets larger Illinois cities act on their own unless the state says otherwise.

Why it matters Gas ban and local siting bills turn on this.

Referendum

A vote by residents on a specific question.

Why it matters Used in the gas ban bills as the path around a limit.

Eminent domain

The power to take private land for a public use with payment.

Why it matters Central to SB2842 on carbon dioxide pipelines.

Peaker plant

A gas plant that normally runs only on the hottest and coldest days. It starts fast but is costly and dirtier per unit.

Heat rate

The fuel energy needed to produce one kilowatt hour of electricity. Lower is more efficient.

Why it matters SB3606 uses it to decide which plants must cut emissions.

Virtual power plant and aggregator

Many small customer owned devices such as batteries managed together to act like one plant. The company that manages them is an aggregator.

Why it matters HB4996 changes pay and data rules for them.

Net metering

A credit on a customer's bill for power they send back to the grid.

Community solar and DER

DER means distributed energy resources, small power sources connected to the local grid. Community solar is a shared solar project whose output is split among subscribers.

Why it matters Our interconnection work.

Decommissioning plan and AIMA

A decommissioning plan says how a project is removed and the land restored. An agricultural impact mitigation agreement, AIMA, is a contract with the state Department of Agriculture that protects farmland during a project.

Why it matters Storage and solar siting bills.

Closed loop cooling

A cooling design that reuses the same water in a sealed system instead of using it once. It uses far less water.

Why it matters Water bills often turn on whether this is allowed or required.

Community benefit agreement

A signed promise from a developer to the host community, such as jobs, payments or protections.

Why it matters Part of the POWER Act.

Rulemaking

After a law passes, an agency writes detailed rules to carry it out. Bills that leave key details to rulemaking pass first and set the real cost later.

Why it matters Why undefined terms are a risk.

Companion bill

The same idea filed in both chambers under different numbers, often by different sponsors, so it has two chances to pass.

Why it matters Our tracker holds many pairs.

Shell bill and gut and replace

A bill with almost no content, filed to hold a place. Later the whole text is replaced with a new proposal that can move quickly.

Why it matters Many big energy deals move this way.

Veto session

Meetings after the November election where lawmakers can act on bills the Governor vetoed and also move other business. This year November 17 to 19 and December 1 to 3.

How the money flows when a very large customer connects

Most data center bills are really about this one picture. Follow the two paths.

A large customer asks to connect
then
The utility studies the request and builds lines and substations for it
then
The ICC approves the cost and it goes into rates

Path one, the customer stays and pays

The new equipment is used and paid for. Fixed costs are spread across more kilowatt hours, so each existing customer's share goes down. This is the growth benefit our SMEs describe.

Path two, the customer cancels or shrinks

The equipment is built but not fully used. Unless the customer posted security or agreed to minimum payments, the remaining cost falls on everyone else. This is the exposure behind HB4172.

The three common protections

Security
A deposit or letter of credit up front. Returned if the customer performs.
Minimum bill
The customer pays for a minimum amount of service even if it uses less.
Exit fee
If the customer leaves early it pays the remaining cost of what was built for it.

Bills that tighten these protect customers and may slow projects. Bills that loosen them speed projects and move risk to customers. Neither is automatically right, the right answer depends on how many requests are real.

How we decide what works

Seven tests drawn from the reasoning in our SME feedback. Each card below scores a bill against them.

1
Customer protection

Does it keep cost and credit risk away from customers who did nothing to cause it?

A good bill makes the party that creates a cost carry it, or posts security for it.

2
Load growth and competitiveness

Does it keep Illinois a place where new customers choose to build and grow?

New load spreads fixed grid costs across more sales. Lost load is lost help on affordability.

3
Reliability and supply

Does it help or hurt having enough power available when it is needed?

Retiring supply early or banning supply types can tighten the system and raise capacity prices.

4
Clarity and definitions

Are the key terms defined well enough to plan around?

Undefined words such as data center or good standing are where surprises hide.

5
Feasibility and timing

Can the thing it asks for actually be done by the date it names?

A deadline set before the supply, studies or rules exist is a risk even when the goal is fine.

6
Burden and jurisdiction

Does it add paperwork, new proceedings or a conflict with FERC, MISO or existing ICC processes?

Extra process costs time. Rules that cross into federal territory invite legal challenges.

7
Prescriptive or outcome based

Does it set a goal or dictate the method?

Outcome based rules age better. Mandated technologies and formulas get overtaken by events.

The five step path to a position

  1. Read what the bill changes, not what the title says. Compare the title, summary and SME text.
  2. Name who is touched. Ameren Illinois directly, our customers, our growth, Ameren Missouri, or no one.
  3. Run the seven tests and write down which ones the bill helps and which it fails.
  4. Pick the label. Support, neutral, neutral with concerns, oppose, no impact, or monitor.
  5. Write the one change that would move us. A position with a path to yes is stronger than a flat no.

Questions to ask on any bill

Use these with your team and with stakeholders. They turn a summary into a conversation.

Scope

  • What exactly is covered, and where does each key definition stop?
  • Does it apply to existing facilities and agreements, or only new ones?
  • Why that size or date, and what happens right above and below the line?

Money

  • Who pays, who benefits, and does any cost land on customers who did nothing to cause it?
  • If plans change after money is spent, who carries the cost?
  • Is a new charge a price signal that changes behavior, or a way to fund something else?

Power and the grid

  • Does it change when or where power is needed?
  • Does it depend on supply that does not exist yet?
  • Does it work the same in MISO and PJM?

Process

  • Who decides, with how much discretion, and who can appeal?
  • What is left to later rulemaking?
  • Is the timeline achievable given our own studies and queue?

Politics

  • Who is asking for this, and what problem are they trying to solve?
  • Who opposes it, and what do they offer instead?
  • Is this a vehicle for a larger deal, and what version could we support?

Us

  • Does it affect Ameren Illinois, Ameren Missouri, both or neither?
  • Which team's work actually changes?
  • Does what we say here match what we said on related bills?

Blind spot checks

  • Do the title, the summary and the SME text describe the same bill?
  • Is there a companion bill with a different position?
  • Has any date in the bill already passed?

Patterns the team may have missed

Findings that come from reading the whole tracker side by side. Each is a question to put to the team.

Same idea, different positions

The four consumer data privacy bills carry the tags Oppose, Pending, Neutral and Neutral, and the SME text on SB3548 reads neutral to support. The gas ban bills split Neutral and Support with matching SME text. The agrivoltaic, gas equity, district energy and storage bond pairs also differ. Companion bills should carry one position unless the text differs.

SME text that may belong to another bill

The SME text on HB5538 (gas deadline extension) talks about wind and solar siting and matches the text on HB5477. The text on HB4910 (workforce hubs) describes project databases and matches HB4940. SB3830 and SB3246 have dashboard summaries that describe different things than their SME text. In each case a tag may rest on a review of the wrong bill.

Shell bills are not harmless

SB3574, SB3575 and SB3576 are technical shells from Senator Rezin and are marked watch only. In Illinois, shells are commonly used to carry large deals in veto session. If new text appears, the response time is days. Decide who reads it and who approves a position.

A cluster that points the same way

HB4798, SB2727, SB2736, HB5538, SB3581, SB3929 and HR744 all try to slow plant retirements or the clean energy timeline. Individually each looks modest. Together they are a theme, and our positions should be consistent across all of them.

Three bills that are really one

HB4956, SB3078 and SB3409 are substantively identical according to the SME. Track and amend them as a group.

Tags that the text does not support

HB5607 and HB5826 are Pending while the SME reads oppose. HB4419 is Neutral while the SME supports. SB3929 is Neutral while the SME says we could support. Fix the tags so the dashboard says what the SME says.

Who has no feedback

Eighteen tracked bills have no SME feedback besides the three shells. They are HB4830, HB5049, HB5479, HR744, SB2181, SB2842, SB2875, SB3030, SB3120, SB3705, SB3761, SB3794, SB3856, SB3927, SB3970, SB3974, SB4004 and SB4020. Our own interests inside bills that do not seem to be about us

The privacy bills could change how we handle customer data. HB4996 asks us to share customer data with aggregators. The storage siting bills would apply to Ameren owned storage. The community solar cap could change how projects line up on our feeders. Read each for what it asks of us, not only of developers.

Stops utilities from asking large new customers such as data centers for deposits, letters of credit or other financial security, as long as the customer follows existing ICC approved tariffs and stays in good standing. A yes vote does

A yes vote removes a utility's ability to require financial security from qualifying large customers before it builds for them.
