The story, pictures and models

The two days in one flow, with October 13 first inside each part.

Oct 13

Reliable power now decides where big tech builds. Faster interconnection, dependable supply, competitive prices, and steady rules can win projects. Texas, Utah, and Arizona were named as competitors.

Oct 13

Behind the meter generation is mostly a bridge. One speaker said hyperscalers see on site generators as a way to get power sooner, not a permanent replacement for the grid. Data centers need very high reliability, and the grid offers more resources, paths, and reserves.

Oct 13

One supply plan had three parts. Existing plant upgrades of about 600 MW, a 2,700 MW load following arrangement, and future clean generation. These are commercial figures, not proof of new plants under construction in Illinois.

Oct 13

How much Constellation upgrade output reaches the wider market is an open question. The notes point to about 800 MW of upgrades. Contracts may assign some output to specific customers. That does not make the power vanish from the grid, but it changes economic rights, buying assumptions, and capacity accounting. Sort this out before calling anything a shortfall.

Oct 13

Transmission service agreements create barriers. Developers raised upfront costs, financial security rules, and wait times. The policy question is how to welcome big investments without existing customers paying for speculative build out.

Oct 13

Words may shift opinion. One speaker said calling them server farms instead of data centers improved views by 25 points to about 55 percent support. No poll was supplied. Unverified

Oct 13

The plan is to hold small utility fixes for a larger data center bill. Possible topics include large load tariffs, interruptible service, generation adequacy, transmission rules, construction, and technical amendments. No bill language exists yet.

Oct 14

Will leaders spend political capital on it? The doubt is about leadership effort, not interest. A complex bill would need utilities, environmental groups, labor, consumer advocates, developers, local governments, and legislators to agree. That adds risk to the October 13 plan to bundle smaller items into it.

Oct 14

Public opinion is split between statewide and local. A speaker said fewer than 1 percent call data centers a top concern, while opposition can be fierce where a site is proposed. Concerns include electricity demand, water, land, taxes, and wider worries about artificial intelligence. Local financial benefits might help. Unverified

Oct 14

FERC acted on October 12. It approved a PJM registry of data center loads and rejected PJM's approach to cutting off certain large data centers in an emergency. That ties back to the October 13 talk on interruptible tariffs.

Oct 14

Disclosure talk raised conflict worries. Participants discussed legislators' Statements of Economic Interests and the chance that data center finances could complicate talks. No lawmaker's conflict was established.

Policymakers need to keep four things apart, since each raises its own legal and operating questions. Watching load growth. Assigning infrastructure cost. Requiring added resources. Authorizing emergency curtailment.

Say it in plain words

Data centers came up all over both days. On October 13 the point was that big tech companies pick sites based on how fast they can get dependable power. Some use their own generators as a bridge, not a permanent fix. One supply plan described about 600 megawatts of plant upgrades, a 2,700 megawatt load following deal, and future clean generation. The key lesson is that a contract is not a new power plant. It can help a customer price its needs without adding a single megawatt to the grid. We also need to know how much of the Constellation upgrade is already spoken for by private contracts. On October 14 the question turned political. Will leaders spend the effort to pass a big bill that every group wants to shape? And on October 12, FERC approved a PJM registry of data center loads but turned down PJM's plan to cut off certain large data centers in an emergency. Those are separate questions, and we should keep them separate.

Go deeper, terms and ideas

Background

Hyperscaler

A company that runs very large computing and data center sites, often for cloud services or artificial intelligence.

Behind the meter

Power made on the customer's side of the utility meter, such as dedicated generators at a data center while it waits for a full grid connection.

Load following

A supply arrangement built around changing demand instead of one fixed volume. It may use wholesale purchases, hedges, or other contracts.

Uprate

An upgrade that raises a plant's output. The open question is who holds the rights to the added output.

Transmission service agreement

A contract that sets commitments and financial duties for transmission service or for building the needed lines. The terms depend on the governing tariff.

Interruptible service and large load tariffs

An interruptible tariff lets a utility reduce a customer's supply under set conditions in return for a lower price. A large load tariff sets special rules and cost sharing for very big customers.

Questions to ask
  • Is this deal new megawatts or a financial contract?
  • Who pays if a project is delayed or canceled?
  • Which rules would apply to emergency curtailment, and who has authority to set them?
  • Who is the sponsor and who is in the coalition?
