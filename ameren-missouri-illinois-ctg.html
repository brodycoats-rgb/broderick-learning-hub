AMEREN MISSOURI
ILLINOIS CTG FACILITIES
Learning and Presentation Guide

A plain-language primer for government relations professionals and lobbyists

Prepared September 2026

Purpose
Use this guide as a practical primer for government relations professionals, lobbyists, and policy staff who need to explain CTG facilities to nontechnical audiences. It is meant to help readers understand the technology, speak credibly about reliability and emissions, and identify which facts require expert validation before external use.

Important data discipline
The source materials use different capacity bases. This guide separates nameplate capability, seasonal or dependable capability, and values used in policy briefs. Do not combine figures from different bases. Legislative districts and final externally approved emissions tables require address-level or unit-level verification before public use.

How to use this guide

This guide is designed to move from basic understanding to briefing readiness. The first sections define the technology and why the facilities matter. The middle sections explain the five plants, emissions, regulation, emergencies, and community impact. The final sections translate that information into advocacy frames, scripts, assessment questions, and validation steps for external use.

  • Read Sections 1 through 4 first. They establish the vocabulary, operating model, and legal framework.
  • Use Section 5 as the five-plant reference.
  • Practice the scripts aloud. The short version is designed for a legislative hallway conversation; the longer version fits a briefing.
  • Complete each knowledge check without looking back. Then use the answer key to identify weak areas.
  • Before external use, complete the validation checklist in the appendix.

Briefing snapshot
The five Illinois CTG facilities are fast-start resources owned and operated by Ameren Missouri, legally Union Electric Company. Their core value is availability during peak demand, outages, transmission constraints, and MISO emergency conditions. The policy challenge is timing: statutory emissions and retirement requirements must be evaluated alongside replacement capacity, deliverability, customer cost, environmental goals, and local tax impacts. Venice is the near-term pressure point in the internal materials because its cited retirement timing is January 1, 2030.

Executive learning objectives

  • Define combustion turbine generation without jargon.
  • Distinguish energy, capacity, dispatchability, nameplate capacity, seasonal capability, capacity factor, and run hours.
  • Describe the five Illinois facilities owned and operated by Ameren Missouri, legally Union Electric Company.
  • Explain the reliability purpose of fast-start peaking and contingency plants.
  • Explain why a facility that runs only a small number of hours may still have high reliability value.
  • Describe CEJA retirement and emissions requirements without claiming that reliability exceptions are automatic or permanent.
  • Explain emissions honestly: natural gas combustion produces greenhouse gases and criteria pollutants, while infrequent operation limits annual totals relative to continuously running plants.
  • Connect plant decisions to local schools, municipal services, public safety, and the tax base.
  • Present a balanced policy message: support transition goals while aligning retirement timing with proven replacement capability.

The central sentence
These plants are not designed to run every day. They are designed to be available, start quickly, and produce power when demand is high, another resource is unavailable, imports are constrained, or MISO declares emergency conditions. Their value should be evaluated by both output and readiness, because a resource can run for relatively few hours and still matter during scarcity conditions.

Slide-by-slide presentation script

Use this script for a briefing with lobbyists, government relations professionals, or policy staff who are unfamiliar with combustion turbine generators. The tone should be practical, conversational, and confidence-building. The goal is not to turn the audience into engineers. The goal is to help them understand why these facilities matter, how to explain their reliability value, and where the externally validated facts are needed before legislative outreach.

Mind map for presenters

Central idea: Illinois CTGs are fast-start reliability resources. First branch: what they are, meaning jet-engine-style generators that can start quickly. Second branch: why they matter, meaning peak demand, outages, transmission constraints, and MISO emergencies. Third branch: what complicates the issue, meaning emissions, CEJA deadlines, replacement timing, customer affordability, and host-community tax impacts. Fourth branch: what we need legislators to understand, meaning retirement timing should be matched to proven replacement capability and a credible community transition plan.

Slide 1. Opening and purpose

Speaker script: We have spent the last several months gathering information, reviewing the data, and building profiles for these plants. The main thing we have learned is pretty straightforward: these are not plants that are meant to run all day, every day. Their value is that they can show up quickly when the grid is under stress. Today, I want to walk through why that matters, so we can explain it clearly and responsibly to legislators.

Presenter notes: Start by reminding the group we have done the homework. Keep it practical and focused on why the plants matter.

Slide 2. Icebreaker quiz

Ask the room: If a power plant runs only a small part of the year, does that automatically mean it is not important? A. Yes, low run time means low value. B. No, a plant can run rarely and still matter when the grid is tight. C. It depends only on property taxes. D. It depends only on emissions. Correct answer: B. Transition: That is the basic idea for this whole presentation. These plants are a lot like fire engines. They may sit most days, but when there is a fire, you need them ready to go.

Presenter notes: Use this to break the ice and make the point that low run time does not automatically mean low value.

Slide 3. What is a CTG?

Speaker script: A CTG is basically a large jet engine connected to a generator. It pulls in air, compresses it, burns fuel, spins a turbine, and turns that motion into electricity. For this audience, the engineering details are less important than the practical point: this kind of plant can start quickly and provide power when the grid needs something dispatchable.

Presenter notes: Keep this simple. The takeaway is not the mechanics, it is quick-start power when the grid needs it.

Slide 4. Why the plants exist

Speaker script: These plants are not built to run nonstop. They are built to be ready when the system is under pressure. That could be extreme heat, a cold snap, a generator going offline, a transmission constraint, or a MISO emergency. So when someone says, “They do not run very often,” the answer is: that may be true, but that is also the point. Their value is being available when the grid needs a quick response.

Presenter notes: This is the main reliability point. They do not run often because they are there for tight conditions.

Slide 5. The five Illinois facilities

Speaker script: The five facilities are Venice, Goose Creek, Pinckneyville, Raccoon Creek, and Kinmundy. They are physically located in Illinois, but they are owned and operated by Ameren Missouri, legally Union Electric Company. That distinction matters. The policy conversation is happening in Illinois, the plants support reliability in MISO, and the owner is a Missouri utility. When we talk with legislators, we need to be clear about all three pieces.

Slide 6. Reliability events and on-demand energy

Speaker script: This is where the reliability story becomes real. During the 2026 events shown in the chart, these CTGs produced power when the system was under stress. For scale, Venice’s 562 MW is roughly enough to serve about 500,000 homes at full output. Across all five plants, the fleet is about 2,092 MW, or roughly 1.9 to 2.1 million homes at full fleet output. The point is not that we want these plants running more often. The point is that when MISO needs fast, dispatchable power, these plants can provide it.

Presenter notes: Say “at full output.” The point is scale, not a fixed customer count.

Slide 7. The Venice question

Speaker script: Venice is important because it is the first of these facilities scheduled to retire, with a January 1, 2030 date in the internal materials. The question is not whether Venice should run all the time. It does not. The question is what happens if the grid needs it during an emergency and it is no longer there.

We cannot go back and prove exactly what would have happened if Venice had not run during a past event. But we can say this plainly: in a serious transmission or supply emergency, losing a fast-start resource like Venice could make the situation harder to manage. In the worst case, that can increase the risk of customer interruptions. That is why we need to answer the reliability question before the plant retires, not after.

Presenter notes: Be careful here. Do not say Venice alone prevents outages. Say losing it could make a bad event harder to manage.

Slide 8. CEJA and the policy clock

Speaker script: CEJA sets the clock for these plants. We should be direct about that, and we should also be direct that Illinois has clean-energy goals. The issue is how to manage the transition in a way that protects reliability, keeps customer costs in mind, respects environmental goals, and gives host communities time to plan. The strongest message is simple: do not retire dependable capacity before the replacement is real, connected to the grid, counted for reliability, and able to deliver when needed.

Presenter notes: Keep the tone balanced. Acknowledge CEJA goals, then bring it back to timing and replacement capacity.

Slide 9. Community and tax-base impact

Speaker script: These plants also matter to the communities around them. They pay property taxes that support schools, city services, public safety, roads, libraries, and other local needs. We should not overstate that by saying a specific job or program will definitely be cut. The better way to say it is that these communities have real tax-base exposure. If a plant retires, the community needs a transition plan too.

Presenter notes: Talk about tax-base exposure, not guaranteed cuts. Keep it grounded in schools and local services.

Slide 10. How to talk to legislators

Speaker script: When we talk to legislators, we should avoid making this sound like a choice between reliability and the environment. It is not that simple. The real question is how to handle reliability, affordability, environmental compliance, and community impact at the same time. If someone raises emissions, acknowledge it. If someone asks why the plants run so little, explain the readiness value. The goal is to be clear, balanced, and credible.

Presenter notes: Do not let this become reliability versus the environment. The stronger message is that all of it has to be managed together.

Slide 11. What we need from this group

Speaker script: What we need from this group is simple. First, understand the basic reliability value. Second, know which facts we can use and which ones still need to be checked. Third, help us turn a technical issue into a clear legislative conversation. No one needs to memorize every number. But we do need everyone to understand the story and know where precision matters.

Presenter notes: Remind the group they do not need to be experts. They just need to understand the story and know what needs checking.

Slide 12. Closing message

Speaker script: The bottom line is this: Illinois can pursue cleaner energy and still be thoughtful about reliability, affordability, and local communities. These plants may not run often, but when the grid is under stress, readiness matters. Our job is to help legislators understand that value and make sure retirement decisions are tied to proven replacement capacity and a real transition plan.

Presenter notes: End on balance. Cleaner energy, reliability, affordability, and community transition all have to fit together.

1. What is a CTG facility?

CTG means combustion turbine generator. A CTG works much like a large jet engine connected to an electric generator. Air is compressed, fuel is burned, hot gases expand through turbine blades, and the spinning shaft drives a generator that makes electricity.

Core components

Component

Plain-language function

Air intake and filters

Pulls in and cleans outside air.

Compressor

Squeezes the air so combustion can occur efficiently.

Combustor

Mixes compressed air with fuel and burns it.

Turbine

Hot gases turn turbine blades and the main shaft.

Generator

Converts mechanical rotation into electricity.

Transformer and switchyard

Raises voltage and connects power to the transmission system.

Fuel supply

Delivers natural gas; some units may have a liquid-fuel capability depending on unit configuration.

Controls and protection

Starts, monitors, and safely shuts down equipment.

Emissions controls and monitoring

Reduce or measure pollutants as required by permits and regulation.

Simple analogy
A CTG is a jet engine whose useful output is electricity instead of thrust.

Simple-cycle versus combined-cycle

The five facilities are discussed as fast-start CTG resources. In a simple-cycle arrangement, the turbine produces electricity directly and the exhaust heat is not routed through a steam cycle to generate additional electricity. That generally supports faster starts and simpler operation, but it also means the plant typically uses more fuel per megawatt-hour than a combined-cycle plant. Avoid saying that lower efficiency means lower value. Efficiency measures fuel use; reliability value measures whether the resource is available when the grid needs it.

2. Why these facilities exist

Four roles to communicate

Role

What it means

When it matters

Peaking

Produces electricity during high-demand periods.

Hot afternoons, cold snaps, or unusually high load.

Contingency

Stands ready if another resource unexpectedly fails.

Generator trips, fuel problems, or forced outages.

Local reliability

Provides generation inside the region when imports are limited.

Transmission constraints or heavy regional import dependence.

Emergency response

Responds when MISO takes emergency operating actions.

Max Gen or other declared emergency conditions.

The fire-station analogy

A fire station is not judged by how many hours its trucks are moving. It is judged by whether trained crews and equipment are ready when an emergency occurs. CTGs are similar. Low annual run hours can reflect their intended role, not lack of value.

Vocabulary that lobbyists must master

Term

Plain-language definition

MW

A measure of instantaneous power output or capability.

MWh

One megawatt produced for one hour; a measure of energy.

Nameplate capacity

The manufacturer-rated maximum output of equipment.

Seasonal capability

Output the plant can reliably provide under seasonal conditions.

Accredited capacity

The amount an RTO counts toward meeting reliability requirements.

Capacity factor

Actual energy produced divided by the energy the plant could have produced at full output all year.

Dispatch

An instruction or market outcome that causes a unit to operate.

Fast start

A unit that can move from offline to producing power relatively quickly.

Resource adequacy

Having enough supply capability to meet demand plus a reserve margin.

Deliverability

Whether power can reach customers through the transmission system.

5. Emissions and environmental performance

What comes from combustion

Category

Examples

Plain-language explanation

Greenhouse gases

Primarily carbon dioxide; methane and nitrous oxide are included in CO2-equivalent accounting

These gases contribute to climate change.

Criteria pollutants / co-pollutants

Nitrogen oxides, carbon monoxide, particulate matter, sulfur dioxide, ozone-related pollutants, and lead categories referenced in CEJA materials

These pollutants affect local or regional air quality and are controlled through permits, equipment, operating practices, and emissions limits.

Fuel-cycle impacts

Upstream methane and fuel production impacts

Plant-stack data do not capture every upstream impact of producing and transporting fuel.

How to explain annual emissions fairly

  • Natural gas combustion is not zero-emission.
  • A simple-cycle peaker may have a higher emissions rate per MWh than a highly efficient combined-cycle unit.
  • Annual emissions depend on both the emissions rate and how many hours the unit operates.
  • Low-run-hour units may have relatively low annual totals while still producing emissions during operation.
  • Do not use a single unit-level NOx rate or an outdated emissions year as a fleet-wide statement.

Safe plain-language phrasing
These facilities burn natural gas, so they emit carbon dioxide and regulated air pollutants when they run. Most operate infrequently, which limits annual emissions, but low annual use does not make them emission-free. Their policy value must be weighed against environmental goals and available replacement resources.

CEJA emissions framework

The attached PJM material states that CEJA requires certain fossil-fueled units to reduce greenhouse-gas-equivalent emissions and specified co-pollutants to zero by January 1, 2030, effectively requiring covered units to retire. That provision is a process-based exception, not a blanket guarantee that every unit will continue operating.

The policy test

Replacement capability means more than adding new megawatts on paper. A replacement resource must be commercially available, interconnected to the grid, accredited for reliability, and deliverable to the area during stressed conditions. That is why wind, solar, batteries, demand response, and transmission can all be part of the answer, but none should be treated as a one-for-one substitute without reviewing timing, accreditation, duration, location, and deliverability.

Five questions before retirement
1. Is replacement capacity built?
2. Is it interconnected?
3. Is it accredited for reliability?
4. Can it deliver power into the affected area during stress?
5. Is there a credible transition plan for local tax revenue and services?

9. Advocacy and policy framing

Core policy thesis
The strongest argument is not that these plants should run more often or remain online indefinitely. The strongest argument is that retirement timing should be matched to verified replacement capability, grid deliverability, customer affordability, environmental compliance, and a credible plan for affected communities.

The balanced frame

Principle

Message

Reliability

Keep dispatchable capacity available until replacement resources are built, interconnected, accredited, and deliverable.

Affordability

Avoid a timing mismatch that increases scarcity, import dependence, or capacity costs borne by customers.

Environment

Acknowledge emissions and CEJA goals directly. Do not argue that natural gas is emission-free.

Community

Plan for tax-base and public-service impacts before statutory closure dates.

Credibility

Use third-party studies, consistent capacity definitions, unit-level validation, and transparent caveats.

Local voices

Host communities should understand the timeline and have a meaningful role in transition planning.

  • If these plants are so important, why do some run only a few dozen hours?

10. Plain-language scripts

Stakeholder preparation checklist
Before meeting with a legislator or staff member, prepare three items: the facility-specific facts for that district or region, the safest explanation of the plant’s reliability role, and the community-impact numbers that have been reviewed for external use. Also prepare one sentence acknowledging emissions and CEJA goals, because credibility depends on addressing the environmental issue directly rather than waiting for the question.

30-second version

Ameren Missouri owns five natural gas combustion-turbine facilities in central and southern Illinois. They are fast-start resources, meaning they can come online when demand spikes, another generator fails, transmission is constrained, or MISO declares an emergency. Most do not run often, but their value is being ready when the grid is stressed. CEJA sets emissions and retirement deadlines, with Venice facing the nearest date in 2030. The policy question is whether dependable replacement capacity and a community transition plan will be in place before these resources retire.

Two-minute briefing

The five facilities are Venice, Goose Creek, Pinckneyville, Raccoon Creek, and Kinmundy. They are located in Illinois but owned and operated by Ameren Missouri, legally Union Electric Company. They are combustion turbines, which work much like jet engines connected to electric generators. Their job is not to run around the clock. Their job is to start relatively quickly and give MISO a dispatchable resource during peak demand, outages, transmission constraints, and emergency conditions.

That is why annual run hours must be interpreted carefully. In the cited 2024 data, Pinckneyville operated far more than the other facilities, while several ran only a small number of hours. Low use can be consistent with a peaking or contingency role. At the same time, these plants burn natural gas and produce greenhouse gases and regulated air pollutants when they operate, so environmental impacts must be acknowledged directly.

CEJA creates the policy clock. Internal materials identify Venice for retirement by January 1, 2030 and the remaining four facilities for 2040. The decision is not simply whether to keep fossil generation or delay clean-energy goals. The decision is whether replacement resources are built, interconnected, accredited, and deliverable before retirement, and whether host communities have a plan for the loss of property-tax revenue supporting schools and local services.

Appendix C. Glossary

Term

Definition

Accreditation

The capacity value recognized by the RTO for reliability planning.

Baseload

Generation designed or economically suited to run for long periods.

Capacity

The ability to produce electricity at a point in time.

Capacity factor

Actual energy output divided by maximum theoretical output for the period.

CEJA

Illinois Climate and Equitable Jobs Act.

Co-pollutant

A regulated air pollutant addressed alongside greenhouse-gas reductions.

CTG

Combustion turbine generator.

Dispatch

Instruction or market outcome to operate a resource.

EJ community

Environmental Justice community under applicable Illinois criteria.

ELCC

Effective Load Carrying Capability; a method for estimating a resource’s reliability contribution.

Firm / dependable capacity

Capability expected to be available under defined reliability conditions.

MISO

Midcontinent Independent System Operator.

Nameplate

Manufacturer-rated maximum output.

NOx

Nitrogen oxides, pollutants associated with combustion and ozone formation.

Peaker

A unit used mainly during high-demand or high-value periods.

Resource adequacy

Enough supply and reserve capability to meet demand.

RTO

Regional Transmission Organization.

Simple cycle

Gas-turbine generation without a steam-bottoming cycle.

Transmission constraint

A limit on moving electricity through the grid.

12-month rolling limit

A compliance measure recalculated each month using the previous twelve months.
