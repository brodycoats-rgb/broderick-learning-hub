Grid Outreach: Legislator Conversation Guide

Reliable. Affordable. Ready.

Prepared for meetings with Senator David Koehler (Peoria) and Representative Katie Stuart (Edwardsville)

September 2026

How to Use This Guide

The meeting objective is to help the member understand why the pending Multi-Year Grid Plan matters now and how planned grid investment protects both reliability and affordability. Lead with the issue most relevant to the member, use one strong proof point, connect it to customers in the district, and close by asking for support for a timely, thorough review of the Grid Plan and aligned state planning.

Each meeting should leave the member with three messages. The distribution grid is under growing strain, planned work costs less than emergency repair, and the pending Grid Plan is the near-term decision that matters.

Each section offers a plain-language explanation, a short script, and responses to likely questions. Use the scripts as preparation rather than reading them word for word. Member profiles appear at the end of the guide.

Suggested entry point by member

  • Senator Koehler. Consider opening with clean energy readiness and value, given his long engagement on energy policy.

  • Representative Stuart. Consider opening with severe weather and affordability, tied to local schools and constituents.

Openers

Open with a minute or two of genuine conversation, adjusted to the length of the meeting and your relationship with the member. These early exchanges often show what is on the member’s mind heading into the fall.

Elections and the Veto Session

Keep it light, curious, and nonpartisan. Ask how the season feels, not who will win.

“How is the fall shaping up for you? It feels like everyone’s calendar got shorter this year.”

“What are you hearing from people back home? Are energy bills coming up much?”

“Do you have a sense of what may move in veto session and what is more likely to wait until spring?”

Listen for

  • Whether constituents are raising energy affordability unprompted. If so, that is a natural opening for the value point.

  • Whether they expect a crowded veto session. That signals how much time they have for grid-related issues.

Personal Openers

“Before we get into it, how is the family doing?”

“Did you get a break this summer, or did the schedule keep moving?”

Use personal questions only when they fit the relationship. Keep them short and genuine, and if the member wants to talk, let them.

The Wider Energy Market

This is the bridge from small talk to substance. It shows you are thinking about the whole system, not just one filing.

“After nearly twenty years of mostly flat demand, we are seeing growth again from data centers, manufacturing, and electrification. The challenge is that much of it is arriving at the same time, on a system built for a very different pattern of use.”

“Capacity prices got everyone’s attention because supply is tightening as demand grows. We also have to make sure the local delivery system can carry that power where customers need it.”

“Generation gets most of the attention. The distribution grid is what determines whether that power reaches homes and businesses reliably.”

The Core Story in Plain Language

The story rests on three messages. The grid is under growing strain from aging equipment, severe weather, and new demand. Planned work costs less than emergency repair. The Grid Plan is the near-term decision that matters. The four points below supply the proof, followed by the ask.

1. The Grid Was Built for a Different Era

Plain version

Most poles and wires in our territory were installed decades ago for steady demand and one-way power flow from a plant to a customer. Today, power moves in both directions, and demand is growing again after roughly twenty years of little change.

Script

“About three quarters of our in-service wood poles are at least 30 years old. That does not mean every pole needs to be replaced now, but it does mean the work has to be prioritized over time. The choice is whether we replace equipment on a planned schedule or during an outage, when the work is more disruptive and expensive.”

2. Severe Weather Is Putting More Stress on the Grid

Plain version

Recent tornado activity and warning levels show that severe-weather exposure is not theoretical. Storm hardening is a practical response to conditions that are already affecting communities across the state.

Script

“As of [date], Illinois has recorded more than 200 confirmed tornadoes and 463 tornado warnings in 2026. Storm hardening is not about a distant risk. It is about helping schools, farms, hospitals, and local businesses recover faster when severe weather hits.”

3. Clean Energy Only Works if the Grid Can Take It

Plain version

Every solar array and every wind project has to connect to something. The interconnection growth in our territory has been extraordinary, and the grid has to be built to absorb it safely.

Script

“In 2015, we had 493 distributed energy interconnections. Today, we have more than 40,000, and connected capacity has grown from 8 megawatts to 967. That is real progress, but it also means the grid has to manage power moving in both directions. Continued growth depends on a system that can do that safely and reliably.”

4. Planned Work Costs Less Than Emergency Repair

Plain version

This is the affordability argument. Planned investment can reduce the higher costs of emergency replacement and prolonged outages.

Script

“Grid investment has a cost, and it should be reviewed carefully. The affordability case is that planned storm-hardening work can avoid two to five dollars in later costs for every dollar invested. Doing the work deliberately is generally less expensive than replacing equipment during an emergency.”

The Ask

“We are asking for your support for a timely, thorough review of the Grid Plan and for keeping the state’s related planning efforts aligned. We are not asking you to intervene in the Illinois Commerce Commission’s process. We want you to understand what is being considered and why the timing matters.”

Key Dates and Process

Members often ask about timing as much as substance. Review these dates before each meeting.

Item What it is Timing
Resource Adequacy Study Statewide study of supply sufficiency by the Illinois Commerce Commission (ICC), Illinois Power Agency (IPA), and Illinois Environmental Protection Agency (IEPA), required under Section 9.15(o) Issued December 15, 2025
Clean and Reliable Grid Affordability Act (CRGA), Senate Bill 25 State energy law that created the new statewide Integrated Resource Plan framework Passed October 2025, effective June 1, 2026
Mitigation Plan Agency plan addressing shortfalls identified in the Resource Adequacy Study, with a 60-day stakeholder comment window after the draft and 30 days for agencies to revise and file Draft expected October 2026
Veto session Legislative window where energy items may resurface Fall 2026
Integrated Resource Plan (IRP) New statewide plan compiled by ICC Staff with the IPA, IEPA, and Illinois Finance Authority Initial IRP due to the ICC by November 15, 2026, with the process running through 2027
Multi-Year Grid Plan, 2028 to 2031 Ameren Illinois electric distribution capital investment plan filed with the ICC, Docket 26-0051 Filed January 2026, ICC decision expected December 2026

Script for the timing question

“This fall matters because several related decisions are moving at once. The draft Mitigation Plan is expected in October, the statewide IRP is due in November, and the Grid Plan decision is expected in December. They cover different parts of the system, but their assumptions and timelines need to line up.”

Objection Handling

Acknowledge the concern, clarify the distinction, then answer with one fact.

If they say You say
“Bills are already too high.” I understand. Much of the recent increase came from regional supply and capacity costs, while this plan addresses the local delivery system. It still has a customer cost, so the investments should be tested carefully. The value is in reducing outage impacts and avoiding more expensive emergency work later.
“Why not wait a year?” A year may sound modest, but the underlying need keeps growing. Delaying planned work can mean more emergency repairs and less flexibility in managing the cost. The better question is whether the proposed work is necessary and paced responsibly.
“This is just utility profit.” That concern is fair. The ICC reviews the plan, and the utility is accountable to reliability measures with financial consequences for missing them. Each investment should be judged on whether it is needed and delivers measurable customer value.
“Can’t clean energy solve this?” Clean energy is part of the answer, but every project still has to connect to the distribution system. As more power flows in both directions, the grid needs upgrades to handle it safely and keep new connections moving.
“What does this do for my district?” It means a stronger local system for schools, hospitals, farms, homes, and businesses. Bring district-specific reliability needs and planned work so the answer is concrete.

Closing the Meeting

“Thank you for the time. To sum up, we would appreciate your support for a timely, thorough review of the Grid Plan. If questions come up, please call me. I will give you a direct answer and follow up when more information is needed.”

Leave-Behind Materials

  • Grid Plan one-page overview

  • District-specific reliability and investment figures, pulled before the meeting

  • Your business card with your cell number written on it

Senator David Koehler, Peoria

Background

  • Long-serving state senator with deep familiarity with the energy sector, including the major omnibus energy debates.

  • Consistent clean energy advocate, with particular history on transportation electrification and state-owned fleet vehicles.

  • Represents the Peoria area, where regional economic development and manufacturing are important.

Suggested Approach

Given his experience, skip the basics. Focus on how distribution-system readiness connects the clean energy policies he has supported to the results he wants to see.

Script

“Senator, you have worked on electrification for years, including the state fleet. The next question is whether the distribution system is ready for the added load. Fleet charging can place a lot of demand on one circuit at the same time, so the policy goal and the grid plan have to move together.”

“The growth in rooftop and community solar shows the policy is taking hold. The Grid Plan is about making sure the system can keep up with it.”

Topics to Explore

  • Fleet electrification as a distribution planning issue. Depot charging concentrates load in one place at one time, which makes it a wires question before it is a vehicle question.

  • Interconnection timelines. Long queues can slow clean energy development, so connect grid investment to faster, more predictable interconnections.

Likely Question

“Is this rate base growth presented as reliability?”

“That is a fair question. The ICC reviews every part of the plan, and we are accountable to reliability measures with financial consequences. I am glad to walk through how any specific investment is justified.”

Representative Katie Stuart, Edwardsville

Background

  • Former teacher who values clear explanations and plain language.

  • Not an energy specialist, but well informed on the issues. Treat her as an informed generalist.

  • Represents a Metro East district with schools, small businesses, and many constituents who felt recent bill increases directly.

Suggested Approach

Lead with people and places rather than technical figures. Use one number per point and tie it to local examples such as schools and small businesses.

Script

“Representative, about three quarters of our in-service wood poles are at least 30 years old. At the same time, Illinois is seeing significant severe weather. The practical question is whether we strengthen the system in a planned way or wait until more equipment fails during a storm.”

“When a storm knocks out power, schools close, cafeterias lose food, and families scramble for childcare. Planned upgrades are how we reduce those disruptions.”

Helpful Explanations

Simple analogies can make the technical points easier to follow.

  • The roof. You can replace shingles on a schedule, or you can replace the ceiling after the rain comes through. Same roof, very different bill.

  • The two-way street. The old grid was a one-way street from the power plant to the home. Rooftop solar turns it into a two-way street, which needs different signals, signs, and intersections.

Likely Question

“My constituents just saw their bills go up. Why are you asking for more?”

“That is completely fair. Most of that increase came from regional supply costs, not the local delivery system. We are not asking anyone to take this on faith. The Commission should test every investment, and we will show you what the plan means for your district specifically.”
