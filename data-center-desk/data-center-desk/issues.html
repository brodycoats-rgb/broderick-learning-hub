Issues

Every core issue, sorted by lane

Where the company takes a public stance, where it holds a view and lets others lead, and where it watches. Each issue gets the plain version and a line you can say.

Active Public stance

  • Directly on the utility
  • We speak first and clearly
  • Tariffs, assurance, who decides, supply mechanics, reliability, readiness, utility role

Passive Hold a view

  • Not the utility's to lead
  • We speak when service, timing, or customer cost is touched
  • Water, air, local control, labor, biometric privacy, narrow bills

Watch Watch and prepare

  • Could move up by amendment
  • Know the bill and keep a line ready
  • Reporting, nondisclosure agreements, incentives, clean energy matching
The lane test, five questions in order
  1. Does it change who pays for the wires, substations, or generation serving our customers, or take away a protection? Yes means Active.
  2. Does it give the utility a duty, or touch the ICC's authority, customer choice, or MISO and PJM rules? Yes means Active.
  3. Does it change when or where large load shows up, or a customer's service date? Yes means Passive with a watch on timing.
  4. Could an amendment make it do any of the first three? Yes means Watch.
  5. None of the above. Passive, stay informed.

Lanes are proposals for discussion by Government Relations leadership. They are not approved company positions. Eric and Betsy used the active and passive framing on the October 8 call.

Who pays for the wires

Active Large load tariffs and cost allocation

In plain words. A large load tariff is a special rate structure for customers whose demand, construction needs, and project risk are far bigger than a typical customer's. Cost allocation decides who pays for generation, transmission, substations, and lines.

Who is saying what. The Governor wants a data center rate class that carries its own costs. Consumer advocates and environmental groups want no cost shift to homes. Industry wants a predictable path. Ohio, Texas, Oregon, and Minnesota have all written large load rules.

Bills. POWER Act (HB5513, SB4016), SB3830

Why this lane. It sets what our customers pay and what we build, and the ICC already has authority over it.

Proposed stance. Support the goal of cost causation and a data center class. Keep tariff terms with the ICC on a full record. Resist hard coded rates and arbitrary multipliers.

What moves it. Already active. Escalate the same day if a bill fixes rates in statute.

Say it plainlyLarge load customers should carry the costs and risks they create. The statute sets the principle, and the Commerce Commission sets the tariff on a full record.

Protection if a project never arrives

Active Financial assurance, minimum bills, exit charges

In plain words. The biggest risk sits between the day the utility starts spending and the day the customer is firmly committed. If the project is delayed, shrunk, or canceled, wires and equipment can sit unused. Deposits, collateral, minimum bills, and exit charges each cover a different slice of that gap.

Who is saying what. HB4172 would bar utilities from requiring deposits or letters of credit from large new customers under ICC tariffs. Developers say heavy up front requirements slow credible projects. Consumer advocates want assurance that is real and collectible.

Bills. HB4172 (in tension), POWER Act tariff language, SB3830

Why this lane. It is the clearest case of risk shifting to customers who did not cause it.

Proposed stance. Preserve financial assurance. Match it to the exposure and the milestone, step it down as the project proves itself, and credit equipment that can serve another customer.

What moves it. Already active. Watch for HB4172 language folded into a larger package.

Say it plainlyA deposit, a minimum bill, and an exit charge can each cover a different part of the risk, but they have to fit together so the same cost is not recovered twice.

Who writes which rule

Active Statute against the ICC against MISO and FERC

In plain words. A good statute sets principles and protections. The technical record, including rate design, belongs to the ICC. Grid operations, interconnection, and wholesale rules belong to MISO, PJM, and FERC. Asking the wrong body to do a job produces rules that are wrong in both directions.

Who is saying what. Sponsors want certainty in the text. The company, the ICC, and regional operators want room to fit rules to evidence. FERC ordered six regional operators on June 18, 2026 to justify or reform their large load rules, so state law must leave that authority intact.

Bills. POWER Act savings clause language, SB3830

Why this lane. It decides whether the details are written on a full record or in a hurry.

Proposed stance. Principles in statute, details at the ICC and MISO. Ask every bill for a savings clause that preserves federal and regional authority.

What moves it. Already active.

Say it plainlyThe legislature should set the consumer protection goal. The Commission is better placed to test evidence, allocate costs, and update technical terms in an open proceeding.

Bring your own power

Active Passive Mechanics and definitions Clean against gas politics

In plain words. The phrase means different things to different people. It can mean new energy, dependable capacity, on site generation, flexible load, or just paying the full cost. Until a bill says which one, it cannot be tested for reliability, timing, or who enforces it.

Who is saying what. Environmental groups want new clean power only and oppose counting gas. Some legislators and labor want data centers able to bring their own gas, and want nuclear to count. Operators prefer paying into a program or buying credits. Consumer advocates and the Illinois Power Agency start with cost. Betsy flagged this as a major area of contention.

Bills. POWER Act, HB5607, SB3761, SB3830, HR744

Why this lane. The mechanics reach delivery, deliverability, and the customer choice law, so we have a say there. The clean against gas fight is not ours to referee.

Proposed stance. Be active on definitions. Is the supply new, deliverable, in the right market area, available when the load runs, and independently verified. Stay out of the choice between clean and gas.

What moves it. If a bill gives the utility a supply or emissions duty, or makes delivery depend on a customer's supply contract, treat all of it as active.

Say it plainlyThat phrase needs a definition. Do we mean new energy, dependable capacity, on site generation, flexible demand, or payment of attributable costs? Those solve different problems.

Reliability, interruptible service, and the two grids

Active Curtailment MISO against PJM

In plain words. Data center load behaves differently from a factory. If many sites drop to backup power at once, generation briefly exceeds load and operators must cut output fast. Promises to curtail need equipment, testing, and consequences. PJM mostly worries about capacity. MISO mostly worries about technical integration.

Who is saying what. The Governor wants reliability for families first, through interruptible service tied to how much clean energy a project supplies. Operators want predictability. Regional operators want data, models, and ride through.

Bills. POWER Act proportional peak use idea, no standalone bill

Why this lane. We run the wires. Who is curtailed, when, and how it is checked affects both service and our regional obligations.

Proposed stance. Support interruptible service only if triggers, notice, compensation, and performance are written down and consistent with MISO rules.

What moves it. Already active. Check any statewide rule against both RTOs before supporting it.

Say it plainlyA promise to curtail needs equipment, testing, and consequences. And MISO and PJM are not the same, so one rule cannot fit both.

Readiness and speed of connection

Active The queue is not the forecast

In plain words. Customers want service in 18 to 36 months, faster than generation and transmission get built. Requests overlap, shrink, and vanish. A readiness ladder moves a project from inquiry to application, study, commitment, construction, energization, and operation, with proof at each step.

Who is saying what. Developers want speed and certainty. Economic development wants to win credible projects. Consumer advocates want speculation screened out.

Bills. Not a standalone bill. Appears in tariff language.

Why this lane. Readiness decides when we build, and Economic Development shares it with us.

Proposed stance. Back site control, paid studies, a credible ramp, and staged commitments. Reasonable readiness standards speed serious projects by separating them from speculative requests.

What moves it. Already active. Coordinate with Economic Development before any public line.

Say it plainlyThe queue is not the forecast. We count readiness, not announcements.

The utility's role and customer choice

Active Delivery against supply Backup generation

In plain words. In Illinois the company that delivers electricity and the company that supplies it may differ. A bill that asks the delivery utility to guarantee a customer's clean energy purchase, or to answer for a customer's backup generators, asks for something the utility does not control.

Who is saying what. Sponsors often write supply goals into delivery law. Alternative suppliers and customers value choice.

Bills. SB3830, POWER Act supply provisions

Why this lane. It decides whether we carry duties we cannot meet.

Proposed stance. Support clear utility roles. Resist assigning the utility responsibility for competitive supply or customer owned backup generation.

What moves it. Already active.

Say it plainlyOur utilities deliver power. Many large customers buy supply competitively. Requirements should respect that.

Reporting and nondisclosure agreements

Watch Energy and water reporting Ending NDAs

In plain words. Reporting bills ask data centers to disclose energy and water use. Other proposals would void nondisclosure agreements that keep projects secret from local officials. Both aim at transparency, and both can collide with trade secrets and security.

Who is saying what. Local officials and residents want to know what is coming. Developers want confidentiality. Economic development relies on confidentiality while it works with prospects.

Bills. SB2181, SB4004, POWER Act NDA ban

Why this lane. Energy data could become a utility reporting duty by amendment, and a broad NDA ban could change how early we learn about a project.

Proposed stance. Support transparency with protection for trade secrets and critical infrastructure. Avoid overlapping reports that several agencies collect.

What moves it. Moves to active if a bill asks the utility to collect or publish customer data, or if the NDA ban covers utility and economic development work.

Say it plainlyTransparency matters, and so does protecting security sensitive and trade secret information. The question is what informs the public without exposing what must be protected.

Tax incentives and the pause

Watch DCEO program Accountability

In plain words. The 2019 program exempts sales and use tax for qualifying projects. The Governor paused new agreements on July 1, 2026. The debate now is how to judge whether the incentives were worth it.

Who is saying what. Labor and industry want the program restored. Environmental groups and the Governor want conditions. The Illinois Policy Institute questioned whether the pause was within the Governor's power.

Bills. SB3120 (water conditions), POWER Act, administrative action

Why this lane. Incentives decide which projects come to Illinois and where, and so how much load we may serve.

Proposed stance. Hold an internal view with Economic Development. Support accountability for performance. Leave the program design to DCEO.

What moves it. Moves to active if incentive conditions are tied to utility service, readiness, or tariff terms.

Say it plainlyIllinois should compare verified investment, jobs, property tax, and community benefits against what the exemption costs, then tie any future incentive to measurable performance.

Clean energy matching

Watch Annual against hourly Additionality

In plain words. A project may buy clean energy attributes for a year and still use the grid at particular hours. Hourly matching, accredited capacity, and transmission access are separate questions. Additionality asks whether a commitment adds a new resource or relabels an old one.

Who is saying what. Environmental groups want additional, hourly, in region supply. Industry wants flexibility. The Illinois Power Agency would administer credit programs under HB5607.

Bills. HB5607, SB3761, POWER Act

Why this lane. Procurement design reaches the supply side and the Illinois Power Agency more than the wires, for now.

Proposed stance. Know the bill. Ask what resource or operating outcome must be delivered and when. Speak up if matching rules strand or delay a customer's service.

What moves it. Moves to active if it changes service timing or places a duty on delivery.

Say it plainlyBefore endorsing a clean power claim, ask matched over what period, supplied under what arrangement, measured at which boundary, and compared with what baseline.

Water

Passive Closed loop systems Mahomet Aquifer

In plain words. Data centers use water directly for cooling and indirectly through power generation. Withdrawal is water taken. Consumption is the part not returned. Source matters as much as quantity, so potable, reclaimed, ground, and surface water pose different limits.

Who is saying what. The Governor wants permits, disclosure, and closed loop systems. Downstate Republicans want aquifer protection. Industry wants site specific standards over bans.

Bills. SB4004, SB3120, SB2181, POWER Act

Why this lane. It is not an electric utility duty, but it decides which projects can be built and how the public sees them.

Proposed stance. Hold a view. Water rules should be source and impact specific. Let water agencies and the Governor's office lead.

What moves it. Speak up if a water rule blocks load we are planning to serve.

Say it plainlyWater rules should name the source and the measure. A low PUE does not settle a community's water concern.

Backup generator air standards

Passive Cumulative impact

In plain words. Backup generators run for tests and emergencies and can affect air quality near homes. The Governor asked for strong standards and attention to environmental justice communities.

Who is saying what. Environmental groups and the Governor's office lead. Industry wants permits that fit testing needs.

Bills. POWER Act, no standalone bill

Why this lane. The edge for us is any rule that treats backup generators as grid support or assigns them to the utility.

Proposed stance. Defer to the Illinois EPA. Resist making the utility responsible for customer owned generators.

What moves it. Speak up only if a rule counts backup generators as grid resources.

Say it plainlyBackup generation is not automatically available grid support. Ask about fuel, permits, synchronization, control, and operating limits.

Local control, siting, and community benefits

Passive Zoning Setbacks Host agreements

In plain words. Who decides where a data center goes and what rules it follows. Towns are moving faster than Springfield. The pieces are siting standards, ending nondisclosure agreements, and community benefit agreements.

Who is saying what. Eric said an industry group is likely to lead and Ameren probably would not take a position. Betsy asked how much of it is the company's problem. Counties worry about preemption. Downstate Republicans want local decisions.

Bills. HB5818, SB4206 and SB1050 Amendment 1, HB5755, SB4203, Senate GOP bill

Why this lane. Different local rules change when and where load shows up, and when it is firm enough to plan for.

Proposed stance. Track it, hold an internal view, and speak when it changes service, timing, or customer cost.

What moves it. Moves up if a referendum or moratorium could delay load already in our plans.

Say it plainlyLocal control is growing because towns are moving faster than the state. We probably would not lead, but it affects where load shows up and when, so we know where each bill stands.

Labor and workforce

Passive Construction jobs Bring your own gas Standards

In plain words. Data centers are some of the largest construction jobs in the country, and the building trades know it. Union opposition helped stop the regulatory bills this spring. Other states tie incentives to wages and training.

Who is saying what. Labor wants incentives restored, gas and nuclear to count, and biometric privacy addressed. Environmental groups oppose gas. Pennsylvania and New Jersey have moved toward labor terms in their standards.

Bills. No 2026 Illinois bill found with labor terms

Why this lane. Labor can decide the outcome of a package, and many of our own employees and contractors are union members.

Proposed stance. Treat labor as its own stakeholder. Do not state a union position beyond what a source supports. Clear any public remark first.

What moves it. Moves to active if gas language reaches existing gas units we want to retain or changes how new supply is counted.

Say it plainlyLabor is not a side issue. Unions helped stop the regulatory bills this spring, and some labor and legislators want data centers able to bring their own gas.

Biometric privacy law

Passive BIPA

In plain words. The Biometric Information Privacy Act governs collecting and storing biometric data. Data centers can use fingerprint or face scans for secure areas. The 2024 amendment changed damages for repeated conduct but kept the duties.

Who is saying what. Industry wants relief. Trial lawyers and progressive members oppose meaningful changes. Internal reporting calls it a major hurdle to any comprehensive bill.

Bills. Not in the bills above. A political price of a broader deal.

Why this lane. It is not about the grid, but it may decide whether the package passes at all.

Proposed stance. Stay out of the merits. Watch it because it sets the timing and the coalition.

What moves it. Not expected to move up.

Say it plainlySome stakeholders cite it as an investment concern, but location claims should be supported project by project.

Foreign ownership, crypto noise, and property compensation

Passive Outside the core

In plain words. Three bills cover narrow things. SB3578 blocks foreign adversary owned data centers unless agencies certify the load is fully self generated. SB3030 sets noise rules for cryptocurrency mining. HB4319 lets owners within 1,000 feet seek compensation.

Who is saying what. Sponsors in each case are focused on their own concern.

Bills. SB3578, SB3030, HB4319

Why this lane. Not directly connected to the utility.

Proposed stance. Do not generalize. SB3578 does not apply to every foreign owned business, and SB3030 does not apply to all data centers.

What moves it. Not expected to move up.

Say it plainlyThese bills cover narrow things. I would not apply their provisions to every data center.
