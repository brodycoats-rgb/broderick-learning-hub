What stands out

Six things worth knowing before the details.

Say it plainly

Here is the short version. We are watching fifteen data center bills. Almost everyone agrees data centers should bring their own power and carry their own costs. The fight is over how strict the rules are, how fast they start, and whether they make Illinois a harder place to build than the states next door. Two things need attention this week. Our tracker still lists HB5607 and HB5826 as pending even though the SME feedback reads as opposed. And HB5826 sets the lowest size threshold I found anywhere, at 5 megawatts.

Position gap

The tracker tags 3 bills Oppose, 4 Neutral, 3 Pending and leaves 5 untagged. SME feedback is on file for 9 of the 15, and it recommends oppose on 5 and neutral with concerns on 4 (SB4016 is split). HB5607 and HB5826 are both Pending in the tracker while the SME text on both reads oppose.

Timing

HB5826 was filed October 19. SME feedback is due October 27. The veto session is scheduled for November 17 to 19 and December 1 to 3, and the Governor says he expects a data center bill to be ready for it.

The outlier

HB5826 sets a 5MW threshold, the lowest in the comparison set. Measures elsewhere start at 10MW to 150MW and mostly work through contracts, minimum payments and collateral. Among the measures reviewed here, none pairs hourly matching with per MWh penalties and a disconnection path.

Against the trend

HB4172 is the only bill that loosens utility protections. Every other cost bill, and nearly every state and regulator, is tightening them through deposits, minimum demand charges and exit fees.

Convergence

Bring your own energy is the one idea with support across the Governor, the POWER Act sponsors, Republican candidate Bailey and HR744. The fight is over how prescriptive it is, how fast it starts and what it costs Illinois in siting.

Data checks

SB3830's dashboard summary describes only the self-direct program while its SME feedback describes the tariff. HB5513 and SB4016 carry nearly identical SME text. Six bills have no SME feedback yet (HR744, SB3761, SB4004, SB3120, SB3030, SB2181).

Energy sourcing and clean energy matching

Bills that tell data centers where their power must come from, or reward them for bringing their own.

JHHB5826Data Center Energy Accountability ActTracker PendingSME Oppose
30 second script

Representative Hoffman filed this on October 19. Starting in 2028, large data centers would have to match their power use with clean energy every hour, using newer resources only. If they fall short they pay into a new state fund, and repeated misses could lead to a disconnection case at the ICC. Our SMEs oppose it as too prescriptive, with a 5 megawatt threshold that is too low and a data center definition that is too loose. Feedback is due October 27.

Sponsor
Jay Hoffman (D)
Status
Filed October 19, 2026 (in the House)

In plain language

HB5826 creates the Data Center Energy Accountability Act. Starting in 2028, large data centers would have to match their yearly electricity use with clean energy certificates, tracked hour by hour, from facilities no more than seven years past their start date. Falling short means paying per megawatt hour into a new state fund called the Electron Accelerator Fund, and repeated noncompliance could lead to a utility disconnection proceeding at the ICC. The big question ahead is how this rigid sourcing approach affects data center siting in Illinois compared with neighboring states.

What it requires

  • Covers data centers at a highest possible hourly demand of 5MW or greater (threshold per your SME document, verify against bill text).
  • Beginning in compliance year 2028, account for electricity demand hour by hour and retire eligible environmental attribute certificates equal to 100% of annual State consumption.
  • Eligible resources and batteries must have a commercial operation date no more than 7 years before the compliance year. Nuclear procurement is limited to 33% of usage, per the SME read.
  • Shortfalls trigger an alternative investment payment per MWh into the new Electron Accelerator Fund, run by the IPA.
  • Owners notify the IPA on interconnection. The IPA may file a utility disconnection proceeding at the ICC against a data center that does not comply responsibly.

SME feedback

We oppose this bill in its current form. It is overly prescriptive. Data centers at 5MW or greater would have to bring their own energy through owned or contracted resources, with rules that favor wind and solar and batteries and limit nuclear to 33 percent of usage. Shortfalls carry a $100/MWh penalty before 2030, then $150/MWh for noncompliant use before noon and $200/MWh after noon. The requirements make Illinois a harder place to locate than neighboring states, and losing hyperscaler load could mean higher average transmission rates plus lost tax revenue, jobs and local tax base, though the exact impact is difficult to quantify. The 5MW threshold is too low, and the term data center is not defined, so it could capture existing businesses with on-site compute needs, such as large insurance companies. Suggested improvements include applying requirements to any incremental large load regardless of end use, broadening eligible resources to carbon free generation and emerging technologies, providing a realistic implementation timeline with protection for existing or committed projects, and focusing on actual cost to serve rather than a specific industry.

Exposure for Ameren Illinois

No direct cost to Ameren Illinois, but a utility disconnection proceeding puts the ICC and utilities in the enforcement path, and lost hyperscale load would reduce the growth that helps spread fixed costs.

Suggested improvements

Apply to incremental large load of any end use, broaden eligible resources to carbon free and emerging technologies, add a realistic implementation timeline with protection for existing and committed projects, and tie obligations to actual cost to serve.

Arguments in favor

  • Gives the "bring your own energy" idea real teeth, which matches what the Governor, the POWER Act sponsors and Republican candidates all say they want.
  • Hourly accounting aims to stop paper matching and ties new clean supply to when data centers actually draw power.
  • The age limit steers procurement toward new resources rather than relabeling existing plants.
  • Clear dollar penalties give developers a known price for falling short.

Concerns

  • The 5MW threshold is the lowest of any comparable measure in the country and could capture ordinary business computing.
  • "Data center" is not defined, so large insurers and similar businesses with on-site compute could be swept in.
  • Resource eligibility favors wind, solar and batteries and limits nuclear to 33%, which cuts against technology neutral planning and firm supply.
  • Penalties of $100 to $200 per MWh could total many millions a year for a mid-size facility (see the model below).
  • Illinois would be harder to site in than neighboring states, risking load growth, tax base and downward pressure on average transmission rates.
Watch Filed October 19, 2026, one month before the veto session. SME feedback is due October 27. Whether this is a vehicle, a supplement or a competitor to the POWER Act is unknown.
JHHB5607Advanced Technology LeadershipTracker PendingSME Oppose
30 second script

Also from Representative Hoffman. It would require large data centers to buy and retire clean energy certificates, and later match their use hour by hour with clean power from the same regional grid. The Illinois Power Agency would decide which data centers are covered. Our SME feedback reads as opposed, mainly because it adds regulatory uncertainty, especially for downstate communities. The tracker still shows it as pending.

Sponsor
Jay Hoffman (D)
Status
Rule 19(a) / Re-referred to Rules Committee (in the House)

In plain language

The bill would require large Illinois data centers to increase their use of clean energy by purchasing and retiring verified clean energy certificates. Over time, they would also need to match their electricity use with clean energy production on an hour by hour basis, and the clean energy would have to come from facilities within the same regional power grid. The Illinois Power Agency would decide which data centers are covered and would oversee compliance while working to keep administrative costs low.

What it requires

  • Large Illinois data centers purchase and retire verified clean energy certificates.
  • Over time, match electricity use with clean production hour by hour, with supply from the same regional grid.
  • The IPA decides which data centers are covered and oversees compliance while limiting administrative cost.

SME feedback

HB5607, as written, would add another layer of regulatory uncertainty that could make Illinois, particularly downstate communities, less attractive to data center investment. Data center developers place a high value on regulatory clarity, and the bill's undefined requirements and potential future rulemaking create added risk at a time when Illinois already faces challenges in this area. While the proposal does not directly affect Ameren Illinois rates or impose new costs on the company, it could discourage or delay major data center projects. These facilities provide consistent load growth that helps make better use of existing electric infrastructure and spread fixed system costs, which can help keep rates more affordable for all customers. If that growth does not materialize, communities could miss out on jobs, tax base growth, and other economic benefits, while customers may experience upward pressure on rates over time. For these reasons, opposition is recommended, and the bill could be improved by offering clearer definitions and a more stable regulatory framework that supports continued investment.

Exposure for Ameren Illinois

SMEs say it does not directly change Ameren Illinois rates or impose new costs on the company. The exposure is indirect, through slower load growth.

Suggested improvements

Clearer definitions and a more stable regulatory framework that supports continued investment.

Arguments in favor

  • Moves toward clean supply without naming a single technology in the summary.
  • Regional grid requirement keeps the benefit local to the system that serves the load.
  • Uses an existing agency (the IPA) rather than a new body.

Concerns

  • SMEs see undefined requirements and future rulemaking as added regulatory risk, especially for downstate communities.
  • Developers value certainty and may look to other states.
  • Loss of load growth could mean fewer customers sharing fixed system costs.
Watch Tag says Pending but the SME recommendation reads oppose, so the tracker and the SME text disagree. HB5826 is a more detailed bill from the same sponsor on the same idea.

Cost allocation and utility protection

Bills that change who carries the cost and credit risk of serving very large loads.

DVHB4172Utility-Project Cost RecoveryTracker OpposeSME Oppose
30 second script

Representative Vella's bill would stop utilities from asking big new customers like data centers for deposits or other financial security, as long as they follow existing ICC tariffs. It would also stop utilities from slowing work because those protections are gone. Our SME concern is that it removes the financial protection that keeps other customers from carrying the risk. It is the only bill in the set that loosens protections.

Sponsor
Dave Vella (D)
Status
Referred to Rules Committee (in the House)

In plain language

HB4172 blocks electric utilities from requiring big new customers, like data centers, to put up security deposits, letters of credit, or other financial assurances as long as they apply under existing ICC-approved tariffs and stay in good standing. It also prevents utilities from slowing or deferring upgrades or interconnection work because those financial requirements are no longer allowed. The big question ahead is whether this shifts expectations around timelines, cost responsibility, or upgrade sequencing when large loads land fast, initial read leans oppose, pending SME confirmation.

What it requires

  • Utilities may not require security deposits, letters of credit or other financial assurance from large new customers that apply under existing ICC approved tariffs and stay in good standing.
  • Utilities may not slow or defer upgrades or interconnection work because those assurances are barred.

SME feedback

This bill removes all financial protections for utilities by prohibiting them from requiring deposits, letters of credit, or other financial security from large-load customers before construction begins. As written, it forces the utility, and ultimately existing customers, to absorb all upfront and credit risk if a speculative project cancels, delays, or downsizes after upgrades are underway. The potential exposure could reach tens of millions of dollars, and the measure would also strain already-backlogged interconnection queues by encouraging a surge in low-commitment applicants. For these reasons, we oppose the bill; it eliminates essential safeguards without offering any alternative protections for ratepayers.

Exposure for Ameren Illinois

Highest direct credit risk to Ameren Illinois and its customers of any bill in the set.

Suggested improvements

Keep security scaled to project size, allow tariff based requirements, and use milestone based release of security.

Arguments in favor

  • Lowers upfront cost and speeds timelines for developers.
  • Supports the argument that large load growth helps spread fixed costs.

Concerns

  • SMEs say it removes all financial protection for the utility and, in effect, existing customers if a speculative project cancels or shrinks, with exposure possibly in the tens of millions.
  • Could invite a surge of low commitment applications into an already backlogged interconnection queue.
  • Runs against the national direction, where states and regulators are adding minimum payments, exit fees and collateral.
Watch The only bill in the set that loosens protections. Every other cost bill tightens them.

Sponsor
Rachel Ventura (D)
Status
Rule 2-10 Committee Deadline Established As April 24, 2026 (in the Senate)
Hearing
Senate Executive Subcommittee on AI and Social Media , April 9, 2026 at 1:30pm and April 10, 2026 at noon

In plain language

SB3830 sets up a data-center self-direct program under the IPA Act, letting large data-center customers lower the renewable-energy procurement charges they pay if they bring new additive clean-energy generation online. It doesn't directly change interconnection rules or service obligations, but it could shape expectations around who pays what when big loads show up, especially if more developers pursue self-direct options as part of their project planning. The SME question is whether this shift in REC-cost responsibility could indirectly affect upgrade funding, timing pressures, or utility-customer negotiations during rapid large-load development.

What it requires

  • New "data center tariff" with tripled REA and BES charges, with offsets for qualifying self-supply.
  • Revenue neutral rate design filing within 90 days.
  • Water and energy reporting, plus self-direct program administration.

SME feedback

SB3830 would impose a new "data center tariff" (including tripled REA/BES charges with offsets for qualifying self-supply), require a rapid, revenue-neutral rate-design filing within 90 days, and layer significant water/energy reporting and self-direct program administration on data centers, creating conflicts with existing customer-choice frameworks and potentially implicating FERC-jurisdictional transmission cost recovery. For Ameren, this adds administrative and compliance burden, planning uncertainty, and a high likelihood of deterring data center development in AIC territory (pushing prospects to co-ops/municipals), which could blunt load growth and economic development. Financial impacts are difficult to quantify but likely include increased utility administration costs, cost-shifting risks, and reduced siting competitiveness. Recommended position: Oppose, with improvements to relocate tariff language to the proper statute (e.g., 16-108), phase and align timelines with utility planning cycles, clarify interactions with RPS/self-direct programs, ensure regulatory consistency across energy/water rules, allow flexible compliance pathways, and assess statewide competitiveness effects.

Exposure for Ameren Illinois

High administrative burden, planning uncertainty and cost shifting risk.

Suggested improvements

Relocate tariff language to the proper statute (for example 16-108), phase timelines to match utility planning, clarify the link to RPS and self-direct programs, and allow flexible compliance.

Arguments in favor

  • Aims to make data centers pay more of the cost they cause.
  • Offsets for self-supply reward bringing new generation.

Concerns

  • SMEs flag conflicts with existing customer choice frameworks and possible FERC jurisdiction over transmission cost recovery.
  • A 90 day filing deadline is compressed against utility planning cycles.
  • Could push prospects to cooperatives and municipal systems, outside Ameren Illinois territory.

Comprehensive frameworks (the POWER Act)

Broad packages covering energy, water, community and fees in one bill.

RGHB5513Hyperscale Data CentersTracker NeutralSME Neutral with concerns
30 second script

Representative Gabel's House version of the POWER Act. It sets up a full framework for hyperscale data centers covering environmental review, water permits, community benefit agreements, public reporting and annual fees tied to peak demand. Most duties fall on developers, but the volume of approvals could slow projects. The SME text matches SB4016, so confirm it was written for this bill.

Sponsor
Robyn Gabel (D)
Status
Rule 19(a) / Re-referred to Rules Committee (in the House)

In plain language

HB5513 creates a sweeping regulatory framework for hyperscale data centers, covering environmental reviews, water-use permits, community-benefit agreements, public reporting, and annual fees tied to peak demand. While most of the requirements fall on developers rather than utilities, the sheer volume of new approvals and studies could influence how quickly big loads move through planning. The key SME question is whether these added obligations might indirectly affect service expectations, interconnection timelines, or upgrade cost-responsibility when a large data-center load arrives fast.

What it requires

  • Hyperscale data centers complete environmental and community impact studies and public engagement.
  • Pay annual fees tied to peak demand into a statewide public benefit fund.
  • Option to self-generate capacity to avoid certain storage related charges.
  • Also requires counties and municipalities to adopt automated permitting for residential solar.

SME feedback

This bill would require hyperscale data centers to complete environmental and community-impact studies, participate in expanded public-engagement processes, and pay annual fees into a new statewide public-benefit fund. It also gives data centers the option to self-generate capacity to avoid certain storage-related charges, while separately requiring municipalities and counties to adopt automated permitting for residential solar. For Ameren, the concern is that the bill could make building or connecting large data centers in Illinois significantly more expensive and complex, potentially slowing or shifting projects to other states and reducing long-term load growth. A neutral-with-concerns position is recommended, with improvements focused on more flexible, outcome-based requirements and streamlined reporting to avoid discouraging investment.

Exposure for Ameren Illinois

Indirect, mainly through slower interconnection and lost load growth.

Suggested improvements

More flexible, outcome based requirements and streamlined reporting.

Arguments in favor

  • Addresses cost shifting, water transparency and community voice in one place.
  • Advocates cite polling of 68% to 70% support (a Clean Jobs Coalition poll, so an advocate source).
  • The Governor supports the direction of bring your own clean energy and closed loop water.

Concerns

  • The Data Center Coalition says the full POWER Act is 638 pages and would make building nearly impossible.
  • Higher cost and complexity could slow or move projects, reducing long term load growth.
  • The Illinois Manufacturers Association argues it hampers development and wants an all of the above approach.
Watch House companion to SB4016. Rep. Gabel says she expects it to pass in the November veto session. The SME text for this bill reads very close to SB4016, worth confirming it is bill specific.

Sponsor
Ram Villivalam (D)

In plain language

SB4016 creates a sweeping regulatory framework for hyperscale data centers, covering cumulative-impact assessments, public notice, community-benefit agreements, bans on NDAs, annual peak-demand fees, water-resource planning, quarterly water-use reporting, and a new Water Impact Permit with hearings and 5-year renewals. Most of these obligations fall on developers, but the volume of new studies, permits, and public-process steps could definitely slow how quickly big loads move from concept to interconnection. The SME question is whether these added requirements materially change service expectations, interconnection timelines, or upgrade-cost responsibility when a hyperscale load shows up fast, and whether utilities will be pulled into water-planning or peak-demand reporting dynamics.

What it requires

  • Cumulative impact assessments, public notice, community benefit agreements and a ban on NDAs.
  • Annual peak demand fees, water resource planning, quarterly water use reporting and a Water Impact Permit with hearings and 5 year renewals.
  • A flexible interconnection tariff section that addresses diesel and fossil backup generators when hyperscale loads are curtailed.

SME feedback

SB4016 addresses real environmental, water, and infrastructure concerns from hyperscale data centers but does so through prescriptive mandates that, from a utility economic development lens, risk reducing flexibility, slowing responsible projects, and undermining competitive supply choice by imposing tariffs and responsibilities on AIC for services it does not provide. The bill could affect Ameren by making Illinois less competitive for large mobile loads, adding planning/coordination/reporting complexity, and creating conflicts with existing customer-choice frameworks and even extending utility responsibility over customer backup generation. Financially, data centers could face significant new fees and compliance costs; utilities would incur new administrative costs and systems work, with potential indirect downside if projects relocate or slow. Recommended stance: neutral with targeted concerns (Whitfield) or oppose in current form (Jones), coupled with improvements to emphasize partnership over mandates, preserve competitive supply choice, clarify utility roles/cost recovery and what constitutes a "mandate," streamline/standardize reporting and fees (e.g., move to a fixed $/kW), and correct technical references (e.g., Sections citing 16-105.5).

Exposure for Ameren Illinois

Moderate administrative and systems cost. Potential rate effects from the energy efficiency cost treatment if the SME reading is right.

Suggested improvements

Emphasize partnership over mandates, preserve competitive supply choice, clarify utility roles and cost recovery, streamline reporting and fees (for example a fixed $/kW), and correct technical references such as the cited 16-105.5 sections.

Arguments in favor

  • The most complete attempt to answer public concern about water, energy and local voice.
  • Supported by the House and Senate POWER Act sponsors, the Citizens Utility Board and the Illinois Clean Jobs Coalition.
  • Flexible interconnection language is consistent with the Governor's call to let utilities prioritize residential customers in tight conditions.

Concerns

  • The Data Center Coalition says it could effectively close the Illinois market if passed as written.

Takeaways and open questions

Observations for discussion, not positions.

Say it plainly

Three points to leave people with. One, bring your own energy has broad support, so the debate is about design and not direction. Two, Ameren Illinois has little direct cost exposure in most of these bills, but the disconnection path in HB5826 and the lost load from a harder siting climate both matter to us. Three, we have gaps to close. Six bills have no SME feedback, and the tracker tags on HB5607 and HB5826 need a decision. The open questions listed here are what I would raise with the team.

Align the tracker with the SME read

HB5607 and HB5826 are both Pending but the SME text recommends oppose on both. Deciding whether to tag them Oppose, or to adopt an oppose unless amended stance, would remove the mismatch before the veto session.

Lead with cost to serve and technology neutrality

Those are the points the SMEs raise on HB5826 and they line up with the national pattern, where cost allocation through tariffs and contracts is the enacted norm and energy sourcing mandates are new. They are also easier to defend publicly than a blanket opposition, given bipartisan support for bring your own energy.

Treat the threshold and definition as the first ask

A 5MW threshold with no definition of data center is the weakest part of HB5826 and the easiest to move. Every other comparison point is at least double.

Find out where HB5826 fits

It was filed a month before the veto session by a longtime House member. Whether it is meant to ride alongside the POWER Act, replace parts of it, or serve as a counter proposal will change how to respond. That is worth asking GR contacts directly.

Close the gaps in SME coverage

Six bills have no SME feedback. SB2181 is the one to prioritize because its IPA ratepayer study could frame later cost allocation bills.

Keep HB4172 in view

It is the one bill that cuts against the tightening trend and against the ICC's own direction on ComEd, so it will draw attention if the cost bills move together.

Open questions

  • What is the exact text on HB5826 for the threshold, the penalty structure and the disconnection trigger? The introduced text was not available for this review.
  • Does the age limit and the 33% nuclear cap apply to the whole requirement or only some portion of it?
  • How would the IPA decide that a data center is not responsibly complying before asking the ICC to open a disconnection proceeding?
  • How would HB5826 interact with ComEd and Ameren large load tariffs, and with the ICC cost allocation investigation?
  • Do the Governor and labor want the final package to restart incentives, and would a restart revive SB3120?
  • Which of the 15 bills is most likely to be a vehicle in the veto session?
