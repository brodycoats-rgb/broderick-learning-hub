Thought Leadership

A director is hired for judgment. The clearest way to show judgment is to hold a few well-reasoned points of view on the issues that will define the next five years, and to be able to say each one in thirty seconds.

These are my working positions. They are personal views for discussion, not company positions.

How to form a point of view

Use this six-step frame to build any position. It is the same structure behind each idea below.

1

Insight

What do you see that others are missing?

2

Thesis

State your position in one sentence.

3

Proof

Two or three facts with sources.

4

Counter

The strongest objection, stated fairly.

5

Response

Why your view still holds.

6

Ask

What should the listener do or believe?

The one-line test. If you cannot say it as "We should move from X to Y by doing Z," it is not a point of view yet. It is a topic.

Eight big ideas

Open each one, read it, then practice the 30-second version out loud. Check it off when you can say it without notes.

Ideas you own
▸Growth that pays its own way

Data centers and other large loads are an economic opportunity Illinois should win, but the customers who cause new grid costs should carry the risk of them, not households.

Why now. Large-load requests in Illinois now exceed what utilities have ever served at peak, and regulators are writing the rules in real time.

Three points

  • Large loads bring construction jobs, tax base and long-term revenue that can spread fixed grid costs
  • Speculative requests clog queues and can strand investment if projects walk away
  • Deposits, collateral, minimum bills and transmission security agreements tie the cost to the customer who causes it

Proof

  • In March 2026 the ICC approved size-based security deposits for one utility's large-load projects after finding its pipeline was about 1.2 times its all-time peak demand (Capitol News Illinois)
  • Nearly all of PJM's 5,250 MW forecast increase for 2027/28 came from data centers (PJM)
Strongest objection. Protections will scare data centers off to other states.
Response. Serious developers already post collateral elsewhere. Clear rules make Illinois more predictable, which is what investors want. The protections fall mainly on projects that were never likely to be built.

Say it in 30 seconds

Illinois should win the data center race, but growth has to pay its own way. When a project asks for hundreds of megawatts, it should put up the security to cover the grid it needs. That protects families from paying for projects that never get built, and it gives serious developers a clear, predictable path.

▸An affordability compact

Affordability is now the frame for every energy decision, and utilities earn trust by separating what they control from what they do not, and acting on both.

Why now. PJM capacity prices cleared at the cap in back-to-back auctions, and bills became campaign issues across the region.

Three points

  • Explain the bill in two parts, supply and delivery, so members can see what drives each
  • Target relief to the households that need it most through discounts and assistance
  • Show what each investment dollar buys in reliability and local jobs

Proof

  • PJM's 2027/28 auction cleared at the $333.44 per MW-day cap, and PJM estimated it would have cleared near $530 without it (PJM)
  • The Illinois Power Agency estimated CRGA saves consumers about $13.4 billion over 20 years (Governor's office)
Strongest objection. Utilities just want to blame the grid operator for bills.
Response. Supply really is the biggest driver of recent increases, but the answer cannot stop there. The utility should own its delivery costs, show their value, and lead on assistance.

Say it in 30 seconds

Affordability is the issue that frames everything. I'd start by showing members the two halves of the bill, supply and delivery, so they see what's driving increases. Then I'd own what we control, show what each dollar buys, and make sure relief reaches the families who need it most.

▸The state's role in resource adequacy

Illinois can no longer leave resource adequacy entirely to regional markets. The state needs a planning role that keeps reliable capacity available while the clean energy transition continues.

Why now. Regional auctions are short of reliability targets, and CRGA gave the ICC new integrated resource planning authority.

Three points

  • Retirements are outpacing new supply in both PJM and MISO
  • Integrated resource planning lets the state see shortfalls before they become emergencies
  • Existing dispatchable resources can bridge the transition while storage and clean supply scale

Proof

  • PJM's 2028/29 auction cleared at the cap with cleared capacity short of the reliability requirement (Modo Energy analysis)
  • CRGA, signed January 8, 2026, created integrated resource planning at the ICC and a 3 GW storage target (Governor's office)
Strongest objection. Planning is a backdoor to keeping fossil plants open.
Response. Planning is how you retire plants safely. It sets the bridge, the timeline and the replacement, so reliability and climate goals both hold.

Say it in 30 seconds

Illinois can't leave reliability entirely to regional markets anymore. The auctions are coming up short. The new planning authority at the ICC lets us see shortfalls coming and build a bridge, keeping reliable capacity available while storage and clean supply catch up.

▸Grid plans as accountability contracts

Multi-year grid plans work best when they read like contracts with customers: what we will build, what it costs, what it delivers, and how we will be measured.

Why now. Illinois moved to performance-based grid planning under CEJA, and annual reconciliations now test spending against approved plans.

Three points

  • Plans tie investment to reliability, affordability and equity outcomes
  • Reconciliations make departures from the plan visible
  • Clear metrics give legislators something concrete to explain to constituents

Proof

  • CEJA created multi-year integrated grid plans with performance metrics for Illinois electric utilities
  • In December 2025 the ICC disallowed part of a utility's reconciliation for costs it found were not prudently incurred (ICC)
Strongest objection. Performance regulation is just paperwork that slows needed investment.
Response. It front-loads the hard conversation. A plan approved with clear metrics is easier to defend than one argued case by case.

Say it in 30 seconds

I think of a grid plan as a contract with customers. Here's what we'll build, what it costs, what you'll get, and how you can measure us. When the plan is that clear, it's easier for a legislator to explain to constituents and easier for the company to defend.

▸Transparent advocacy as a trust strategy

In a state with a long history of influence scandals, the most effective government relations strategy is radical transparency: information, follow-through and clean process.

Why now. Members and the public are skeptical of utility lobbying, and every interaction is judged against that history.

Three points

  • Relationships built on information outlast those built on favors
  • The same answer in public and in private builds credibility over time
  • A strong compliance partnership protects the team and the members

Proof

  • Illinois saw major utility-related bribery convictions in 2023 and sentencings in 2025, which reshaped expectations of utility lobbying (WTTW)
Strongest objection. Transparency means giving up leverage.
Response. The leverage that lasts is being the most trusted source of facts in the building. Members call that person first.

Say it in 30 seconds

In Illinois, trust is the scarcest asset a utility has in Springfield. My approach is simple: be the most reliable source of facts in the building, give the same answer in public and in private, and partner closely with compliance. That's what makes members call you first.

▸The energy workforce as economic development

Utilities should be treated, and should act, as one of the state's largest workforce development engines.

Why now. The grid build-out needs thousands of skilled workers, and CRGA expanded pre-apprenticeship programs and labor standards.

Three points

  • Grid investment creates careers in every district, urban and rural
  • Training partnerships with community groups, labor and colleges open those careers to more residents
  • Workforce outcomes give legislators a reason to support investment

Proof

  • CRGA expanded pre-apprenticeship programs and project labor agreements for clean energy projects (ISAC)
Strongest objection. Workforce programs are public relations, not core business.
Response. Without workers, the capital plan does not get built. Workforce is a supply chain issue.

Say it in 30 seconds

Utilities are one of the biggest workforce engines in Illinois. Every grid dollar creates careers in every district. When we train local residents for those jobs, investment becomes something legislators can champion at home.

▸Electrification at the right pace

Electrification should move at a pace the grid, customers' budgets and the gas system can support, with clear signposts rather than fixed dates.

Why now. The ICC's Future of Gas proceeding and rising electric demand are forcing hard choices about both systems.

Three points

  • The electric grid must be ready before load shifts onto it
  • Gas customers left on a shrinking system can face rising costs
  • Signposts tied to reliability and cost let policy adjust as technology matures

Proof

  • The ICC opened a Future of Gas proceeding to examine the long-term role of the gas system in Illinois (ICC docket 24-0158)
Strongest objection. Signposts are an excuse to delay.
Response. Signposts make progress measurable. They speed up when conditions are right and protect customers when they are not.

Say it in 30 seconds

Electrification is coming. The question is pace. If load moves faster than the grid can handle, or gas customers are stranded on a shrinking system, people get hurt. I'd set signposts tied to reliability and cost, so we move quickly when conditions are right and protect customers when they aren't.

▸Early warning, right-sized

Government relations teams do not need a crystal ball. They need disciplined listening: relationships, internal listening posts, national trend tracking and a statutory deadline calendar.

Why now. Thousands of bills are introduced in Illinois each session, and ideas often migrate from other states.

Three points

  • Most early intelligence comes from relationships, not analytics
  • Business units see operational problems before they become bills
  • Multi-state tracking shows which ideas are likely to arrive next

Proof

  • My own test of a formal forecasting function, using several hundred bills across six sessions, found that relationships already caught most early signals
Strongest objection. If you are not forecasting, you are reacting.
Response. The listening system is the forecast. The goal is to never be surprised, not to predict every bill.

Say it in 30 seconds

You don't need a crystal ball in Springfield. You need disciplined listening: strong relationships, regular check-ins with the business units, a watch on other states, and a calendar of statutory deadlines. I tested a bigger forecasting idea and found that system already catches what matters.

My first-year big idea

Signature initiative

A Springfield fact base

A quarterly, member-friendly data briefing on reliability, affordability, assistance enrollment and investment, broken out by legislative district. Shared openly with every member and staff, regardless of party or position on our bills.

Problem it solves

Members hear about utilities mostly through complaints and lobbying. They rarely get neutral, local facts they can use with constituents.

How it works

Pull data the company already reports, map it to districts, publish a two-page brief each quarter, and offer a short district meeting with every member.

What it builds

Credibility as the most reliable source of facts in the building, earlier warning of member concerns, and a record of transparency.

Pitch it in 30 seconds. "In my first year I'd launch a quarterly district fact base for every member. Reliability, affordability, assistance and investment in their own district, in two pages. It turns us into the most trusted source of facts in Springfield, and it gives members something useful to bring home."
