Learn: the issue in eleven short lessons

Plain-language summaries first, then the dense reference below. Try each retrieval prompt before you read.

1. The national arc: from recruitment to guardrails

Retrieve first. Before reading: in your own words, why did states move from "win the project" to "who pays"?

States first competed to recruit data centers with tax exemptions and quick approvals. As AI-scale campuses reached hundreds of MW, and sometimes gigawatts, the queue itself became a policy problem, and then the question became who pays for generation, transmission, substations and stranded assets. Water, backup-generator air emissions, noise and local benefits followed. In June 2026 FERC ordered six RTOs/ISOs to justify or reform their large-load rules, so state laws must leave federal and RTO authority intact.

Mainstream (settled enough to lead with)

  • A separate process for the largest loads
  • Evidence of site control and credible ramp schedules
  • Customer-funded studies and customer-specific facilities
  • Minimum contract terms, minimum demand payments, collateral, exit fees
  • Transparent water and energy information, with security and trade-secret protection
  • State principles implemented through full commission proceedings

Still contested (do not state as settled)

  • Whether data centers supply new generation or just pay full cost of service
  • Whether renewable requirements are annual, hourly, regional or in-state
  • Whether flexibility is voluntary and compensated or mandatory in emergencies
  • How shared transmission and generation costs are allocated
  • Whether statewide rules preempt, supplement or preserve local siting authority
  • Whether tax incentives remain justified once infrastructure and resource impacts are counted
Key takeaway"Pay your own way" is the most defensible bipartisan frame, and local legitimacy now matters as much as statewide incentives.

2. The Illinois arc

Retrieve first. Name two things Illinois did before 2026 and one thing it did in 2026.

Illinois created a Data Center Investment Program in 2019 (sales and use tax exemptions, plus a construction wage credit in underserved areas) and then saw major campuses announced well beyond the Chicago market. In 2026 lawmakers tracked 14 data-center bills, held April hearings on local impacts, energy and water, and no single bill settled the issue. On June 5, 2026 Governor Pritzker directed DCEO to pause processing new program agreements beginning July 1 and called for rate, water, clean-energy and transparency guardrails. The fall veto session is the next legislative opportunity, so stakeholder negotiation matters as much as bill monitoring.

Key takeawayIllinois has no single settled framework yet. The 2026 bills are a menu of options, and the next phase is negotiating the architecture.

3. Footprint: count, size and power

Retrieve first. Before reading: why might one state have "36", "60" and "278" data centers at the same time?

There is no single authoritative public count of Illinois data centers. Datasets define a facility, building, campus, status and capacity differently, so use ranges and name the dataset and date. Cleanview (Sept. 2026) lists 36 operating centers (1,400 MW) and 66 planned projects (9,647 MW). Aterio (Sept. 13, 2026) tracks 278 centers with an active pipeline of 16,640 MW. USDataMap lists 60 facilities and 4,123 MW. The EEI public list (May 2026) is a set of examples, not a complete inventory. See the Charts section for the comparison.

Verify before external use. One internal inconsistency to know about: the Aterio status split in the source (82 operating, 23 under construction, 159 announced) adds to 264, not the 278 centers tracked. Cite the dataset totals as reported and verify with Aterio before external use.
Key takeawayCite a range, name the dataset and its date, and prefer megawatts by status over a count of buildings.

4. Power, environmental and community impacts

Retrieve first. Which "avoid saying" statement would you be most tempted to use, and what is the safer version?

Each impact has a plain explanation, something to measure, and something not to say. Demand: measure requested, studied, contracted, energized, peak, average and ramped MW, and do not say every announced MW will operate. Grid investment: split customer-specific from shared cost and reuse value. Reliability: continuous load can improve utilization but adds risk if supply and network capability arrive late. Water: cooling design and source matter more than the label "data center". Air: backup generators cause localized emissions in testing and emergencies. Noise and land use: a single statewide setback will not solve every site. Economic benefit: permanent staffing is usually smaller than construction employment.

Key takeawayMeasure the specific impact (withdrawal, consumption, permitted hours, ramp), not the label.

5. Tax benefit and the Governor's stance

Retrieve first. Recall the three numbers that define who qualifies for the 2019 program.

To qualify, a project must invest at least $250 million over 60 months, create at least 20 new operations or maintenance jobs paid at 120% of the county average wage, and show carbon neutrality or a qualifying green-building certification. Certificates can run up to 20 years in renewable five-year increments, and underserved areas can earn a credit of 20% of eligible construction wages. Capitol News Illinois reported that 27 data centers received more than $983 million in incentives from 2020 through 2024. Present that as the reported value for that period, not the lifetime cost. The Governor's direction covers a data-center rate class, water permits, clean-energy support, limits on local NDAs, and a pause on new incentive agreements.

Key takeawayIncentives are now an accountability question, and cost allocation is an executive priority, not only an advocacy proposal.

6. The 2025-2026 bills as a policy menu

Retrieve first. Group the 14 bills into themes before you open the table. Which theme has the most?

Think of the 14 tracked bills as options, not a package: financial assurance (HB4172), property-impact compensation (HB4319), heat reuse (HB4989), the broad POWER Act (HB5513), clean-energy certificates (HB5607), "own energy" resolution (HR744), foreign-adversary ownership (SB3578), self-direct clean energy (SB3761, SB3830), a hyperscale framework (SB4016), annual reporting and an IPA study (SB2181), crypto-mining noise (SB3030), water stewardship (SB3120) and the Mahomet Aquifer (SB4004). For each, the page pairs the opportunity with the negotiation point. The full table is in the Reference section.

Key takeawaySupport the objective when the mechanism works; seek changes when a bill creates cost-shift, jurisdiction, timing, confidentiality or customer-choice problems.

7. Ameren Illinois: the thin line to walk

Retrieve first. Finish the sentence: "Ameren Illinois is principally a ___ utility in a ___ state."

Ameren Illinois is principally a delivery utility in a customer-choice state: a large customer can take delivery service from Ameren Illinois while buying supply from a competitive provider. Legislation should keep delivery infrastructure, competitive supply, regional transmission and environmental requirements distinct. The practical posture is to support the objective and ask for a workable mechanism, with the ICC deciding technical tariff terms on evidence.

Can credibly support

  • Project-readiness standards
  • Cost causation
  • Financial security
  • Transparent reporting
  • Workable local engagement
  • ICC-led tariff review

Needs modification

  • Prescriptive renewable obligations
  • One-size thresholds
  • Overlapping reporting
  • Unrealistic implementation dates
  • Broad NDA prohibitions

Should be resisted

  • Major infrastructure without enforceable payment protection
  • Arbitrary tariff multipliers
  • State direction of FERC/RTO matters
  • Utility responsibility for customer-owned backup generation
Key takeawaySupport the objective, fix the mechanism, and always offer a constructive alternative instead of simply opposing.

8. The utility path and the large-load tariff

Retrieve first. Where in the nine-stage path does the utility first take financial exposure, and what protects it?

The utility task is to turn a proposed project into a credible, financeable, operable and fairly allocated service commitment without moving speculative, stranded-cost, reliability or jurisdictional risk to existing customers. A large-load tariff is a risk-allocation framework, not simply a higher rate. Each element has a purpose and a drawback: eligibility threshold, study fees, site control and milestones, contract term, minimum billing demand, financial assurance, exit charge, capacity reassignment, expansion trigger, flexible service, reporting and cost allocation. The planning questions (load status, coincidence, distribution scope, transmission dependency, supply, construction sequence, reuse, reliability, water and portfolio effect) should be answered before a position is taken. See the stage-by-stage graphic in Charts.

Economic-development upside

  • Capital investment and construction activity
  • New large-load revenue spread over fixed costs (only with approved contract and ramp)
  • Infrastructure catalyst (identify what is reusable)
  • Regional competitiveness through a predictable process

Risk if the balance is wrong

  • Too permissive: speculative requests distort forecasts and expose customers
  • Too restrictive: credible projects choose another state first
  • Overstated benefits cost credibility
  • Risk-only messaging looks anti-investment
Key takeawayMatch utility spending to enforceable customer commitments at each stage; a proposed megawatt is not an operating megawatt.

9. BIPA and data-center expansion

Retrieve first. Is BIPA an interconnection law? What is it, then?

BIPA is not an electric-interconnection law, but it can affect physical security systems, workforce access controls and the perceived legal risk of hosting biometric information. It includes a private right of action. Illinois amended it in August 2024 (electronic signatures; repeated collections using the same method count as a single recoverable violation), and in 2026 the Seventh Circuit held the damages amendment applies retroactively to pending cases. SB3856 proposed a broad "security purpose". A credible amendment would be narrow, technology-neutral, tied to strong retention and security safeguards, and explicit that it does not allow sale, profiling or unrelated secondary use.

Key takeawayTreat BIPA as a parallel compliance and investment-climate issue, and do not claim it alone caused a project to leave.

10. State models and what the page says Illinois should take from them

Retrieve first. Which two states use a 25 MW line, and what does Ohio add to it?

Virginia (separate GS-5 class, 25 MW+, 14-year obligation), Ohio (AEP tariff above 25 MW, 85% minimum billing), Texas (SB 6 interconnection, commitment, disclosure and emergency flexibility), Georgia (100 MW, longer contracts and minimum billing), Florida (50 MW+, take-or-pay, exit fees, collateral), Wyoming (dedicated procurement), Oregon (new rate class) and Pennsylvania (threshold tests on aggregated nearby facilities). The pattern: evidence-based commission proceedings, financial assurance and ramp obligations, and general large-load rules instead of end-use discrimination.

Key takeawayThe durable venue for technical terms is a regulatory proceeding; copying another state's percentage is not evidence.

Skeptical questions: drill cards

Answer aloud first, then open the card. Structure: acknowledge, one verified fact, tradeoff, decision test. [Bracketed] items need clearance.

Are data centers raising rates?

They can create costs and they can add revenue. It depends on the infrastructure required, tariff terms, contract commitments, market effects, and whether the project actually operates as forecast. The goal is to assign costs and risks correctly before investment occurs. [Do not claim savings or increases without an approved case-specific analysis.]

Why should data centers receive tax incentives at all?

That is now an accountability question. Compare verified investment, jobs, property tax and community benefits against the exemption value and infrastructure impacts, then condition any future incentive on measurable performance. The reported figure is more than $983 million to 27 data centers from 2020 through 2024, which is the value for that period, not the lifetime cost.

Should data centers just bring their own power?

The phrase captures a real concern but is too imprecise for statute. Is the objective new energy, accredited capacity, on-site generation, storage, flexible load or full cost responsibility? Define it, then pick the mechanism.

Why not cut off data centers first in an emergency?

Emergency operations run through tariffs and reliability protocols involving utilities and regional operators. A flexible or interruptible service can be designed, but trigger, notice, compensation and performance rules must be explicit.

Is BIPA driving data centers out of Illinois?

BIPA creates compliance and litigation considerations, mainly for biometric access systems and hosted biometric information. Some stakeholders cite it as an investment concern, but location claims should be supported project by project, not assumed.

Is Ameren trying to slow data centers down?

The objective is to serve credible growth while protecting existing customers. Reasonable readiness standards and financial commitments can speed serious projects by separating them from speculative requests.

Why not let the legislature set the tariff?

The legislature should set the consumer-protection objective. The ICC can test evidence, allocate costs, review contracts and update technical terms in an open proceeding.

Will financial assurance scare projects away?

It can tie up developer capital, which is why the page favors risk-based forms, step-downs, credit standards and periodic review rather than a flat requirement. A predictable path with clear commitments is what credible projects need.

How many data centers does Illinois have?

There is no single authoritative public count. Datasets range from 60 facilities to 278 centers tracked depending on definitions. I would rather talk about megawatts by status. [Name the dataset and date if you quote a figure.]

Executive Recap

Illinois is no longer debating data centers only as economic-development prospects. The issue now sits at the intersection of affordability, reliability, water use, land use, environmental review, local control, customer choice, and regional market rules. The strategic path is to welcome credible investment while making sure the projects that create new costs and risks are the ones responsible for them.

Recommended Ameren Illinois posture: Support responsible data-center growth, but insist on enforceable project-readiness standards, customer-specific cost responsibility, risk-based financial assurance, coordinated reporting, and a full ICC process for any large-load tariff. Avoid taking a position that sounds either anti-growth or overly promotional before the technical record is clear.

  • Core message: Illinois can compete for data-center investment without asking existing customers to underwrite speculative infrastructure.

  • Policy standard: Legislation should set clear principles, while the ICC and other proper agencies should handle technical implementation through evidence-based proceedings.

  • Negotiating posture: Support the objective when the mechanism is workable; seek modifications when the bill creates cost-shift, jurisdictional, timing, confidentiality, or customer-choice problems.

  • GR discipline: Lead with verified facts, identify what is known versus assumed, and always offer a constructive alternative rather than simply opposing a proposal.

For leadership and external conversations, the simplest frame is this: Illinois should be open for credible large-load investment, but not open-ended financial exposure. The strongest Ameren Illinois position is practical, customer-focused, and institutionally disciplined.

Executive Orientation
The issue in one sentence
Data centers have shifted from a specialized economic-development topic to a statewide affordability, reliability, water, land-use, privacy, and governance issue because proposed load is arriving faster and at a greater scale than traditional utility planning, local siting, and legislative processes were designed to manage.

Illinois should move toward a large-load framework that welcomes serious projects, protects existing customers, and keeps technical decisions with the agencies best positioned to make them. For Ameren Illinois, the practical position is clear: support project-readiness standards, customer-specific cost responsibility, risk-based financial assurance, coordinated reporting, and full ICC review of any tariff design. The company should resist proposals that move speculative risk to existing customers, ignore Illinois customer choice, duplicate agency authority, or impose statutory mandates that cannot be implemented through evidence.

  • National policy is moving toward separate large-load tariffs, minimum payment obligations, financial assurance, longer contract terms, exit protections, project-readiness screening, and more transparent environmental reporting.

  • Illinois entered the debate as an aggressive incentive state, but the Governor paused new incentive agreements beginning July 1, 2026 and called for rate, water, clean-energy, and transparency guardrails.

  • Illinois does not have a single settled data-center framework. The 2026 bills instead form a policy menu covering cost recovery, reporting, water, community impacts, clean-energy procurement, heat reuse, foreign ownership, and privacy.

  • For Ameren Illinois, the defensible position is responsible growth, correct cost allocation, protection from speculative-project risk, clear agency authority, and consistency with Illinois customer choice and regional market rules.

Recruitment States competed for capital investment, construction activity, jobs, and tax base. Tax exemptions, expedited approvals, economic-development rates. The dominant question was how to win the project.
Concentration Northern Virginia and other hubs demonstrated the cumulative effect of continuous high-density load. Special rate classes, infrastructure planning, local siting scrutiny. The question became whether existing systems could absorb growth.

What has become mainstream

  • A separate process for the largest loads.

  • Evidence of site control and credible ramp schedules.

  • Customer-funded studies and customer-specific facilities.

  • Minimum contract terms, minimum demand payments, collateral, and exit fees.

  • Transparent water and energy information, with security and trade-secret protection.

  • State policy principles implemented through full commission proceedings.

What remains contested

  • Whether data centers should supply new generation or merely pay their full cost of service.

  • Whether renewable-energy requirements should be annual, hourly, regional, or in-state.

  • Whether flexibility should be voluntary and compensated or mandatory during emergencies.

  • How shared transmission and generation costs should be allocated.

  • Whether statewide rules should preempt, supplement, or preserve local siting authority.

  • Whether tax incentives remain justified once infrastructure and resource impacts are counted.

5. Illinois Tax Benefit and the Governor's Stance

Current program architecture

  • Qualifying projects must make at least $250 million in capital investment over the applicable 60-month period.

  • Projects must create at least 20 new full-time or equivalent operations or maintenance jobs, with compensation at or above 120% of the county average wage.

  • Projects must demonstrate carbon neutrality or qualifying green-building certification within the program requirements.

  • Certificates may provide exemptions for up to 20 years in renewable five-year increments.

  • Projects in qualifying underserved areas may receive a construction-employment tax credit equal to 20% of eligible construction wages.

7A. Utility Perspective: Defining a Defensible Ameren Illinois Path
Utility perspective in one sentence
The utility objective is not simply to connect large load or slow it down. It is to convert a proposed project into a credible, financeable, operable, and fairly allocated service commitment without transferring speculative, stranded-cost, reliability, or jurisdictional risk to existing customers.

A. The full utility arc, from prospect to operating customer

Stage Economic-development objective Utility and regulatory question Best-practice control
Market development Make the service territory competitive for credible investment. What system capabilities, sites, timelines, and constraints can be communicated accurately? Joint economic-development and system-planning screening with disciplined confidentiality.
Initial inquiry Help a serious prospect understand the path to service. Is the request exploratory, duplicative, or supported by a defined site and realistic load profile? Separate informal inquiry from a formal study request.
Project qualification Determine whether the project is mature enough to enter planning. Does the developer control the site, have a credible customer or financing pathway, and understand permitting? Site control, study deposit, load-ramp schedule, ownership disclosure, and milestone requirements.
System study Identify delivery facilities, regional dependencies, schedule, and cost. Which investments are customer-specific, which are reusable, and which require MISO or other approvals? Transparent study scope, customer-funded study costs, and clear allocation of responsibilities.
Commercial commitment Match utility spending to enforceable customer obligations. What happens if the project delays, downsizes, changes ownership, or exits? Financial assurance, minimum billing demand, contract term, exit charge, and capacity-reassignment provisions.
Construction Coordinate utility work with land use, permits, procurement, and customer milestones. Should utility construction continue if the customer misses a development milestone? Stage-gated notice to proceed, milestone certification, and security adjustments.
Energization and ramp Bring facilities into service without confusing reserved capacity with actual consumption. Is the customer ramping as represented, and who bears unused-capacity cost? Measured ramp reporting, minimum commitment, and periodic forecast reconciliation.

C. Large-load tariff perspective

A large-load tariff is a risk-allocation framework, not simply a higher electric rate. It should turn a proposed project into enforceable commitments that match the utility investments, reserved capacity, and system obligations created by the request.

Tariff element Purpose Benefit Potential drawback Ameren Illinois consideration
Eligibility threshold Identify loads whose scale or characteristics create unusual planning risk. Proportional treatment and administrative clarity. A single MW threshold may miss aggregated campuses or capture lower-risk industrial loads. Consider size, aggregation, voltage, load factor, expansion, and customer-specific investment.
Study and application fees Recover evaluation costs and discourage casual queue entries. Screens early speculation. Excessive fees may deter legitimate mid-sized projects. Scale fees to study complexity and credit unused amounts where appropriate.
Site control and milestones Demonstrate project maturity. Improves forecast quality and queue discipline. Rigid milestones may not reflect permitting or supply-chain delays. Allow defined extensions for documented causes while preventing indefinite placeholders.
Contract term Keep the customer committed while utility assets are recovered. Reduces stranded-cost risk. Long terms reduce customer flexibility. Match term to asset life, reuse potential, and unrecovered exposure.
Minimum billing demand Recover fixed costs when actual demand is below reserved capacity. Aligns payment with infrastructure built. A fixed percentage may overrecover or disregard ramp realities. Use an approved ramp schedule and cost evidence rather than copying another state percentage.
Financial assurance Provide collateral for construction, billing, and exit obligations. Protects customers if the project fails. Can tie up developer capital and affect competitiveness. Use risk-based forms, step-downs, credit standards, and periodic review.
Exit charge Recover prudent costs when a customer terminates early. Addresses abandonment risk. Can become punitive if it ignores avoided or reusable costs. Require mitigation and credit the customer for facilities reused for others.
Capacity reassignment Allow unused capacity to be released or transferred. Improves system utilization and mitigates loss. Operational and contractual complexity. Define utility approval, affiliate treatment, timing, and customer credits.
Expansion trigger Require review when a customer adds material load. Prevents unstudied incremental impacts. Can slow ordinary operational changes. Set a materiality threshold and distinguish temporary spikes from permanent expansion.
Flexible or interruptible service Compensate verified load reduction under defined conditions. May support reliability and reduce infrastructure need. Not every workload can curtail; performance may be uncertain. Use testing, telemetry, notice, measurement, and nonperformance rules.
Reporting and telemetry Provide accurate load and operational information. Improves planning and settlement. Cybersecurity and confidentiality concerns. Collect only necessary information and protect critical infrastructure and customer data.
Cost allocation Assign direct and shared costs using regulatory evidence. Protects other classes and supports defensible rates. Allocation disputes can be complex and time-consuming. Preserve full ICC review and distinguish delivery, supply, and transmission jurisdiction.

F. Best-practice positions Ameren Illinois could lead on

1. One verified load vocabulary: Require consistent reporting of inquiry, studied, contracted, energized, and operating MW. This would improve legislative, regulatory, and public understanding.

2. Project-readiness before major utility commitment: Support site control, paid studies, credible development milestones, and a realistic load ramp before material construction exposure begins.

3. Risk-based financial assurance: Lead on a transparent methodology that considers creditworthiness, project stage, customer-specific investment, asset reuse, and step-downs as risk declines.

4. Full ICC process for large-load tariffs: Support statutory customer-protection principles while preserving evidence, stakeholder participation, cost-of-service review, and updating authority at the Commission.

5. Technology-neutral large-load treatment: Where the risk is comparable, consider whether rules should apply to large loads generally rather than only to data centers. Preserve separate provisions only where an end-use characteristic creates a documented difference.

6. Coordinated state reporting: Advocate one reporting architecture with clear agency ownership, consistent definitions, and protections for trade secrets, customer information, and critical infrastructure.

7. Source-specific water policy: Support measurement and planning while distinguishing withdrawal from consumption and potable, reclaimed, surface, and groundwater sources.

8. Contracted flexibility, not rhetorical curtailment: Support voluntary or negotiated flexible-load products with explicit triggers, verification, compensation, and consequences.

9. Reuse and mitigation credits: Ensure exit obligations reflect actual unrecovered cost after reasonable mitigation and credit for facilities repurposed for other customers.

10. Regional and federal savings clauses: Make clear that state requirements do not override FERC, MISO, NERC, transmission-owner, or other applicable authority.

11. Predictable paths for mid-sized and hyperscale projects: Use graduated obligations so requirements rise with scale, infrastructure exposure, clustering, and environmental impact.

12. Public-benefit evidence: Support transparent reporting of investment, construction labor, permanent jobs, infrastructure payments, taxes, incentive value, and realized community commitments, with each measure reported on the correct timeline.

G. Recommended decision framework for the correct path

Test Proceed / support when Modify or oppose when
Customer protection The proposal assigns direct costs and secures material utility exposure. Existing customers could fund speculative, abandoned, or customer-specific facilities.
Legal authority The proper entity acts within state, federal, local, and RTO boundaries. A state bill directs transmission, supply, or operations outside the responsible entity’s authority.
Evidence The threshold and obligation are tied to documented risk or impact. The proposal relies on a label, headline MW, or unsupported assumption.
Proportionality Requirements scale with size, clustering, infrastructure, water, and project maturity. A small or lower-risk facility receives the same burden as a gigawatt campus.
Implementability Definitions, timeline, rulemaking, confidentiality, enforcement, and transition are workable. The deadline assumes an ICC or permitting process can be completed without evidence or due process.
Economic-development competitiveness Credible projects receive a predictable path and know the required commitments. Rules are open-ended, duplicative, or materially less predictable than peer states without added customer value.
Environmental integrity The rule measures actual withdrawal, consumption, emissions, or local impact. The rule assumes every design and water source has the same effect.
Durability The framework can be updated through Commission or agency processes as technology changes. The statute hard-codes a technology, percentage, or operating practice likely to become outdated.
Public credibility The company can explain the position as protecting customers while enabling responsible investment. The position appears designed only to maximize load or avoid accountability.

8. The BIPA Issue and Data Center Expansion
Bottom line
BIPA is not an electric-interconnection law, but it can affect physical security systems, workforce access controls, and the perceived legal risk of hosting or processing biometric information. Treat it as a parallel investment-climate and compliance issue, not as proof that a specific project will leave Illinois.

What is verified

  • BIPA regulates collection, use, storage, disclosure, and retention of biometric identifiers and information and includes a private right of action.

  • Illinois amended BIPA in August 2024 to allow electronic signatures and limit repeated collections or disclosures of the same identifier using the same method to a single recoverable violation.

  • In 2026, the Seventh Circuit held that the 2024 damages amendment applies retroactively to pending cases.

  • SB3856 proposed defining a broad "security purpose" and waiving certain collection requirements in specified security circumstances, including access control and protection of property and people.

Sources: S15, S16, I4

Why data centers care

Use case Potential issue Defensible path
Employee or contractor facility access Fingerprint, face, hand, or iris systems may trigger notice, consent, retention, and security obligations. Maintain consent, retention/deletion, vendor-control, and security practices; evaluate a narrow security-purpose clarification.
Tenant-controlled systems in colocation facilities Operator, tenant, and vendor roles may be unclear. Define controller/processor responsibilities contractually and avoid assuming the landlord is outside the chain.
Hosting biometric datasets Physical host, cloud provider, customer, and downstream processor may have different factual roles. Avoid categorical exemptions; clarify possession, control, processing, and security-purpose boundaries.
Investment perception Private litigation risk can be cited as a comparative siting disadvantage. Use verified legal changes and specific compliance burdens, not unsupported claims that BIPA alone caused lost projects.

GR negotiating frame on BIPA

Protect the original privacy purpose while clarifying legitimate security use. A credible amendment would be narrow, technology-neutral, conditioned on strong retention and security safeguards, and explicit that security-purpose treatment does not authorize sale, profiling, or unrelated secondary use.

10. What Mainstream Discussion Often Misses

The denominator problem: Project counts are political shorthand, but MW by status and time horizon are more useful. Requested load is not operating load.

The campus-versus-building problem: One campus may contain many buildings. Counts can rise without representing distinct grid interconnections.

The speculative queue problem: A large request can change planning assumptions before a project has land, financing, tenants, permits, or a realistic ramp.

The reuse-value problem: Not every canceled-project asset is stranded. Some substations or lines may serve future customers; tariff design should credit reuse.

The electric-supply versus delivery problem: Illinois customer choice makes bundled-utility language especially risky.

The regional displacement problem: A project moving across a state line may still affect the same RTO market and regional prices.

The firmness problem: Nameplate MW, contract MW, peak MW, average MW, and firm MW are not interchangeable.

The water-source problem: Potable water, reclaimed water, groundwater, and surface water have different public impacts.

The backup-generation problem: Installed backup MW does not necessarily equal permitted, fueled, synchronized, or dispatchable grid support.

The tax-benefit timing problem: Upfront exemptions, construction activity, operating jobs, property taxes, and infrastructure costs occur on different timelines.

The cybersecurity and national-security problem: Large campuses are critical digital and energy infrastructure; transparency rules need security safeguards.

The workforce sequencing problem: Construction labor demand can be significant, but permanent staffing and local workforce pipelines should be analyzed separately.

The BIPA boundary problem: Physical access, hosted data, cloud processing, and tenant systems involve different legal facts.

The governance problem: Multiple agencies collecting similar water or energy information can create burden without producing a coherent public record.

The exit-ramp problem: Good legislation needs transition rules, cure periods, variances, grandfathering standards, and a process for projects already in development.

12. Storyline and Scripts

A. Universal storyline: bullet version

  • Illinois spent several years recruiting data centers as major capital investments.

  • AI accelerated proposed projects from tens of megawatts to hundreds of megawatts and, in some cases, campus-scale gigawatt requests.

  • That growth exposed questions the original incentive structure did not answer: who pays, how projects prove they are real, how grid reliability is protected, how water and local impacts are managed, and what happens if a project never materializes.

  • The 2026 General Assembly considered a broad set of bills, but no consensus framework passed in the spring.

  • Governor Pritzker then paused new incentive agreements beginning July 1, 2026 and called for comprehensive guardrails.

  • The credible policy path is not a blank check and not a blanket ban. It is project readiness, full and fair cost responsibility, financial assurance, transparent environmental information, local engagement, and proper agency jurisdiction.

  • Ameren Illinois must support responsible growth while protecting customers and avoiding commitments outside its delivery role or beyond state authority.

  • The next phase is negotiation over the architecture of an Illinois large-load framework.

B. Executive team script

“The data center issue has moved well beyond economic development. Illinois originally built its policy around attracting investment, but AI-driven projects are now arriving at a scale that raises separate questions about grid infrastructure, supply, water, local impacts, and customer risk. The spring session showed that legislators agree there is a problem to address, but they do not yet agree on the mechanism. We saw bills on financial assurances, reporting, clean energy, aquifer protection, community agreements, heat reuse, and even foreign ownership. After no comprehensive bill passed, the Governor paused new incentive agreements beginning July 1 and called for a broader framework.

Our position needs to stay disciplined. We should support responsible data center growth and agree that existing customers should not subsidize speculative or customer-specific investments. We should support project-readiness standards, reasonable financial security, transparent information, and a full ICC process for any large-load tariff. At the same time, we need to prevent legislation from treating Ameren Illinois as a bundled generation utility, assigning us responsibility for customer-owned equipment, or directing outcomes that belong to FERC, MISO, or competitive suppliers.

The strategic opportunity is to move from reacting to individual bills toward shaping a coherent framework. The most defensible model is simple: credible projects are welcome, projects pay the costs and risks they create, environmental obligations are tied to actual impact, and technical terms are implemented by the proper regulator. That approach lets us protect customers without appearing opposed to economic growth.”

C. Legislator / sponsor script

“I agree with the concern you are trying to address. Existing customers should not be left paying for a large project that is delayed, downsized, or canceled. The question is how we write that protection so it actually works in Illinois. Our utilities deliver power, many large customers buy supply competitively, and transmission is governed through regional and federal structures. I would suggest the statute establish the customer-protection standard, then direct the ICC to determine the tariff, financial assurance, minimum commitment, and exit terms through a full proceeding. That gets to your objective without setting an arbitrary statewide number or creating a conflict with customer choice. We can also pair that with water and energy reporting through one coordinated system, so lawmakers and communities have reliable information.”

D. News outlet script

“The simplest way to understand the debate is that Illinois recruited data centers under one set of assumptions, and the scale of proposed AI growth changed the conversation. These facilities can bring major investment, construction work, and local tax revenue. They can also require substantial electric and water infrastructure. The public-policy question is not whether every data center is good or bad. It is whether a project is credible, who pays for what it requires, how communities understand the impacts, and what happens if the promised load or benefits never arrive. A workable Illinois policy would protect existing customers, require transparent project commitments, and still provide a predictable path for responsible investment.”

E. Community / local official script

“A local government should not have to choose between economic development and basic information. Before a large campus is approved, the community should understand the construction schedule, road and water needs, generator testing, noise mitigation, public-safety requirements, and expected tax benefits. The state can create a consistent minimum process, but the agreement should reflect the actual site. A project in an established industrial area should not be treated exactly like one next to homes or dependent on a constrained groundwater source.”

F. Difficult-question responses

Question Response
Are data centers raising rates? They can create costs and they can add revenue. The answer depends on the infrastructure required, tariff terms, contract commitments, market effects, and whether the project actually operates as forecast. The policy goal is to assign costs and risks correctly before investment occurs.
Why should data centers receive tax incentives? That is now an accountability question. Illinois should compare verified investment, jobs, property tax, and community benefits against the exemption value and infrastructure impacts, then condition any future incentive on measurable performance.
Should data centers bring their own power? The phrase captures a legitimate concern but is too imprecise for statute. We need to define whether the objective is new energy, accredited capacity, on-site generation, storage, flexible load, or full cost responsibility.
Why not curtail data centers first in an emergency? Emergency operations are controlled through tariffs and reliability protocols involving utilities and regional operators. A flexible or interruptible service option can be designed, but the trigger, notice, compensation, and performance rules must be explicit.
Is BIPA driving data centers out of Illinois? BIPA creates compliance and litigation considerations, especially for biometric access systems and hosted biometric information. Some stakeholders cite it as an investment concern, but project-location claims should be supported project by project rather than assumed.
Is Ameren trying to slow data centers? The objective is to serve credible growth while protecting existing customers. Reasonable readiness standards and financial commitments can speed serious projects by separating them from speculative requests.
Why not let the legislature set the tariff? The legislature should set the consumer-protection objective. The ICC is better positioned to test evidence, allocate costs, review contracts, and update technical terms through an open proceeding.

14. Bill Review Checklist

  • Definition: What facility, threshold, technology, and project status are covered?

  • Authority: Which entity controls the decision today?

  • Problem statement: What documented harm is the bill solving?

  • Cost: Which cost, caused by whom, incurred when, and recoverable how?

  • Customer choice: Does the bill distinguish delivery from supply?

  • Federal/regional boundary: Does it preserve FERC, MISO, PJM, and NERC authority?

  • Project readiness: Are site control, financing, tenants, permits, and ramp verified?

  • Risk protection: Are deposits, collateral, minimum commitments, and exit terms available?

  • Environmental specificity: Are water source, consumption, emissions, noise, and location treated accurately?

  • Confidentiality: Are trade secrets, customer data, and critical infrastructure protected?

  • Implementation: Are timeline, rulemaking, transition, cure, variance, appeal, and enforcement workable?

  • Duplication: Does another agency or bill already collect the same information?

  • Equity: Are benefits and burdens measurable and geographically understood?

  • Alternative: What amendment preserves the sponsor’s objective with less risk?

  • Position status: Is this official SME feedback, preliminary GR analysis, or a leadership-approved position?

Appendix A. One-Minute Issue Summary

Illinois is trying to update a data-center recruitment policy for a new era of AI-scale demand. The original approach emphasized incentives and investment. The new debate is about whether the grid, water systems, communities, and existing customers are protected when projects arrive at hundreds of megawatts or more. In 2026, legislators introduced separate proposals on rates, financial security, water, reporting, clean energy, community impacts, heat reuse, foreign ownership, and privacy. No comprehensive framework passed in the spring, and Governor Pritzker paused new incentive agreements beginning July 1 while calling for reform. The best path is a balanced large-load framework: credible project milestones, full cost responsibility, financial assurance, transparent environmental data, local engagement, and clear jurisdiction. Ameren Illinois can support those goals while insisting that legislation respect Illinois customer choice, ICC rate-setting, and federal and regional authority.

Appendix B. Five Principles to Repeat

  1. Existing customers should not finance speculative projects.

  2. The rules should apply to measurable load and risk, not merely a business label.

  3. The legislature should set principles; expert agencies should implement technical standards.

  4. Transparency should be meaningful while protecting security and legitimate confidential information.

  5. Responsible economic development requires both a path to build and enforceable accountability.
