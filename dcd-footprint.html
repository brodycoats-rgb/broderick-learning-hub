Water

Water use depends far more on the cooling design than on the size of the building. A campus that evaporates water to cool can use a small town's supply. A closed loop campus can use less than a car wash.

Why the range is so wide

  • Evaporative cooling uses the most water and the least electricity. It is the most common design for large sites.
  • Closed loop and direct to chip liquid cooling use little water after the first fill, but more electricity. Joliet's utility director said a closed loop is drained and refilled about every 10 to 15 years.
  • Power plant water is about 12 times the direct cooling use nationally, so the grid's fuel mix matters as much as the building.
  • Where the water comes from matters most. Lake Michigan allocations, river water, and treated wastewater are different from a slow recharging aquifer like the Mahomet in central Illinois, which can take centuries to recharge.
Say it plainlyNationally, data centers use a small share of water, far less than golf courses. Locally, one campus can be a big new user, so the questions are which cooling design, which water source, and who reports the numbers.

Land, by size

There is no single acres per megawatt number. Land depends on how tall the buildings are and how much room the substation, generators, cooling yards, setbacks, and stormwater need. Campuses are getting bigger because AI loads need transmission level power and room to grow.

Rules of thumb, with care

  • Developers often plan about 0.5 to 1 acre per megawatt. Land constrained markets go denser by building up.
  • Buildings cover only part of a campus. Meta's DeKalb site has 2.3 million square feet of buildings, about 53 acres, on 505 acres of land.
  • Land that sells for about $10,000 an acre can be worth six figures an acre once a power path is secured.
  • Joliet's 795 acre campus is about 600 football fields, or a bit more than two average Illinois farms.

TechTarget, Cleanview, Baxtel, WGLT, Capitol News Illinois. The 53 acre and 600 field figures are simple conversions.

Say it plainlyA small data center fits on a lot. A hyperscale campus is the size of a large farm, and the newest AI campuses are the size of Central Park. Most of that land is substation, cooling, roads, and buffer, not computer rooms.

Taxes, both directions

States usually give up sales tax on equipment. Local governments usually collect property tax on the buildings and gear. Whether the trade is worth it depends on how big each side is and whether local abatements were given.

What a data center can receive in Illinois

  • Exemption from state and local sales and use taxes on qualifying equipment and construction materials.
  • Certificates last up to 20 years, issued in five year terms.
  • A 20 percent credit on construction wages for projects in underserved areas.
  • To qualify, at least $250 million of investment, at least 20 jobs at 120 percent of the county wage (the statute says average, DCEO materials say median), and a green building or carbon neutral certification within two years.
  • New agreements have been paused since July 1, 2026. Existing agreements are honored.

20 ILCS 605/605-1025, DCEO, Capitol News Illinois.

Jobs and the national picture

  • The same Illinois study projects about $57 billion of new investment through 2035, with 2,800 direct permanent jobs, about $20 million of investment per permanent job. It also counts about 41,751 jobs from consumer spending and thousands of construction jobs paying over $100,000.
  • About three dozen states offer sales tax breaks for data centers.
  • Good Jobs First found states lose more than $3 billion a year on these breaks. Illinois is one of 10 states giving up more than $100 million a year each.
  • Virginia gave up $1.6 billion in fiscal 2025, up 118 percent in one year. Georgia's cost rose to $625 million from $10 million in 2020.
Say it plainlyThe state gives up sales tax and the town gains property tax. Few permanent jobs come per dollar invested, but the local tax base can grow a lot if the town does not also give the taxes back.

Your day runs through a data center

Most people never see a data center, but almost everything on a phone, a card, or a smart device passes through one. Here is a typical day for an adult in the United States.

Wake up

Phone alarm syncs, weather app updates, overnight texts and email arrive, and the smart thermostat checks in.

Commute

Maps and traffic, music or podcast streaming, ride share or transit apps, toll and parking payments.

Coffee

A card tap or phone payment is approved in seconds by a card network's data centers.

Work

Email, shared documents, video calls, payroll, customer systems, and now AI assistants.

Lunch

Food delivery or a restaurant app, a mobile order, a bank transfer to split the bill.

Errands

Online shopping, package tracking, pharmacy refills, a doctor's patient portal and electronic records.

Home

Video streaming, social media, online games, a video call with family, smart doorbell clips saved to the cloud.

Behind the scenes

Photo backups, the electric smart meter, school and work logins, credit checks, weather forecasts, and 911 dispatch systems.
