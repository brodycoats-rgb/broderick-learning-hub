The Director's Playbook

Running government relations for a large organization is a different job from being its best lobbyist. The director owns the agenda, the team, the executive relationship and the company's reputation in the Capitol.

This page lays out what great looks like across eight skill sets, how the work runs through the year, how to lead the team, and where I stand today with my next step for each. Rate yourself as you go. Your ratings save in this browser.

The test a hiring panel applies is simple. Will this person make the whole team better, or just be busy? Every section below is built to answer that question.

The job at scale

Many audiences

The General Assembly, the Governor's office, the state utility commission, city councils and county boards, federal affairs colleagues, trade associations, labor and advocates. A director keeps the message consistent across all of them.

Many companies

Large utilities sit inside holding companies with sister utilities in other states. Positions in one state echo in others, and the parent company's priorities shape the agenda.

Many politics

A statewide footprint means urban, suburban and rural members with very different concerns. Chicago, the collar counties and downstate each need their own approach.

Eight skill sets

Open each one for what great looks like, the behaviors a panel listens for, the common failure, and where I stand.

▸Political strategy·

What great looks like. Reads the chamber, the calendar and the leaders, and picks the few fights that matter.

Behaviors a panel listens for

  • Knows the vote count before anyone asks
  • Sequences the work: who to see first, when to file, when to wait
  • Spends political capital deliberately and keeps a reserve
  • Thinks two sessions ahead, not one bill ahead

Common failure

Fighting every bill with the same intensity, so nothing gets the attention it needs.

Where I stand. Eighteen years in Springfield across ten General Assemblies and four governors. I help head off harmful bills by working with industry peers, meeting chief sponsors and rallying the industry.
My next rep. Write a one-page session strategy each January: three priorities, two defensive watches, one long-game investment.

▸Policy and regulatory fluency·

What great looks like. Understands the substance well enough to translate it, and knows where the legislature ends and the regulator begins.

Behaviors a panel listens for

  • Explains capacity markets, rate design or a grid plan in two minutes
  • Keeps legislative work consistent with the regulatory record
  • Knows which issues belong to FERC, the RTO, the ICC or the General Assembly
  • Spots the clause in an amendment that changes cost recovery

Common failure

Mastering process but not substance, or the reverse.

Where I stand. Core focus on the General Assembly, the ICC and MISO and PJM capacity and reliability. I follow EEI, AGA and EIA data and worked EIMA, FEJA, CEJA and CRGA.
My next rep. Keep a running list of the five technical topics members ask about most, with a two-minute plain-language answer for each.

▸Coalition building·

What great looks like. Finds the overlap among groups that rarely agree, and holds it together under pressure.

Behaviors a panel listens for

  • Writes down every partner's must-haves early
  • Brings labor, business, advocates and peers to the same table
  • Spots defection risk before it happens
  • Shares credit generously

Common failure

Treating coalitions as lists of logos rather than relationships that need tending.

Where I stand. I work with industry peers on overlapping issues and coordinate cross-functional teams on data center, battery storage and decarbonization bills.
My next rep. Map each priority bill's coalition on one page: allies, swing groups, opponents, and what each needs.

▸Advising executives·

What great looks like. Gives leaders the answer, the risk and the options in the time they have.

Behaviors a panel listens for

  • Leads with the conclusion, then the evidence
  • Delivers bad news early with options
  • Preps executives for testimony and member meetings
  • Knows when to say 'not yet' to a leader's idea

Common failure

Long memos that bury the answer, or surprises that reach executives through the press.

Where I stand. I lead or support report-outs, strategic calls, end-of-session reports and executive briefings, and I built briefing materials for my company's president.
My next rep. Cut every executive brief to one page with the answer in the first line.

▸Building and developing a team·

What great looks like. Sets clear coverage, hires for judgment, and grows people through real reps in front of members.

Behaviors a panel listens for

  • Designs coverage by caucus, committee and issue
  • Delegates whole outcomes, not tasks
  • Gives one piece of specific feedback at a time
  • Builds a bench so the team survives turnover

Common failure

Staying the team's best lobbyist instead of becoming its leader.

Where I stand. Seven years leading a team of seven as a program director, managing a board, outside counsel and accountants, then four years as a senior HR consultant advising leaders on realignment, discipline, terminations and succession. Today I lead cross-functional groups without formal authority.
My next rep. For each person, name the one stretch assignment that would grow them this session.

▸Contract lobbyists and budget·

What great looks like. Runs the outside team like a portfolio with assignments, reporting and accountability.

Behaviors a panel listens for

  • Written assignments and contact reports for every contract lobbyist
  • Reviews value against cost each year
  • Keeps registration and disclosure clean
  • Knows the budget line by line

Common failure

Paying for relationships nobody can describe, which is both wasteful and risky.

Where I stand. I work with and at times direct contract lobbyists and inside counsel on my issues.
My next rep. Draft a one-page contract lobbyist scorecard: coverage, reporting, results, compliance.

▸Compliance and political programs·

What great looks like. Treats ethics as a process every person on the team can follow, not a judgment call.

Behaviors a panel listens for

  • Partners with compliance before, not after
  • Separates official business from political activity
  • Knows gift, contribution and disclosure rules cold
  • Makes it easy for the team to ask

Common failure

Assuming good intentions are enough.

Where I stand. I have coordinated more than 30 political fundraising events since 2017 within ethics rules and know my company's PAC bylaws and governance.
My next rep. Write the team's five-rule political activity checklist and review it with compliance.
1 Learning3 Solid5 Could teach it
▸Crisis communication·

What great looks like. Gets accurate information to elected officials before their constituents call them.

Behaviors a panel listens for

  • Pre-storm briefings, then a fixed update cadence
  • One inbox for constituent cases with fast turnaround
  • Owns mistakes plainly and follows up with proof
  • Coordinates every message with operations and communications

Common failure

Going quiet when the news is bad.

Where I stand. Like everyone in GR, I work with my company's enterprise storm response from before a storm hits through restoration, keeping affected legislators informed.
My next rep. Build a storm update template that a teammate could send without you.

A session week

Monday

Team huddle. Bills that moved, positions needed, who covers what.

Tuesday to Thursday

Springfield. Committees, member meetings, contract lobbyist check-ins, late language review.

Thursday

Executive brief. Fifteen minutes on what changed and what is needed from leadership.

Friday

One-page update. What changed, what we need, what is coming.

Leading the team

Coverage design

Assign each person caucuses, committees and issues so every member and every priority has an owner. Overlap on the top five relationships so no single departure leaves a gap.

Hiring

Hire for judgment, discretion and curiosity. Relationships can be built. Integrity and judgment cannot be taught on the job.

Developing lobbyists

Real reps in front of members, a debrief after each, and one piece of feedback at a time. Let people lead meetings before they feel ready.

Delegation

Hand off whole outcomes with a clear finish line. Keep for yourself only what requires the director: executive counsel, the hardest relationships and final calls.

Contract lobbyists

Written assignments, contact reports after every meeting, a Monday check-in during session and an annual value review. Clean registration and disclosure always.

Culture

Bad news travels fast and is never punished. Credit goes to the team. Relationships are built on information and follow-through, never favors.

Reporting value

Five measures a director can bring to leadership after every session.

Outcomes

Bills passed, amended or stopped, and what each was worth to customers and the company

Access

How quickly the team can reach key members and staff, and whether they call us before they act

Speed and quality

Time from bill filing to approved position, vote count accuracy, executive ratings of briefings

Compliance

Clean registrations and disclosures, zero findings, training completion

Team health

Coverage gaps, development plans in place, retention

The first 100 days

Days 1 to 30

Listen

  • Meet every team member one on one: what works, what does not, what they want to grow into
  • Meet the executive team, regulatory, legal, compliance and communications
  • Meet contract lobbyists and review every agreement
  • Meet energy committee chairs, minority spokespeople and key staff
  • Read the last two end-of-session reports and every open regulatory docket
Days 31 to 60

Align

  • Agree on session priorities and success measures with the senior vice president
  • Set team coverage, decision rights and the weekly rhythm
  • Launch the weekly one-page executive update
  • Pick one early win that shows the team is working differently
Days 61 to 100

Deliver

  • Run the pre-filing plan and sponsor outreach
  • Brief executives on the session outlook with a vote count
  • Hold first development conversations with each team member
  • Report the first measurable results to leadership
