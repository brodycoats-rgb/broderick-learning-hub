Markets, plans and big customers

Two lessons about who decides and who pays. How Illinois's two regional markets differ, how three similar sounding plans fit together, and how to protect customers from the cost of very large new loads and confusing supplier bills.

What this part is for

Parts 1 and 2 were about physics and supply. Part 3 is about structure and money. Most legislative conversations get stuck here, because the same words (plan, market, rate) mean different things to different people. Learn which plan answers which question, and who carries which risk, and you can keep a conversation on track.

8

Markets, plans and who decides

You will be able to explain why Illinois sits in two markets, why Illinois and Missouri differ, the argument for and against price caps, and which of three plans answers which question.

In plain words

Illinois is split between two regional grid operators. Missouri's utility builds and owns its generation. Ameren Illinois mostly delivers power that others make. That difference changes who takes the risk, who pays, and who builds. And on top of that, three different plans each answer a different question. Mixing them up is the fastest way to lose a legislator.

Part A, Two markets and two business models

How to read itThe state is drawn as two zones, not to scale. Tap MISO or PJM to see which part of Illinois it covers and what it does.
What it showsThe line between the two operators is a market boundary, not a county line you can see. Each side has its own market and capacity rules.
So whatA statewide Illinois policy lands differently north and south. When someone asks about "the Illinois market," ask which one.

A state study is looking at regional market arrangements. The presenter cautioned that moving Ameren Illinois from MISO to PJM would not be a simple change. He said transmission obligations would remain and costs would be very large, and that state law cannot override federal interconnection rules. Those are company views to label as such.

The investment timing problem

A power plant takes five to ten years to plan and build. Capacity auctions look one year ahead. That mismatch makes long lived investment hard to justify. The presenter argued that price caps hide the scarcity signal that would tell developers to build. The counterargument is real too. Prices with no ceiling can expose customers to extreme costs without guaranteeing a plant arrives in time. Good design has to protect investment and customers together. Auction figures cited, $332 per MW day and a hypothetical $750 to $777 uncapped, should be tied to the exact auction before use.

Model 8.3

The time gap

How to read itThe orange bar is the time to build. The teal bar is how far ahead a capacity auction looks, one year. The gap between them is the uncertainty a developer carries.
What it showsA plant has to be committed years before the auction that would pay for it. A one year price signal is a thin basis for that bet.
So whatThis is the root of the price cap debate. Both sides are trying to solve a timing mismatch, and they disagree about who should carry the risk.

A teaching model. Use the cited $332 per MW day as the starting price and $777 as the hypothetical uncapped price. The purchase is an imaginary 1,000 MW.

What it showsThere is no free lunch. A stronger signal to build costs customers more in the short term, and a lower ceiling protects customers while weakening the signal.

Part B, Three plans that get confused

Which plan answers it?

How to read itPick a plan for each question. The explanation shows why.
What it showsThe three plans are separate decisions in separate proceedings with separate timelines.
So whatApproval of a plan is not the same as approval of the rates to pay for it. Keep the three questions apart in every conversation.

The 2028 to 2031 Grid Plan was described as awaiting a final Commerce Commission decision in December 2026. Labor groups and the Clean Jobs Coalition gave testimony in support. That matters because grid modernization can unite groups that disagree on other energy topics. Reliable wires serve current customers and make room for more renewable energy.

Evidence beats assertion

The storm hardening dispute is a useful case study. The judge's proposed order did not support all the requested proactive hardening. A proposed order is not a final order. The way to make the case is with measures people can check.

MeasureWhat it shows
Outage frequency and durationWhether customers actually have fewer and shorter outages (SAIFI and SAIDI from Part 1)
Storm exposureHow much of the system sits where storms hit hardest
Avoided repair costWhat a failure would cost compared with planned replacement
Restoration timeHow quickly customers get power back
Effects on vulnerable and critical customersWho is protected, such as hospitals and households that depend on power

The reliability and affordability trap

One more lesson came from the president's remarks. If you are very reliable, people ask why bills are still high. If you have many outages, they complain less about price. Reliability and affordability pull against each other in the public mind, so you have to talk about both at once.

Bills feel highBills feel fair
Service is very reliable"Why am I paying so much for something that works?" Customers notice price because nothing else is wrong.The goal, and the hardest to keep.
Service has frequent outagesAnger lands on both, and trust falls fast.People complain about outages and talk less about price.

Common mix-ups

  • A utility being a wires company is not a weakness or a strength by itself. It is a different job.
  • Capping prices protects customers short term and may weaken the signal to build. Removing caps does the reverse.
  • The Grid Plan is not the IRP. One is wires, the other is supply.
  • Approval of a plan is not the same as approval of rates to pay for it.

Check before using outside

The auction figures, and the Missouri build plan, since both 6 GW and 9 GW were mentioned. Reconcile capacity, project types and time horizons. Also the final decision timing. Describe the order as proposed until the Commission rules.

Say it

Full version, about 45 seconds

Illinois is split between two regional grid operators, and Missouri works differently from us. There, the utility builds the power plants and the cost is spread through regulated rates. In central and southern Illinois, we deliver power that other companies make, and customers feel the wholesale market more directly. Plants take years to build, but the market looks one year ahead. Any fix has to give builders a reason to invest and protect customers from runaway prices at the same time.

Plans version, about 45 seconds

There are three different plans, and each answers a different question. The resource plan asks what power the state needs. The grid plan asks what wires and equipment we have to build to deliver it. The rate plan asks how that gets paid for. Our grid plan covers 2028 to 2031, and labor and clean jobs groups testified in support of it, because a modern grid serves today's customers and makes room for tomorrow's renewable energy.

Hallway version, about 30 seconds

Three plans, three questions. What power do we need, that is the resource plan. What wires do we build, that is the grid plan. How do we pay for it, that is the rate plan. Keep them apart and the conversation gets a lot easier.
Legislator asksHow do we know the spending is justified?
You sayBy comparing the cost of planned replacement with what a failure would cost in outages, emergency repair and safety. We should show measurable results, like fewer and shorter outages.
Legislator asksWhy can't Illinois just do what Missouri does?
You sayMissouri's utility builds and owns generation and spreads the cost through regulated rates. Here the market and state procurement do much of that. Neither is risk free. They put the decisions and the risks in different places.

Data centers, large loads and the one bill problem

You will be able to explain cost causation, the greater of financial security formula, and why a letter of credit is not a payment. You will also be able to read a bill as supply plus delivery, explain purchase of receivables, and describe the reform options and their tradeoffs.

In plain words

Two cost fairness questions share this lesson. First, a data center can ask for hundreds of megawatts. Serving it may need expensive lines built years before it opens. If the project is cancelled, someone is left holding the cost. The tariff aims to make sure it is the developer, not everyone else's bills. Second, many customers get their power supply from a third party but still get a bill with Ameren's name on it. If the supply price surprises them, they call Ameren. The question is who should bill for, and carry the risk of, the supplier's charges.

Part A, Data centers and the large load tariff

What the proposal does, step by step

1 ApplySize, place, customer, schedule
2 Readiness reviewSite control, end user, power plan
3 StudyWhat upgrades are needed
4 SecurityA guarantee tied to the cost
5 Revenue protectionExtra charges if revenue falls short
  • The proposal in the suspended docket used a 100 MW large load threshold, with a possible drop to 50 MW if a MISO order in November requires it.
  • Study deposits were proposed at $20,000 for smaller applicants, up from $10,000, and $200,000 for loads of 100 MW or more.
  • Projects would join a tiered queue based on site control, end user identity and energy supply plans. Existing industrial customers keep priority over speculative requests.
  • Financial security would be the greater of two amounts. One is the 20 year revenue requirement of the needed transmission facilities. The other is 80 percent of the expected ten year transmission revenue.
  • Large loads in neighboring cooperative territory would need transmission service agreements so no one can dodge the rules by crossing a boundary.
  • Ameren's direct testimony was expected in December, depending on the MISO order.

Territory matters too. Leaders asked for room to fit Ameren's territory instead of copying a ComEd approach, because the geography, network and economic development goals differ. The message is not anti data center. It is separating serious, funded projects from speculative requests.

Common mix-ups on large loads

  • A letter of credit is security held in reserve, not cash paid on day one.
  • The tariff is a proposal in a suspended docket. It is not approved.
  • Cost causation means the customer who creates the cost pays for it where the tariffs allow.

Part B, Retail suppliers and the one bill problem

  • Leaders cited about 441,000 customers, roughly 45 percent of the residential and small commercial base, taking supply from third parties.
  • They cited analyses showing these customers pay about 20 percent more than the state default rate on average, and said many callers did not know they had switched. Treat these as company reported claims.
  • Not every offer costs more. Fixed terms, promotions, renewable options and contract timing all affect a fair comparison.
Model 9.3

Read a bill

An imaginary household. All prices here are invented teaching numbers, not any real rate.

Bill on default supply
Bill on third party supply
How to read itEach bill is supply plus delivery. Delivery stays the same either way. Only the supply part moves with the supplier's price.
What it showsA supply price that is 20 percent higher is a smaller percent of the total bill, because delivery is unchanged. It is still real money for a household.
So whatBe precise about which percent you mean. "20 percent more on supply" and "20 percent more on the bill" are different claims.

Utility consolidated billing means one bill carries both delivery and supplier charges. Purchase of receivables means the utility buys the supplier's customer balances, here described as about 90 days at a small discount, and under current rules the gains and losses flow back to regulated customers. Leaders argued this blurs who is accountable and takes real collection risk away from suppliers.

How to read itUnder purchase of receivables the utility pays the supplier right away, less a small discount. If customers do not pay, the loss flows to regulated customers under current rules. In the other case the supplier carries it.
What it showsThe same unpaid balance lands on different people depending on the billing arrangement.
So whatThis is the accountability argument. If a supplier does not bear its own collection risk, regulated customers can end up funding it.
OptionUpsideTradeoff
Better disclosure and price comparisonsQuick, low conflictMay not fix risk allocation
Supplier bills its own customersClear accountabilityMore bills and possible confusion
Dual letters during a changeEases the transitionNeeds design and testing

Consumer advocates are wary of extra bills for households already confused by supply versus delivery. That concern is fair, so judge any reform by whether customers actually understand, not by how many pieces of paper they get. Utilities also operate under competitive neutrality rules, which limit what a service representative may say about a customer's deal. Expect organized opposition from suppliers and trade groups to any change in their billing arrangements.

Common mix-ups on supplier billing

  • Ameren's name on the bill does not mean Ameren set the supply price.
  • A utility cannot simply tell customers to switch back. Neutrality rules apply.
  • A percent higher supply price is not a percent higher total bill.

Check before using outside

For the large load tariff, the docket status and every number. Project examples shown in the meeting were awaiting executive approval before wider sharing, so the practice numbers above are invented on purpose. For supplier billing, the 20 percent figure, who is counted, the time period, and how losses on purchased receivables are treated by regulators.

Say it

Data centers, full version, about 45 seconds

We want data centers and the jobs that come with them. But one large project can require major line upgrades before it is built. If it never opens, existing customers should not be stuck with that bill. So the proposal asks for study deposits, proof the project is real, and a financial guarantee sized to the cost it creates. The goal is not to discourage serious projects. It is to tell serious, funded ones from speculation and make sure the cost lands where it belongs.

Supplier billing, full version, about 45 seconds

Your electric bill has two parts. Supply is the power itself, and delivery is the wires. Customers can buy supply from a third party, but the bill still shows Ameren's name. We have heard that many customers on third party supply pay more than the default rate and some do not realize they switched. We are not saying every offer is bad. We are asking whether customers get clear information, and whether suppliers should carry more of the billing responsibility themselves.

Hallway version, about 30 seconds

Two fairness questions. If a data center cancels after we have built lines for it, who pays, and the answer should be the developer, through a guarantee. And on supplier bills, customers should know when they have switched and what they are paying. Neither is anti business. Both are about putting costs where they belong.
Legislator asksWhy not use the same rules as ComEd?
You sayOur territory is different. We have different networks, different needs and we want to attract businesses. We support strong customer protection, but the solution should fit the territory.
Legislator asksWhy not just tell customers to switch back?
You sayUtility communications have to follow customer choice and competitive neutrality rules. What we can support is clear, understandable information that helps people compare their options.
