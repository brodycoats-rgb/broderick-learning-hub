Illinois’s Battery Storage Procurement

What the August 2026 awards mean for Ameren Illinois and its customers

Executive learning paper · September 26, 2026

Illinois has selected projects; it has not yet demonstrated their lifetime value to customers. The next task is to turn contracted storage into dependable service while managing a payment obligation that changes with wholesale-market conditions. For Ameren Illinois leadership, the central issue is the relationship between contract cost, customer benefit, and delivery risk.

This paper assumes familiarity with battery technology. It concentrates on the state’s procurement design and the financial decisions that follow. Numerical scenarios are illustrations, not forecasts or disclosures of Ameren’s contracts. Strategic recommendations are the author’s analysis, not official company positions.

1. How Illinois arrived here

The program developed through policy analysis, regulatory design, and legislation. The ICC’s 2025 storage workshops produced a May 2025 procurement framework. An earlier proposed 1,500 MW procurement did not itself establish the authority for the eventual 2026 event. That distinction matters: developing a procurement framework and authorizing utilities to incur recoverable obligations are separate steps. IPA’s 2026 procurement plan, historical discussion

The Clean and Reliable Grid Affordability Act, signed January 8, 2026 and effective June 1, supplied the legislative framework. It set a 3,000 MW storage objective for projects committed to operation by the end of 2030, with an initial procurement followed by additional events. IPA CRGA overview

The policy choice was consequential: support privately developed storage through long-term utility contracts. It creates an investment signal today while leaving much of the eventual customer cost dependent on tomorrow’s markets. The strategic question is therefore broader than whether Illinois secured enough bids: did it obtain commitments that can deliver useful capacity at an acceptable total cost?

2. What the auction actually accomplished

Bidding occurred August 26, 2026; the ICC approved results September 1. The outcome was uneven across the two regional markets:

Procurement Target Awarded Difference
Ameren Illinois / MISO 450 MW 520 MW 70 MW above target
ComEd / PJM 588 MW 80 MW 508 MW below target
Total 1,038 MW 600 MW 438 MW below target

These are awards, not operating capacity. The statewide result should not obscure the different outcomes in the two regions. IPA results announcement

The published average winning strike price was $110.52/MWh. That number describes the accepted bids; it is neither a household electricity rate nor a forecast of net payments. The administrator has explicitly said separate MISO and PJM averages are unavailable. It would be misleading to use the statewide figure as Ameren’s contract price. Official results notice, FAQ 74

From qualification to a binding price

Developers first demonstrated eligibility and readiness, then supplied certifications and bid security. Qualified projects submitted a strike-price bid. Each utility’s procurement was evaluated separately: bids above its confidential benchmark were excluded, and remaining bids ranked from lowest strike upward. Whole-project selection rules permitted limited target overshoot. An award above the MW target therefore does not, by itself, establish a procurement error.

The accepted project’s bid becomes its contract strike price. This is a pay-as-bid procurement, not a single clearing price paid to every winner. The ICC approves the selected results; utilities become contractual buyers. Final RFP rules

The benchmark screens bid acceptability. It cannot establish the final twenty-year customer bill, because future index values and performance remain unknown. Nor does the shortfall alone prove that the benchmark was too stringent: interconnection, readiness, financing, and participation may also explain supply. Those explanations require evidence before recommending a higher price or weaker qualification standard.

3. Strike price: the anchor, not the amount always paid

An indexed storage credit (ISC) is a contractual accounting unit tied to availability, rather than a purchased MWh of discharged electricity. Ameren is buying these credits; the seller owns and operates the project. The initial contracts run for twenty years. IPA bidder presentation, contract overview

The essential settlement relationship is:

Daily payment = (strike price − energy reference − capacity reference) × credited ISCs.

The strike price is the project’s accepted bid. The index combines a reference energy-arbitrage value and a reference capacity value. A positive difference means the utility owes the seller; a negative difference means the seller owes the utility. Daily amounts aggregate into monthly settlement. The mechanism offsets reference market value against the fixed strike. It does not reimburse the developer’s actual costs or guarantee its actual profit. ISC agreement, definitions and Articles 4–5

Three prices should stay separate in any presentation:

Price What it answers
Strike price What fixed bid anchors this project’s settlement?
Net ISC value After subtracting the index, what is owed per credited unit today?
Retail charge After aggregating recoverable costs and applying the tariff, what does a customer pay per kWh?

Converting $110.52/MWh into 11.052 cents/kWh is mathematically possible but does not calculate a customer’s storage charge. The quantities being priced and the retail billing denominator are different.

4. A complete worked payment example

Assume a hypothetical 100 MW project with a $110/MWh strike, full daily availability, and 400 credited ISCs. These are illustrative inputs, not an awarded project’s disclosed terms.

The energy reference uses the day’s four highest and four lowest hourly prices, adjusts charging cost using 85% efficiency, floors each paired spread at zero, and averages the four spreads. The capacity reference uses the applicable capacity price and ELCC accreditation, divided by four. ELCC represents the capacity contribution recognized for reliability purposes. ISC agreement, Sections 1.51–1.52 and Exhibit F

For a deliberately simple day, assume each of the four high prices is $100/MWh and each low price is $34/MWh:

  • Charging-cost adjustment: $34 ÷ 0.85 = $40/MWh.
  • Energy reference: $100 − $40 = $60/MWh.
  • Capacity reference, assuming a $200/MW-day price and 60% accreditation: $200 × 0.60 ÷ 4 = $30/MWh.
  • Combined index: $60 + $30 = $90/MWh.
  • Net ISC value: $110 − $90 = $20.
  • Daily utility payment: $20 × 400 = $8,000.

The project’s actual trading revenue could differ from the $90 reference. The index is a standardized calculation, not a statement of what its operators earned or a promise that they can execute every indexed trade.

What changing markets do to the same contract

Combined index Strike less index Daily settlement on 400 credits If repeated for 365 identical days
$50 $60 Utility pays $24,000 Utility pays $8.76 million
$90 $20 Utility pays $8,000 Utility pays $2.92 million
$130 −$20 Seller pays $8,000 Seller pays $2.92 million

The annual column is arithmetic, not an annual forecast. Actual settlements change daily and depend on contractual availability rules. In particular, the agreement treats positive and negative settlement days differently; an unplanned outage does not simply erase a seller’s negative-payment obligation. ISC agreement, Article 4

This illustrates the principal customer exposure: lower reference market revenues increase the support payment, all else equal. A fixed strike does not mean a fixed annual cost.

5. How the obligation reaches customer bills

Illinois law authorizes recovery of prudent and reasonable storage procurement and implementation costs through an ICC-approved tariff. It allocates storage costs across all retail customers through a uniform cents-per-kWh charge shown separately on bills. The statutory framework therefore extends beyond customers buying default supply from the utility. 220 ILCS 5/16-108(k)

Conceptually, the calculation proceeds from project settlements to the utility’s recoverable revenue requirement and then to a retail rate:

Illustrative retail charge = annual net recoverable dollars ÷ applicable annual billed kWh.

Assume, solely to show the conversion, $15 million of annual net recoverable costs and 30 billion applicable billed kWh:

$15,000,000 ÷ 30,000,000,000 = $0.0005/kWh = 0.05 cents/kWh.

A customer using 1,000 kWh in a month would pay $0.50 under that hypothetical rate. This is not an Ameren bill estimate. An actual calculation requires the approved tariff, utility-specific settlements, permitted administrative costs, reconciliation treatment, timing, and billing volumes. A forecast should also identify when seller payments reduce collections or produce customer credits. This paper does not establish a current storage-specific Ameren tariff rate or its effective date.

A storage charge and total customer value are different measures

Customers could pay a positive storage charge while benefiting from lower energy or capacity costs elsewhere in their bills. Conversely, a small charge does not prove a project delivers meaningful reliability value. A defensible assessment compares the customer’s total cost with storage against a credible without-storage case, using consistent assumptions and time periods.

This is especially important because the mechanism moves against market revenues. If additional storage helps reduce market prices, the index may fall and contract support may rise. A model that claims wholesale savings while keeping the index artificially unchanged can overstate the net benefit. Equally, counting seller repayments as a separate benefit after already netting them from program cost double-counts the same dollars.

For a household, the question is the combined bill effect. For a large commercial customer, scale and predictability also matter: a small cents-per-kWh change can become a material annual expense at high consumption.

6. How an Ameren executive should assess the program

Separate recoverability from affordability. A mechanism allowing cost recovery addresses the company’s exposure; it does not establish that the resulting customer burden is reasonable. Leadership needs both an earnings-and-cash-flow assessment and a customer-cost assessment. Because this procurement buys credits from third-party projects, the award should not be interpreted as an equivalent addition to Ameren-owned rate base.

Demand a range, not one annual payment number. Request low-, central-, and high-cost scenarios using Ameren’s actual contract portfolio. Vary energy spreads, capacity prices, accreditation, commissioning dates, availability, and billed sales. Show annual customer impacts and lifetime present value separately. A twenty-year total without discounting or a single favorable market year gives an incomplete view.

Connect accreditation to both cost and reliability. In the worked example, reducing accreditation from 60% to 40% lowers the capacity reference from $30 to $20. That increases support by $10 per credit, or $4,000 per fully credited day. At the same time, less capacity is being recognized for reliability. That combination merits particular attention: customers could support more while receiving less accredited capacity.

Verify what the contract actually ensures. The administrator clarifies that capacity-market participation is not itself required by the ISC agreement; nonparticipation does not automatically set the reference accreditation to zero. A procurement award consequently should not substitute for confirming how a project will contribute to the relevant resource-adequacy requirement. FAQ 71

Treat construction milestones as part of the affordability case. Delays can postpone expected market benefits and leave an identified need unresolved. Track interconnection, permits, financing, construction, and commercial operation. Credit support helps manage financial exposure; it does not replace an undelivered physical resource. The required security also depends on allowable unsecured credit, rather than necessarily requiring every seller to post the headline collateral amount in cash. FAQ 65

A useful internal reporting package would put four items together: project delivery status, forecast net settlements, estimated customer bill effects, and accredited capacity expected when needed. Keeping these in separate presentations makes it harder to see whether higher spending is buying better outcomes.

7. The next policy decisions

The initial award leaves 2,400 MW toward the broader 3,000 MW objective. The IPA’s September 23 request for information seeks input on procurement timing, regional allocation, project maturity, and possible eligibility changes. Responses are due October 28, 2026, at 5 p.m. Central. A draft storage procurement plan is scheduled for June 1, 2027. IPA request for information

For Ameren, the opportunity is to shape the next procurement around customer value and delivery credibility. Three priorities follow from the analysis:

  1. Use regional evidence to set the next quantities. The statewide shortfall should not automatically determine MISO’s next allocation. Examine resource needs, queue maturity, and competition in each region.
  2. Test the cost of speed. Earlier commitments may support delivery, but rushing immature projects can embed risk premiums. Later procurement can improve information while leaving less construction time. Compare both using realistic schedules.
  3. Evaluate design changes by who bears risk. A different contract or broader eligibility may attract more bids, but ask whether it changes operational control, market exposure, development risk, and the customer’s total obligation. A lower headline strike is not sufficient evidence of a better deal.

The initial projects were solicited against a December 31, 2029 commercial-operation deadline, subject to contractual extensions. Later procurements are being developed around the broader 2030 objective. Neither an award nor a statutory deadline establishes that a specific project will be operating on time. IPA bidder presentation, IPA request for information

8. Use during the department presentation

Follow the presenters’ sequence from results to costs to implementation. Listen for the evidence connecting each stage, then use the relevant question rather than working through a long checklist.

When they discuss… A useful question
Auction results “What does the 520 MW Ameren result tell us about competition and value, and what remains confidential or unknown?”
Strike prices and customer impact “Can you take one illustrative project from strike price, through the index and annual settlement, to the customer’s cents-per-kWh charge?”
Benefits “What is the expected net customer impact after both contract payments and market savings, and which assumptions drive the range?”
Reliability and delivery “How much accredited capacity do we expect, by when, and what milestones could change that expectation?”
Next procurement “Which changes should we advocate for now to improve customer value without weakening the likelihood of delivery?”

Brief meeting introduction

“Thanks for joining. I asked for this session to better understand the storage procurement, its cost to our customers, and the decisions ahead for Ameren. Thank you to our regulatory colleagues for walking us through it.”

9. Legislator briefing: extensive script

About 7–9 minutes with pauses. Written for a legislator or legislative staff member. The bracketed cues are memory prompts, not spoken lines. This is a practice script grounded in the sources above; recommendations express a proposed framing rather than an approved Ameren position.

[The Capitol: establish the policy]

“Thank you for taking the time. I want to walk through what Illinois has done on battery storage, what the first procurement accomplished, and how we should evaluate the cost to customers.

The most useful way to follow this is through five steps: the state establishes the policy, developers compete for contracts, the contracts determine payments, those costs flow through the utility’s approved tariff, and the projects must deliver the expected benefits.

The program grew out of policy work and the ICC’s 2025 storage workshops. The Clean and Reliable Grid Affordability Act supplied the legislative framework in 2026, including a broader objective of 3,000 megawatts committed to operation by the end of 2030.

That history matters because the law does more than express support for storage. It establishes a way to make long-term commitments that developers can use to support investment. Those commitments also create obligations that have to be evaluated from the customer’s perspective.”

[The bid room: explain what was selected]

“The first bidding event took place on August 26, and the ICC approved the results on September 1. The statewide target was 1,038 megawatts, and the approved awards totaled 600.

The regional picture is important. The Ameren Illinois procurement in MISO awarded 520 megawatts against a 450-megawatt target. The ComEd procurement in PJM awarded 80 against a 588-megawatt target. Those are different outcomes, and the next policy response should reflect the conditions in each region.

Developers had to qualify and demonstrate readiness before submitting their price. The procurement then screened bids against a confidential benchmark and ranked acceptable bids by strike price. Because projects are selected as whole projects, the rules allowed limited overshoot of a target.

Each successful project carries its own accepted price into its contract. The published statewide average winning strike price was $110.52 per megawatt-hour. That is useful context, but it is not Ameren’s disclosed average, and it is not what a household pays for electricity.

Most importantly, an award is a commitment to develop a project. It is not proof that the project is already operating.”

[The contract desk: explain the money]

“The utility contracts for indexed storage credits. These are accounting units used to calculate payments based on availability. The developer owns and operates the battery; the utility does not acquire the battery through this credit purchase.

The key term is strike price. Think of it as the fixed anchor in the contract. We subtract a reference value for energy-market opportunities and a reference value for capacity. The difference determines whether the utility pays the developer or the developer pays the utility.

Let me use an illustration. Assume a strike of $110 and a combined market reference of $90. The difference is $20 per credited unit. For a hypothetical 100-megawatt project receiving 400 credits that day, the utility pays $8,000.

If that reference falls to $50, the payment rises to $24,000 for the same number of credits. If the reference rises to $130, the direction reverses: the developer owes the utility $8,000.

Those numbers are examples, not actual Ameren contract disclosures. But they show the central point: a fixed strike does not produce a fixed annual customer cost.

There is another important distinction. The reference is a formula. It is not the developer’s actual earnings statement. The developer can perform better or worse than that reference. So we should describe this as an indexed payment arrangement, rather than a guarantee of a particular profit.”

[The kitchen table: connect it to the bill]

“Now bring that calculation to the customer’s bill. Illinois law provides for recovery of prudent and reasonable storage procurement costs through an approved tariff, with a uniform per-kilowatt-hour charge across retail customers.

To calculate the customer impact, we need the utility’s net recoverable costs and the applicable billing volume. We cannot just take the auction’s average strike price and convert it into a household rate.

For example, suppose annual net recoverable costs were $15 million, spread over 30 billion kilowatt-hours. That would equal five-hundredths of a cent per kilowatt-hour. A customer using 1,000 kilowatt-hours would pay fifty cents for that month. Again, that is an illustration, not an Ameren rate forecast.

The next question is whether customers receive benefits that justify the expense. Storage may affect wholesale energy and capacity costs, so the separate storage charge is only one part of the assessment. We need to compare the total customer cost with storage against a credible case without it.

That requires consistent modeling. If storage helps reduce market prices, the contract’s reference value may also fall, increasing the support payment. We should account for both effects rather than count the savings and assume the support obligation stays unchanged.

For a legislator, I would frame the accountability question this way: what will customers pay, what will they receive, and how will we know whether the program is delivering?”

[The project gate: test delivery and the next decision]

“From an Ameren perspective, I would evaluate this on three tracks: affordability, delivery, and financial administration.

On affordability, we need a range of customer impacts under different market conditions. On delivery, we need to know when the projects will be connected and how much capacity they will actually contribute to reliability. On financial administration, we need to understand settlement, recovery, and the timing of any customer credits.

Accreditation is particularly important. A project’s nameplate megawatts are not automatically the same as the capacity recognized for reliability. A lower accreditation can also reduce the capacity reference in the payment formula, increasing customer support, all else equal. We need to see both consequences together.

The next procurement should build on what this first event teaches us. The IPA is seeking stakeholder feedback by October 28, and a draft storage procurement plan is scheduled for June 2027. This is an opportunity to examine regional needs, project readiness, competition, and how contract design allocates risk.

My suggested approach is to support decisions that connect credible delivery with demonstrable customer value. An auction result is an important milestone. The standard of success is whether the projects arrive when needed and provide benefits that justify the total cost to customers.”

10. Legislator briefing: moderate script

About 3–4 minutes. Use for a short office visit or a staff briefing. The five bold cues follow the same sequence as the longer script.

Policy. “Illinois’s first storage procurement moves the state from policy development into long-term project commitments. The program grew out of regulatory work and the Clean and Reliable Grid Affordability Act, which established a broader 3,000-megawatt objective for projects committed to operation by the end of 2030.

Procurement. Bidding took place August 26, with results approved September 1. The state awarded 600 megawatts against a 1,038-megawatt target. Ameren’s MISO procurement awarded 520 megawatts, while ComEd’s PJM procurement awarded 80. Those regional differences matter when designing the next event.

Qualified developers competed on a strike price, with acceptable bids ranked against a confidential benchmark. Each winning project receives its own accepted contract price. These are project awards; they are not yet evidence of operating capacity.

Payment. The contract buys indexed storage credits from a privately owned project. The strike price is the fixed anchor. We subtract reference energy and capacity values to calculate the net payment.

For a simple example, assume a $110 strike and a $90 combined reference. The utility pays the $20 difference for each credited unit. If the reference rises above the strike, the developer owes the utility. If the reference falls, the utility’s support payment increases. The reference is a formula, not a guarantee of the developer’s actual earnings.

Customer. That is why the published $110.52 statewide average strike is not a household electricity rate. Customer charges depend on net recoverable costs spread over the applicable kilowatt-hour sales through the approved tariff.

For illustration, $15 million spread over 30 billion kilowatt-hours would mean fifty cents for a customer using 1,000 kilowatt-hours in a month. That is not an Ameren forecast; it simply shows how the calculation reaches a bill.

We also need to evaluate possible energy and capacity savings. The right question is the total customer impact with the program compared with the cost without it, using consistent assumptions.

Delivery. From a utility perspective, I would focus on whether the projects will arrive on time, how much accredited capacity they will provide, and the range of customer costs under different market conditions.

As the IPA prepares the next procurement, the opportunity is to improve competition and delivery while keeping customer value central. The first auction establishes commitments. The next test is whether those commitments produce dependable benefits at a reasonable total cost.”

12. A story you can walk through from memory

Imagine taking a legislator on a five-stop walk. This is an imagined memory aid, not an account of a real meeting.

First, stand at the Capitol. The legislator hands you a folder labeled “authority.” It contains the law and the state’s objective. Say: “The state created the framework for long-term storage commitments.”

Next, enter a bid room. Developers place envelopes on a table. A screen checks qualifications and the benchmark before the acceptable prices are ranked. Say: “Developers compete; the ICC approves awards; the utilities become contract buyers.”

Then, sit at a contract desk with a calculator. Write three numbers: 110 − 90 = 20. Draw an arrow that can point either toward the developer or toward the utility. Say: “The strike is fixed, but the net payment moves with the index.”

Now, sit at a customer’s kitchen table. Put the contract beside an electricity bill. You need one more calculation before explaining the charge: net recoverable dollars divided by applicable billed kilowatt-hours. Say: “The strike is not the retail rate; total customer value includes both costs and benefits.”

Finally, arrive at a project gate. The contract folder will not open the gate by itself. The project needs permits, financing, interconnection, and construction. Say: “An award becomes useful only when the project delivers the expected service.”

Your route is Capitol → bid room → contract desk → kitchen table → project gate. If you lose your place, picture the next location. You only need to remember five ideas: authority, selection, settlement, bill, delivery.

A two-minute rehearsal

  1. Look at the five locations for ten seconds, then look away.
  2. Give one sentence at each stop. Do not chase the exact wording of either script.
  3. Explain 110 − 90 = 20, then explain why that $20 is not the customer’s retail rate.
  4. Finish with one question: “What do customers pay, what do they receive, and will it arrive on time?”

One evaluation criterion: Could your listener distinguish the strike price, the net contract payment, and the customer’s bill charge? If yes, the most important financial distinction came through.

Question A concise response you can use
“Will this lower bills?” “We need the net comparison: contract costs and other implementation costs alongside energy and capacity benefits. The award alone does not establish bill savings.”
“Is $110.52 what customers pay?” “No. It is the published statewide average winning strike. The index is subtracted to determine settlement, and the retail charge requires a separate calculation.”
“Does Ameren own these batteries?” “This procurement contracts for credits from projects owned and operated by their developers.”
“Why did the state miss its target?” “The shortfall was concentrated in PJM. We should examine participation, readiness, interconnection, and pricing evidence before assigning a single cause.”
“What should we watch next?” “Project delivery milestones, the customer-cost range, and changes proposed for the next procurement.”

If you need to recover your train of thought, use: “Let me connect that back to the customer: the contract determines settlement, the tariff determines the charge, and project delivery determines whether the expected benefit arrives.”
