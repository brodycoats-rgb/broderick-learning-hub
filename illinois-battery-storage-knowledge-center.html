Illinois Battery Storage Knowledge Center

From policy development to the August 2026 procurement—and the work ahead

Research current through September 26, 2026
Purpose: Understand why Illinois is pursuing battery storage, who makes the decisions, how the first procurement worked, and what must happen before customers receive the intended benefits.

1. Start with the central story

Illinois’s battery-storage effort involves three connected challenges:

  1. Establishing a workable policy and contracting framework.
  2. Attracting projects that can be financed, permitted, and connected.
  3. Turning those projects into dependable service at a reasonable customer cost.

The August 26, 2026 procurement was a major step in the second challenge. It produced awards supporting 600 megawatts of new storage, against a 1,038 MW target. The ICC approved the results September 1. The outcome was geographically uneven: 520 MW in MISO territory and 80 MW in PJM territory. IPA results announcement

The broader program established through the Clean and Reliable Grid Affordability Act—CRGA, Public Act 104-0458—targets 3,000 MW committed to commercial operation by the end of 2030. CRGA was signed January 8, 2026, and became effective June 1. IPA CRGA implementation page

The distinction to remember is:

A policy target describes the objective. An award establishes a contractual commitment. An operating project provides the physical service.

2. What a battery actually contributes

A battery stores electricity generated elsewhere and releases it later. Its value depends on the problem it is being asked to solve.

For example, electricity demand may remain high after solar output declines. A charged battery can help cover that evening period. At another location, a battery might help manage a short local peak. Those are different applications and require different evidence.

Five terms to understand

Term Plain-language meaning Example
MW—megawatts How quickly the battery can deliver power A 100 MW battery can discharge at a rate of 100 MW.
MWh—megawatt-hours How much energy it can deliver A battery with 400 MWh of usable energy holds enough for a defined period of discharge.
Duration How long it can sustain a specified output 400 MWh ÷ 100 MW = four hours.
Round-trip efficiency How much charging energy comes back out At 85% efficiency, 100 MWh charged yields approximately 85 MWh discharged.
State of charge How much energy is currently available A large battery with little charge cannot immediately deliver its full-duration service.

Worked example

A hypothetical factory uses 25 MW continuously. Over 24 hours, it needs:

25 MW × 24 hours = 600 MWh.

A 25 MW / 100 MWh battery could cover roughly four hours at that output, before practical limitations. It would then need recharging.

That battery may be valuable, but its power rating alone does not demonstrate that it can serve the factory around the clock.

Equipment size and reliability contribution are different

Reliability planning asks how much dependable support a resource provides during stressed conditions. The storage contract recognizes this distinction through an RTO capacity-value measure called effective load carrying capability, or ELCC, or its applicable replacement. Final ISC agreement, definitions

For a policymaker, the useful questions are:

  • Is it charged when needed?
  • How long can it run?
  • Can it recharge between difficult periods?
  • Is it connected where its output can help?
  • How much of its rated capacity counts toward reliability requirements?

3. How Illinois arrived at the auction

The August auction grew out of several years of analysis, legislation, and procurement design.

The policy-development timeline

Period Development Why it matters
2021–2022 CEJA-era storage policy work included an ICC Energy Storage Program Report released in May 2022. Illinois examined storage’s benefits, costs, and deployment barriers before designing this procurement.
March 2024 IPA released its final Policy Study, including analysis of state-supported storage deployment. The state evaluated proposed policy effects on reliability, rates, emissions, jobs, and communities.
Early 2025 The ICC conducted storage procurement workshops. Attention shifted toward workable contracts, eligibility, geographic allocation, and customer protection.
May 1, 2025 ICC staff submitted its procurement report. The report supplied recommendations and a model contracting framework.
Late 2025–June 2026 CRGA moved from passage to enactment and effectiveness. The new law provided the framework for the 2026 procurement.
August–September 2026 Bids were submitted and results approved. Policy design became specific project awards.

Sources: IPA discussion of the 2022 report, IPA annual report documenting the final 2024 study, ICC 2025 workshop record, and CRGA implementation.

Why the 2025 work matters

The workshops considered questions that remain central today:

  • What contract would lenders accept?
  • Who would operate the batteries?
  • How should utilities recover contract costs?
  • What project-development milestones should bidders meet?
  • How should procurement be divided between MISO and PJM?
  • How should labor and equity requirements apply?

The earlier work contemplated an initial procurement of up to 1,500 MW. It should not be confused with the final 1,038 MW target for August 2026. ICC workshop record

The IPA’s electricity procurement plan explains that the earlier contemplated 2025 procurement required additional legislative authorization that did not pass during the spring 2025 session. Designing a procurement process did not itself authorize that auction. IPA 2026 Electricity Procurement Plan

The reliability-planning connection

Separately, Illinois’s December 2025 Resource Adequacy Study examined whether sufficient resources would be available under future conditions. The state describes its findings as potential shortfall risks under certain scenarios, with subsequent mitigation planning intended to evaluate responses. IPA mitigation-plan explanation

The learning point is that a modeled risk helps identify a need; procurement and project development determine whether a response materializes.

4. Who does what

The state is coordinating several institutions with different responsibilities.

Participant Role in this effort
General Assembly and Governor Establish the legal authority, objectives, and requirements.
Illinois Power Agency—IPA Develops and administers the procurement program within that authority.
Illinois Commerce Commission—ICC Reviews and approves procurement results and exercises its applicable regulatory responsibilities.
NERA Economic Consulting Serves as procurement administrator, handling the bidding process and evaluation.
Bates White Serves as procurement monitor for the Commission.
Ameren Illinois and ComEd Serve as buyers under the applicable indexed storage credit contracts.
Winning developers and project companies Undertake project development and fulfill the seller’s contractual obligations.
MISO and PJM Provide the regional grid and wholesale-market framework in which projects connect and participate.

The administrator, monitor, utility buyers, and approval sequence are identified in the ICC’s public results notice.

Local governments, emergency responders, labor organizations, landowners, and host communities also matter to implementation. Their concerns become concrete through site design, construction, emergency planning, workforce arrangements, and community engagement.

Ameren learning point: A contract associated with Ameren Illinois does not establish that Ameren owns the battery. Always distinguish the buyer, project owner, grid connection, and physical location.

5. How the August procurement worked

The event used a formal request for proposals, or RFP, with advance qualification and standardized documents.

The preparation sequence

April 22 Initial draft contract and preliminary requirements
May 13 First-round comments due
June 1 Final draft materials
June 15 Second-round comments due
June 29 Final contract and RFP documents
July 16 Part 1 proposals due
August 12 Part 2 proposals, excluding bids, due
August 26 Bid submission
August 28 Administrator and monitor reports
September 1 ICC decision
September 4 Scheduled contract execution deadline

These dates come from the published procurement calendar. The final June materials are available on the procurement administrator’s website.

How bids were selected

Ameren and ComEd procurements were evaluated separately. Bids failing the applicable price benchmark were excluded. Remaining bids were ranked by strike price, and projects were selected in that order.

The rules allowed the marginal project—the next project in the ranking—to push procurement above the target, provided the excess was no more than 50%. Projects were not selected out of price order simply to create a better numerical fit. Final RFP rules, Section V.2

Why this matters: A target is not an instruction to purchase any offer necessary to fill it. Qualification, price, and project-size rules can leave an event under target—or permit it to finish above target.

6. What Illinois was purchasing

Developers bid to sell indexed storage credits, or ISCs, through a 20-year contract. IPA storage program overview

An ISC is connected to a project’s availability under the contract. The pricing arrangement compares the project’s bid strike price with an index representing energy and capacity market value.

In simplified terms:

Contract value = strike price − reference market value.

If the reference value falls, support payments can rise. If it rises above the strike price, the seller can owe money to the utility. The actual calculations include contractual availability and settlement rules. Final ISC agreement

A hypothetical pricing example

Assume a hypothetical strike price of $100/MWh:

Reference value Simplified difference Direction
$70/MWh +$30/MWh Support payable to the project
$100/MWh $0/MWh No difference
$120/MWh −$20/MWh Payment owed back to the utility

These are teaching numbers, not the winning bids.

The distinction matters for customer-cost analysis. Multiplying a strike price by project capacity does not calculate a household bill impact. Capacity is measured in MW, settlement quantities have their own rules, and market reference values change.

The contract also links utility payment obligations to authorized cost recovery and requires an interconnection agreement before payments. ISC agreement, Sections 5.4–5.6

What can—and cannot—be concluded

The results demonstrate that the event secured substantially more capacity in the MISO procurement than in PJM.

They do not, by themselves, tell us:

  • How much potential capacity never entered the bidding process.
  • Which development obstacles discouraged participation.
  • How many unsuccessful offers failed qualification or price requirements.
  • Whether all winning projects will reach operation on schedule.

Those distinctions matter before attributing the shortfall to one cause.

Analytical conclusion: The next procurement needs both a credible project pipeline and suitable purchasing rules. Changing only the target does not necessarily produce more buildable projects.

8. From award to operating battery

The post-award period combines contractual obligations with physical development.

Award → executed contract → financial assurance → development and approvals → construction → testing → commercial operation → continuing performance

Several activities overlap. This sequence is a teaching aid, not a claim that every project begins permitting only after winning.

The IPA’s post-award guidance identifies obligations including:

  • Execution of the agreement and project-specific product order.
  • Performance assurance generally based on $50,000 per MW, due within 15 business days after approval.
  • Operational demonstrations and reporting.
  • Labor, equity, and cybersecurity compliance.

Operating requirements include at least 20 MW, four-hour discharge capability, specified efficiency standards, and minimum annual availability. The guidance states that the underlying contract and RFP govern if its summary differs from them. IPA post-award requirements

Two deadlines to keep separate

The first procurement targeted projects operating by December 31, 2029, with a contractual extension pathway into 2030. The broader program’s initial objective is 3,000 MW by the end of 2030. ICC results notice

A practical project-review card

For each winner, track:

Question Evidence to seek
Is the project contractually committed? Executed agreement and required assurances
Can it connect? Interconnection agreement, studies, upgrade obligations
Can it be built at the proposed site? Site control and applicable approvals
Can it be financed? Financing commitments and construction authorization
Is equipment available? Procurement and delivery schedule
Can it perform? Commissioning and operational test results
Is it delivering lasting value? Availability, settlement, and compliance records

These are recommended review questions. This guide does not establish each winner’s current status against them.

9. Labor, equity, safety, and community interests

These considerations affect project delivery and the quality of the resulting investment.

Construction labor

The IPA explains that the general contractor must enter a project labor agreement before construction, covering workers needed to construct the facility. Such agreements address employment conditions and labor-management arrangements for the project. IPA PLA guidance

Prevailing wage is a separate obligation. Contractors must pay applicable compensation and maintain required payroll documentation. A PLA and prevailing-wage compliance are related but distinct requirements. IPA prevailing-wage guidance

Operations labor

A labor peace agreement concerns ongoing onsite operation and maintenance. It addresses labor organizing access and economic disruption provisions. It serves a different stage of the project’s life than the construction PLA. IPA labor-peace guidance

Workforce equity

For the August 2026 procurement, the Minimum Equity Standard requires a project workforce comprising at least 10% Equity Eligible Persons, with compliance planning and reporting. The IPA’s guidance describes this procurement’s MES obligation as applying through the construction phase, rather than continuing through operations and maintenance. IPA MES guidance

Do not assume that this percentage or reporting framework will remain unchanged for later procurements.

Community and safety review

Useful questions for a proposed site include:

  • What equipment and safety systems are proposed?
  • How will emergency responders prepare?
  • Who maintains emergency contact information?
  • What access, noise, and construction impacts require attention?
  • How will equipment be managed at the end of its useful life?
  • Which commitments are enforceable, and who verifies them?

The IPA links a battery-storage fire-safety toolkit as a learning resource. It is not itself a project approval.

CRGA also created an ICC local-siting dispute-resolution process. That should not be interpreted as making every site or approval automatic. ICC state energy-planning overview

11. What happens next

The immediate decision window

On September 23, 2026, IPA issued a request for information on the first procurement and future procurement design. Responses are due October 28, 2026, at 5 p.m. Central.

The agency is asking about:

  • Participation barriers and lessons from 2026.
  • Timing and number of future events.
  • Alignment with interconnection milestones.
  • Geographic allocation and treatment of unfilled targets.
  • Whether to procure the remaining 2,400 MW in 2027 or spread procurement into 2028.
  • Co-located or hybrid resources.
  • Project-maturity requirements and alternative contracting approaches.

These are questions under consideration, not adopted changes. September 23 IPA request for information

The longer timetable

The plan-development dates and unresolved ICC schedule are published on the IPA storage program page.

The central design tradeoff

Very strict entry requirements may exclude projects that could become viable. Loose requirements may produce awards that are unlikely to become operating facilities.

The policy task is to attract meaningful competition while requiring convincing evidence of delivery.

12. Ameren Illinois: how to evaluate the effort

For Ameren-focused government affairs, five questions organize the work.

First, will the projects arrive when the system needs them?
Track the development schedule alongside the resource-need timeline.

Second, will they deliver the right service?
Examine duration, charging opportunities, location, and dependable capacity.

Third, what is the customer value?
Separate contract payments from possible wholesale-market benefits and other costs. A complete assessment needs assumptions, timing, and sensitivity analysis.

Fourth, what could delay implementation?
Identify the particular barrier: interconnection, permitting, equipment, financing, labor availability, or another requirement. Different barriers require different responses.

Fifth, what decisions can government affairs influence now?
The feedback process is an opportunity to present evidence about procurement design and implementation. A useful submission connects a proposed change to the problem it solves, its authority, and its customer consequences.

A hypothetical executive-meeting example

Question: “We procured 520 MW for the Ameren area. Does that solve our reliability issue?”

Modeled response:

“The awards are a meaningful addition to the development pipeline. To evaluate reliability, we still need to know when the projects will operate, how much dependable capacity they provide, and whether they can deliver during the hours and at the locations that matter. We should track those conditions alongside the state’s resource-planning work.”

Practice cue: Amount. Timing. Performance. Cost.

Brief meeting introduction

Thanks, everyone, for joining. I asked for this session so we could better understand Illinois’s battery storage procurement and what it means for Ameren Illinois and our customers. Thanks to our regulatory colleagues for walking us through it.

Illinois Battery Storage: Wednesday Presentation Companion

Listening, interpreting, and contributing from an Ameren Illinois government-affairs perspective

Your purpose in the room

Leave able to explain:

  • What the state required and what the procurement accomplished.
  • What Ameren Illinois is responsible for.
  • How and when customers could benefit—and what they may pay.
  • What remains uncertain.
  • Where government affairs can help.

You do not need to ask every question below. Listen for what the presenters answer naturally, then select the question that closes the most important remaining gap.

Your listening cue: Need → Process → Result → Responsibility → Customer → Next decision.

1. When they explain why Illinois pursued storage

What you are hearing

The presenters may discuss reliability, changing electricity demand, the generation mix, affordability, and legislative requirements.

What to think about

“Which specific problem is this procurement intended to address?”

Storage can serve different purposes. Helping cover a regional supply shortfall is different from reducing exposure to expensive hours or improving a particular local circuit. A broad reference to “reliability” may encompass several things.

A useful question

“For Ameren Illinois customers, which problem is this procurement primarily intended to address, and how will we measure whether it succeeds?”

Why this question helps

It connects the policy objective to a measurable outcome. It also gives you language to use when a legislator asks, “What are customers getting for this?”

Benefit to explore

Storage may add flexibility and support the system during valuable hours.

Concern to listen for

Whether the presentation clearly connects the selected resources to the identified need, including timing and location.

Write down:
“The primary customer problem this addresses is __________.”

2. When they describe the law and agency process

What you are hearing

CRGA, IPA procurement requirements, ICC approval, standardized contracts, and the roles of the administrator and monitor.

What to think about

“Which decisions were fixed by statute, and which involved agency judgment?”

That distinction matters for advocacy. An implementation concern may be addressed through agency feedback, a regulatory proceeding, a regional-market process, or legislation. The appropriate route depends on who has authority.

A useful question

“Which parts of this process are fixed in statute, and where does the IPA have flexibility to make improvements before the next procurement?”

If they identify a particular problem

“Would addressing that require legislation, an agency decision, or action through MISO?”

Why this question helps

It moves discussion toward a practical route for solving the problem.

Write down:
“The decision-maker for this issue is __________.”

3. When they explain how the auction selected projects

What you are hearing

Qualification requirements, project readiness, pricing, benchmarks, and selection rules.

What to think about

“How did the process balance competitive pricing with confidence that projects would actually be built?”

A low-priced commitment has limited value if the project cannot reach operation. Conversely, requirements that are too restrictive can reduce competition.

A useful question

“How did the qualification process assess whether a project could meet its operating deadline, and what development risks remain after selection?”

What a helpful answer would contain

Specific milestones, rather than a general statement that the projects were vetted—for example, the relevant evidence of interconnection progress, site readiness, or financial commitment.

Benefit to explore

Qualification and contractual requirements can improve confidence in delivery.

Concern to listen for

Whether “qualified to bid” is being treated as equivalent to “fully ready to build.”

Write down:
“The largest remaining delivery risk is __________.”

4. When they present the results

The established starting point is:

Procurement Target Awarded
Ameren / MISO 450 MW 520 MW
ComEd / PJM 588 MW 80 MW
Statewide 1,038 MW 600 MW

The ICC approved the results September 1, following the August 26 procurement. IPA results announcement

What to think about

“The statewide shortfall and the Ameren-area outcome tell different stories.”

Avoid assuming that the geographic difference has one known cause. The results alone do not establish why projects did not participate or win.

A useful question

“What do we know about the difference between the MISO and PJM results, and which lessons are relevant to the next procurement for our customers?”

A more focused follow-up

“Does the MISO result suggest a repeatable project pipeline, or did this event capture a limited group of particularly ready projects?”

Why this question helps

It tests whether the first result provides a sound basis for expectations about later rounds.

Benefit to explore

The Ameren/MISO procurement exceeded its initial target.

Concern to listen for

Whether a strong first-round result is being used to imply that later development will be equally straightforward.

Write down:
“The results establish __________; they do not yet establish __________.”

5. When they explain what this means for Ameren

What you are hearing

Contract responsibilities, administration, cost recovery, project oversight, interconnection, and customer communications.

What to think about

“What are we responsible for, what can we influence, and what belongs to the developer or another institution?”

A utility’s role as contract buyer is different from owning and operating a battery. Company benefits may include system and customer outcomes; do not assume that every procurement creates a utility earnings opportunity.

A useful question

“Could you separate Ameren’s contractual and administrative responsibilities from the developer’s delivery obligations, particularly if a project is delayed?”

If they discuss financial implications

“From the company’s perspective, is the main financial issue cost recovery and cash timing, or are there other material exposures we should understand?”

Why this question helps

You need to know what the company can reasonably be held accountable for when policymakers or customers raise concerns.

Concern to listen for

A mismatch between operational control and public expectations. Ameren may receive questions about outcomes that depend partly on developers, agencies, or regional processes.

Write down:
“Ameren controls __________ and depends on others for __________.”

6. When they explain customer costs and benefits

This is the section where I would listen most closely.

What to think about

“How do costs and benefits reach a customer, and do they arrive at the same time?”

Listen for distinctions among:

  • Contract payments and administrative costs.
  • Possible wholesale energy or capacity benefits.
  • The timing of charges and benefits.
  • Differences among customer groups.
  • Expected outcomes versus demonstrated outcomes.

A program may provide value while still creating a visible charge. A projected market benefit is not automatically a guaranteed reduction on an individual bill.

Your strongest customer question

“How should we explain the expected net effect on an Ameren Illinois customer’s bill, including when costs begin and when benefits are expected?”

If they do not yet have an estimate

“What information or milestone will allow us to make that assessment with more confidence?”

If they say the program will save customers money

“What is the comparison behind that estimate—what would customers otherwise be paying?”

Why these questions help

They establish the timing, assumptions, and comparison needed for a credible affordability explanation.

Benefit to explore

Whether the program can reduce exposure to expensive or constrained periods.

Concern to listen for

Whether modeled savings, contract payments, and household bill effects are being combined into one number without explanation.

Write down:
“The customer benefit depends most on __________.”

7. When they discuss reliability and operating performance

What you are hearing

Megawatts, battery duration, availability, capacity accreditation, and expected operating dates.

What to think about

“How much dependable support will be available when it matters?”

Keep three concepts distinct:

Rated output → stored energy and duration → recognized reliability contribution.

Also distinguish regional resource adequacy from local outage performance. A resource that supports the regional system does not necessarily prevent outages caused by a damaged distribution line.

A useful question

“How should we translate the 520 MW of awards into dependable reliability value, considering duration, charging needs, and MISO’s capacity rules?”

If the discussion turns to customer messaging

“Which reliability benefits can we reasonably connect to these projects, and which outage concerns require other investments?”

Why this question helps

It prevents an oversimplified promise that “more batteries” means protection from every kind of outage.

Write down:
“The reliability benefit is __________, subject to __________.”

8. When they discuss implementation and next steps

What you are hearing

Project milestones, future procurements, stakeholder comments, and longer-term planning.

What to think about

“Which decision is still open, and where would our input be useful?”

The IPA’s September 23 request for information asks about procurement timing, geographic allocation, project maturity, resource types, and contracting. Comments are due October 28, 2026, at 5 p.m. Central. These are active design questions rather than settled changes. IPA stakeholder request

Your strongest government-affairs question

“As the company considers feedback for the next procurement, what implementation issue would be most useful for government affairs to help address?”

If an issue is identified

“What evidence and stakeholder engagement would help, and when would that input be most valuable?”

Benefit to explore

The company can apply lessons from the first event while the next process is being developed.

Concern to listen for

Whether a proposed change solves a documented problem and preserves customer protection.

Write down:
“The next decision is __________, and our useful contribution is __________.”

Three questions to have ready

If the presentation answers most of your questions, these are the three I would keep in reserve:

Customer understanding

“What is the clearest way to explain the expected customer benefit, its timing, and the uncertainty that remains?”

Company responsibility

“Which risks can Ameren manage directly, and which depend on developers or regional processes?”

Government-affairs contribution

“Where would government affairs involvement be most useful before the next procurement?”

Choose the one that best fits what remains unanswered. One well-timed question can be more useful than several partially answered ones.

How to enter the discussion naturally

Use a brief connection to the presentation, then ask one question.

After a slide on customer benefits:

“That helps connect the procurement to customer value. When we explain this externally, what should we say about when customers would begin seeing that benefit?”

After a discussion of project risks:

“You mentioned interconnection as a remaining dependency. Where can the company influence that timeline?”

At the end of the presentation:

“For those of us working with legislators and local officials, what is the most important point to get right—and what should we avoid overstating?”

That last question is particularly useful if the technical discussion has been thorough and your remaining need is a reliable external explanation.

If you hear a statement that seems too broad

Clarify the scope before challenging the conclusion.

If you hear… You can ask…
“We secured 520 MW.” “Are we describing contracted capacity here, and what are the next milestones toward operation?”
“This will lower bills.” “What assumptions drive that result, and over what period?”
“This improves reliability.” “Which type of reliability improvement are we expecting?”
“The auction fell short.” “What do we know about the cause, and what is still being evaluated?”
“We need a policy change.” “Which requirement needs to change, and who has authority to make that change?”

These questions invite precision while giving the presenter room to explain.

Mark statements as:

  • Fact: A completed event or established requirement.
  • Expectation: A forecast or anticipated outcome.
  • Open decision: Something still being developed.

This will help you avoid repeating an expectation later as a company commitment.

A short rehearsal before Wednesday

Read these three cues once:

Customer. Responsibility. Next decision.

Then practice one question aloud twice. Keep it under 25 seconds and avoid adding a second question before the first is answered.

If you lose your place, use:

“Let me narrow that to the customer impact.”

Or:

“The part I’m trying to understand is the timing.”

After the presentation, complete this sentence:

“The procurement accomplished __________. For Ameren and its customers, the next important test is __________.”

Your measure of success: Can you explain what happened, why it matters, and what remains uncertain in one minute?
