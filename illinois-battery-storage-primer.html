Module 0

The big picture in one minute

You will be able to tell the whole story in five sentences, then hang every later detail on this map.

Plain-language script

Illinois passed a law to buy 3,000 megawatts of grid batteries by the end of 2030. The first auction contracted 600.

Illinois wants a large fleet of batteries to keep the lights on and bills steady as older power plants retire and demand grows. The Clean and Reliable Grid Affordability Act, known as CRGA, made 3,000 megawatts the state goal.

The Illinois Power Agency runs the buying. Developers bid for 20-year contracts. The Illinois Commerce Commission approves the winners. The local utility signs the contract and collects the cost from customers through a line on the bill called a rider.

The first auction ran on August 26, 2026. The state asked for 1,038 megawatts and contracted 600. Ameren Illinois's region got 520 megawatts from four projects, more than its 450 megawatt target. ComEd's region got 80 megawatts from one project, far below its 588 megawatt target.

Nothing from this auction is running yet. Ahead are feedback on the auction due October 28, a statewide reliability plan in mid-November, an Ameren Illinois rider that the debrief says should be filed this year, and more auctions in 2027.

Module 1 of 11

Why the grid needs batteries

You will be able to explain in two sentences what a grid battery does and why Illinois wants more of them.

Plain-language script

A grid battery moves electricity from a time when it is cheap to a time when it is needed.

Electricity has one unusual rule. The amount put onto the grid has to match the amount people use, every second of the day. Power plants do that job. In the late afternoon and evening, demand is high and solar output is fading, so the grid needs supply that can arrive fast.

A grid battery is a large bank of rechargeable cells plugged into the high-voltage system. It charges when electricity is plentiful and cheap. It discharges when electricity is scarce and expensive. The batteries in this course are designed to deliver full power for four hours. That is what "four-hour duration" means. A 100 MW battery with four hours of duration holds up to 400 MWh.

Two limits matter from the start. A battery does not make energy. It stores energy made elsewhere and loses some on the way, so it takes in more than it gives back. And four hours is short. A battery helps with an evening peak. It helps much less with a cold snap that lasts several days.

Why is Illinois buying them now? State agencies report resource adequacy challenges in both grid regions that serve Illinois. In ICC workshops in early 2025, NRDC and the Union of Concerned Scientists each presented analyses estimating that Illinois could need up to 3,000 MW of storage by 2030. That figure became the state goal. For scale, news coverage cites about 141 MW of utility-scale storage installed in Illinois today, and another outlet says about 200 MW.

Module 2 of 11

Who's who

You will be able to name who buys, who approves, who pays, and which grid operator covers which part of Illinois.

Plain-language script

Five groups matter. One fact about the grid confuses almost every newcomer, so learn it first.

The General Assembly passes the laws. The Illinois Power Agency, or IPA, runs the buying. The Illinois Commerce Commission, or ICC, is the state utility regulator and approves the results. Utilities, Ameren Illinois and ComEd, sign the contracts and collect the money from customers. Developers build and run the batteries.

Here is the fact. Illinois is split between two regional grid operators, called RTOs. MISO runs the grid in central and southern Illinois, where Ameren Illinois serves customers. PJM runs the grid in northern Illinois, where ComEd serves customers. Each has its own rules, its own waiting line for new projects, and its own capacity market. The storage program buys separately in each region.

The IPA hired a consulting firm, NERA, to run the bidding itself. NERA is called the procurement administrator.

Key terms

Ameren angle

Ameren Illinois is the utility buyer for projects in MISO Zone 4. ComEd is the buyer for projects in the PJM ComEd area. Ameren Illinois does not select the winners. S8S3

Module 3 of 11

How we got here

You will be able to place the key dates in order and say which law did what.

Plain-language script

The storage program started as a workshop and became a statewide goal in about a year.

An earlier law, Public Act 103-1066, told the ICC to run workshops on how to buy utility-scale storage. Seven workshops drew more than 200 people between late January and March 2025. The ICC staff report followed on May 1, 2025.

That report made the main design choices. Use a 20-year contract called an indexed storage credit. Start with about 1,038 MW of standalone batteries, split 450 MW for Ameren's zone in MISO and 588 MW for ComEd's area in PJM. Aim for at least 3,000 MW by 2030.

The General Assembly passed the Clean and Reliable Grid Affordability Act on October 30, 2025. Governor Pritzker signed it on January 8, 2026 as Public Act 104-0458, and it took effect June 1, 2026. CRGA made the 3,000 MW target part of state law and set the first procurement for no later than August 26, 2026.

You may notice that the ICC report talks about an August 2025 bidding date. That date came from the earlier law. The first auction ran a year later, under CRGA. The auction took place on August 26, 2026, and the ICC approved the results on September 1.

Module 4 of 11

What the law requires

You will be able to list CRGA's storage goal, the main bidder rules, and the price check that protects customers.

Plain-language script

CRGA sets a goal, says how to buy, and puts guardrails on price, labor and planning.

The goal is 3,000 MW of new storage under contract and committed to reach commercial operation by December 31, 2030. The IPA must buy it in rounds. Round one targeted 1,038 MW of standalone storage. More rounds are planned for 2027 and possibly 2028.

Bidders face rules. Contracts run 20 years. Projects must follow prevailing wage and project labor agreement requirements, sign labor peace agreements, and meet a minimum equity standard. Each bid carries a fee of at least $10,000. Bidders must show experience bringing at least 100 MW of energy facilities to commercial readiness. Large utilities cannot bid.

The price check works like this. The procurement administrator sets a confidential benchmark. A bid above it is not cost-effective and cannot win. The IPA then sends its picks to the ICC for approval.

CRGA also plans ahead. The IPA must release a draft Energy Storage Resources Procurement Plan by June 1, 2027 and update it every two years. The law creates a statewide Integrated Resource Plan. It also lets the IPA explore a Storage for All program that pairs batteries with Illinois Solar for All projects, using up to 25 percent of that program's budget. Storage projects also get the state's clean energy siting approach, which advocates describe as aligning storage siting and permitting with wind and solar.

Key terms

Ameren angle

Projects had to connect in MISO Zone 4 or the PJM ComEd area. The ICC staff explained that this keeps Ameren Illinois customers paying for reliability inside Ameren's own territory, and ComEd customers paying for reliability inside ComEd's. S8

Module 5 of 11

How the first auction worked

You will be able to describe who could bid, how winners were picked, and why Ameren's region finished above its target.

Plain-language script

Think of a sealed-bid auction with a hidden price ceiling.

Developers bid a price for a 20-year contract. NERA compares each bid to the confidential benchmark, then picks the lowest-priced qualifying bids until each region's target is full.

Eligibility in round one was narrow. Standalone batteries, at least 20 MW, four hours of duration, connected to MISO Zone 4 or PJM's ComEd area. Projects had to be in a grid operator's waiting line or well into development. Commercial operation was due by December 31, 2029.

The targets were soft. If the last project that fit would push a region over target, and skipping it would leave the target unfilled, the rules allowed going over. That is how Ameren's region ended at 520 MW against a 450 MW target.

The ICC approved the results on September 1, 2026 and contracts were awarded. The public notice names the winners and their towns. It gives one average winning price for the auction and no price for each project.

Module 6 of 11

How the 20-year contract pays

You will be able to explain the strike price, the two reference prices, and who pays whom when markets move.

Plain-language script

This is the hardest module and the most important one. Take it slowly.

A developer bids a strike price, in dollars per megawatt-hour. Think of it as the price that lets the project pay for itself. The contract then compares that strike price to a reference price built from the market. The reference price has two parts. One is a theoretical energy profit: what a four-hour battery could earn by charging in the four cheapest hours of a day and discharging in the four priciest. The other is a theoretical capacity payment: what the grid operator's capacity market would pay for dependable supply.

The gap between the strike price and the reference price moves the money. If market revenue is low, the gap is positive and the utility pays the developer the difference. If market revenue is high, the gap is negative and, in the ICC's model contract, the developer pays the difference back.

Notice who controls the battery. The developer owns it, runs it and sells into the market. The utility does not dispatch it. That is the main difference from a tolling contract, where the utility controls the battery and pays a fixed fee. A tolling contract can look like a lease on the utility's books, which can raise borrowing costs. That is one reason ICC staff recommended the ISC for round one.

Because the reference price is a formula, it is not the developer's actual trading profit. A developer who operates well can beat the formula. One who operates poorly can miss it. Payments also depend on the battery meeting minimum performance requirements. If it does not, payments can be suspended or ended. The IPA stays involved as a referee: under the model contract it can approve deadline extensions and mediate disputes.

Module 7 of 11

Round one results

You will be able to recite the round one numbers, name the four Ameren projects, and say what is known and unknown about the PJM shortfall.

Plain-language script

The state sought 1,038 MW and contracted 600 MW. The gap is almost all in northern Illinois.

Ameren's region, MISO Zone 4, sought 450 MW and got 520 MW from four projects. ComEd's region, PJM, sought 588 MW and got 80 MW from one project. The 438 MW shortfall sits almost entirely in ComEd's territory.

Keep one idea straight. These are contracts, not batteries. Nothing from this auction is operating yet. Developers have until the end of 2029, with extensions possible, to connect and start running. News coverage notes that 600 MW is more than four times the roughly 141 MW of utility-scale storage installed in Illinois today.

Why did PJM come up short? Nobody outside the process can say for certain. The public record names winners and does not name losing bidders, so we do not know whether developers bid and lost on price or did not bid at all. Experts quoted by Capitol News Illinois pointed to the rigid first-round rules and to a mismatch between the required operating date and where projects sat in PJM's slow interconnection line. Several projects were also aging out of that line around the time of the auction.

The same experts called the result a step forward and not a failure. They expect round two to tell us more, because those bids will come from developers who responded to the new law.

Module 8 of 11

What it means for Ameren Illinois and customers

You will be able to describe Ameren Illinois's role, how costs reach the bill, and why every cost figure so far is a projection.

Plain-language script

Ameren Illinois signs the contracts and collects the money. It does not own or run the batteries.

For the four MISO projects, Ameren Illinois is the buyer. Its job is administrative. It settles payments with the developers, checks the performance data they report, monitors collateral, and designs and runs the customer charge.

Collection happens through a rider, which is a line on the bill that recovers one kind of cost. ICC staff recommended a pass-through rider like the one ComEd uses for carbon mitigation credits. The utility sets charges from a forecast of contract costs, then reconciles each year against what was actually paid. Costs can be spread by usage in kilowatt-hours or by each customer's share of peak demand. The ICC report left that choice to the tariff case.

How big is the bill effect? Early public estimates were small. The IPA's 2024 refresh, reported by the ICC, put the average Ameren residential customer between about 19 cents and $1.69 a month, depending on program size (1 to 3 GW) and capacity prices. The debrief gave a preliminary rate of $0.00121 per kWh for the 520 MW, which is $1.21 for 1,000 kWh. Those are different calculations. Neither is an approved rate.

Gross and net are different numbers. The debrief cited up to about $1.6 billion in maximum contract value over 20 years and about $890 million in projected customer cost after market offsets. The offsets move with energy and capacity prices, so the net figure is a projection. A similar pattern showed up before the auction. The IPA estimated that all 3 GW would cost about $9.7 billion to develop and operate over 20 years, and about $1 billion to customers after revenue is credited back.

Module 9 of 11

Risks and open questions

You will be able to separate what the meeting established from what it only raised.

Plain-language script

Five risks come up again and again. For each one, know what is established and what is only a worry.

First, delivery. A contract does not guarantee a battery. Justin pointed to an estimated 60 percent failure rate for awarded utility-scale renewable contracts as a caution. That was an analogy and not a finding about these projects. Mike asked whether a developer could win a contract and then sell the project. Justin said that is possible. Nobody said a winner plans to sell.

Second, money. The cost depends on market prices for 20 years. High market revenue lowers customer support. Low revenue raises it.

Third, accreditation. Grid operators count only part of a battery's size as dependable capacity. The meeting cited about 74 percent for a four-hour battery in MISO and about 50 percent in PJM. At 74 percent, a 100 MW battery counts as 74 MW. If that percentage falls, capacity revenue falls and customer support rises. A 2025 Ameren comment to the ICC used about 44 percent in an illustration, so confirm the current method before quoting any percentage.

Fourth, place. Four of the five projects are in downstate towns. The meeting raised construction traffic, road damage, soil compaction on farmland, the amount of land used, reduced local control and whether host communities receive a fair benefit. One legislator has called repeatedly about a project near his farm.

Fifth, safety. The meeting raised battery fires and the readiness of rural and volunteer fire departments. Claims in the meeting that no fire standards or codes apply were not substantiated. Do not repeat them as fact.

Module 10 of 11

The bigger reliability picture

You will be able to place batteries inside the state's planning studies and name the open reliability worries.

Plain-language script

Batteries are one tool in a larger plan, and the plan is still being written.

Illinois agencies published a joint Resource Adequacy Study on December 15, 2025. It found resource adequacy challenges in both PJM and MISO that are likely to affect the cost and reliability of electricity for Illinois customers.

CRGA then created a statewide Integrated Resource Plan, the IRP. The ICC leads it, working with the IPA, the Illinois EPA and the Illinois Finance Authority. The first IRP is due in mid-November 2026. A related Resource Adequacy Mitigation Plan uses the same inputs. The law also orders a joint IPA and ICC study of whether Illinois should stay in its current grid operators, due December 1, 2026.

The IPA says it will use the round one results, the Resource Adequacy Study and the IRP to adjust future storage targets and the split between MISO and PJM.

In the debrief, reliability worries went beyond batteries. They covered imported gas generation, future nuclear plants, and winter fuel supply. Those are questions about whether resources get built and whether they can run when the weather is worst.
