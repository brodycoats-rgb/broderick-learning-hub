Neutral and effective at the same time

Nobody in this fight is neutral. The utility delivers power, so more load can broaden the base that pays for fixed costs, if the load is real and the contract is sound. Pretending otherwise is not credible. What does work is being open about the stake and disciplined about the scope.

1. Say the stake out loud

"We deliver power. Credible growth can help everyone. We also should not carry risk that belongs to someone else." Candor earns more trust than a claim of neutrality.

2. Be specific where it is ours, quiet where it is not

The lane system does this. A short list of things we will always speak on, and a long list we will leave to others.

3. Support the goal, then offer the mechanism

"We agree with the goal. Here is what makes it work in our territory." This lets a sponsor keep the objective while changing the tool.

4. Be the source people check with

System facts, how a tariff works, what MISO is doing, and what a hearing notice really says. Staff call the person who gets the details right.

5. Define terms, do not pick sides

On bring your own power, ask whether the supply is new, deliverable, in the right market area, available when needed, and verified. Those questions are neutral and they decide the outcome.

6. Never surprise anyone

Share concerns with sponsors and staff early, with backup wording, so a no never arrives as an ambush.

7. Point to the record

The ICC, MISO, FERC, and independent analysts carry more weight than a company claim. Use their work and cite the date.

8. Keep promises small and checked

"I can confirm we discussed the option. I cannot confirm an endorsement. I will take the language through review."

The apparent conflict is really one customer relationship at different stagesEconomic development wants projects to come. Cost protection wants friction before the utility spends. Both are the same customer at different points on the readiness ladder. Reasonable standards speed serious projects by separating them from speculative requests, so being strict early is a way of being welcoming later.

View two, the posture ladder

Not every issue needs the same volume. Pick a rung on purpose, and say which rung you are on.

Lead. We bring the language and the argument. Example topics are cost causation, financial assurance, and who sets the tariff.
Support with conditions. We agree with the goal and ask for specific changes. Example topics are the POWER Act, interruptible service, and reporting.
Inform. We supply facts and answer questions but take no position. Example topics are MISO and PJM differences and how readiness works.
Hold and watch. We keep an internal view and speak only if our service is touched. Example topics are water, local control, and labor.
Stay out. Clearly someone else's issue. Example topics are biometric privacy and foreign ownership.

View four, three futures

A package in veto session

Needs three fifths for an immediate effective date. Sponsors have talked about a vehicle bill with POWER Act language.

Signposts. A new amendment appears late, a hearing posts with short notice, labor and environmental groups both move.

Our move. Same day read of the language, clear fallback wording ready, calls to sponsors and staff.

It slides to January

A simple majority is enough in a lame duck session and the election is over.

Signposts. Leaders talk about timing, the working group stalls, labor holds out.

Our move. Use the extra weeks to line up Legal, the ICC contact, and Economic Development notes.

It fragments

No package. Narrow bills pass, or the ICC and DCEO act under existing authority, and MISO and FERC move on their own timelines.

Signposts. No vehicle by January, regulators open dockets.

Our move. Shift effort to the ICC and MISO, where the large load tariff is already moving.

Scenarios are planning aids and carry no probabilities. Betsy said the issue could move in veto session, in early January, or drag into spring.

Bar lengths illustrate how a pipeline narrows. They are not data. The seven measures come from the forecast discipline section of the working paper.

Project Chanel in Pontoon Beach shows the top of the funnel working. 500 MW of contracted first phase load and about $1 billion of expected investment, in an industrial area between two interstates, with the utility, developer, DCEO, and the Illinois economic development corporation coordinating early. The policy question is how to repeat that without carrying the risk of the projects that never arrive.

View six, nine tests for any proposal

Customer protection

Does it keep existing customers from paying for what they did not cause?

Legal authority

Does it respect the ICC, customer choice, and federal and regional authority?

Evidence

Is there a documented harm, and does the mechanism address it?

Proportionality

Does the burden fit the risk, or does it apply one size to everyone?

Implementability

Are dates, rulemaking, and enforcement workable?

Economic competitiveness

Does it help Illinois win credible projects?

Environmental integrity

Are water, air, and supply claims accurate and measured?

Durability

Will it hold up to challenge and change in the market?

Public credibility

Could we defend it at a town meeting?

View seven, support, modify, resist

Can credibly support

  • Project readiness standards
  • Cost causation and a data center class
  • Financial security
  • Transparent reporting
  • Workable local engagement
  • ICC led tariff review

Needs modification

  • Prescriptive renewable obligations
  • One size thresholds
  • Overlapping reports
  • Unrealistic implementation dates
  • Broad nondisclosure bans

Should be resisted

  • Major infrastructure without payment protection
  • Arbitrary tariff multipliers
  • State direction of FERC or RTO matters
  • Making the utility answer for customer owned backup generation

Recommended positions from the playbook. They are not a leadership approved platform. Seven baseline principles sit behind these lists, including welcome credible investment, protect existing customers, preserve financial assurance, preserve regulatory discipline, align with utility roles and customer choice, use regional solutions, and prefer workable performance based standards.

Two calculators

How much energy is a megawatt

Edit the numbers. The household figure is an assumption you can change.

0 MWh a year
0 households' worth of yearly electricity

Energy equals MW times load factor times 8,760 hours. The default of about 10,800 kWh is a rounded US household average and varies by home and by state. This is arithmetic and not a rate or revenue estimate.

Exposure after protections

Hypothetical dollars, in millions. Each offset is counted once and must be collectible.

Remaining exposure

Remaining exposure equals the larger of zero or committed cost minus paid contributions, reuse recovery, and collectible security. It leaves out timing, financing, and legal priority. A learning model and not a tariff or damages calculation.
