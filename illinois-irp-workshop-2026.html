The central question: what resources will Illinois need to keep power reliable as demand grows and CEJA changes the existing plant fleet?

Core ideaIllinois may produce plenty of electricity over a year and still be short of dependable capacity in the hardest hours. The workshop modeled two futures to see what that means for existing gas plants. The results are preliminary and make no decision on any plant.

1. The two scenarios, side by side

Key takeawayThe model runs Illinois twice and changes one major rule: whether CEJA-affected plants leave on the modeled current-policy schedule or can stay available longer under interim emissions limits. Everything else is held the same so the difference is visible.

Reference Case

  • Existing CEJA units follow current-policy retirement assumptions, with identified reliability and ownership updates.
  • Model selects more new firm resources and out-of-state capacity.
  • Renewables and storage built substantially.
  • About 99.9% clean in-state generation in 2045 (deck measure).
  • Zone 4 reliance on imports rises over time.

CEJA Extension Case

  • Units stay available through the horizon while meeting interim emissions reductions.
  • Existing plants provide some firm MW, so fewer replacements are selected.
  • Renewables and storage still built; the buildout is not removed.
  • About 92% clean in-state generation in 2045 (same measure).
  • Zone 4 reliance on imports still rises over time.
Backup-power analogy. A generator that runs only a few hours a year can still be valuable if those are the hours when the main supply falls short. That is why the model can keep a gas plant available, run it infrequently and avoid building some replacement capacity. This illustrates the model result. It is not a conclusion about a particular plant.

If Illinois stays with the Reference Case

The cleaner generation mix comes with a practical delivery challenge. Replacement capacity, storage, transmission, contracts and imports must be ready when existing units leave. For the Ameren Illinois zone, the central test is whether dependable MW can serve customers in the tight hours, not merely whether Illinois produces enough annual electricity statewide.

If Illinois allows extensions

Existing units could provide a bridge while new resources develop. The model selects fewer new resources, suggesting a possible system-cost advantage, but the preliminary slides do not establish customer bill savings or a plant-specific business case. Maintenance, fuel, emissions, legal compliance and environmental justice impacts remain. Zone 4 still needs imports and new resources.

Decision questionFor a specific plant and a specific number of years, is its dependable capacity more useful than the alternatives once customer costs and local impacts are fully counted?
Do not blur these. The separate PJM request to keep identified 2030 units online through 2031 is not the broad CEJA Extension Case. Neither scenario itself grants Venice an extension. Source: official workshop slides 9, 13, 17-23, 28 and 38, linked in Reference.

3. What the model varied

The load cases test reference, low and high data center demand, and faster electrification. Policy cases test CEJA unit extensions, a clean energy standard, data center flexibility during the 100 highest peak hours, and greater demand response. Other cases test faster or cheaper advanced technology, more import capability and specific interregional lines.

The workshop showed results for five cases: reference, CEJA extension, low data center, high data center and electrification. The other cases remained part of the wider study. The SOO Green and Grain Belt cases add those lines as no-cost resources in the model, so their results cannot be treated as full real-world project economics.

4. Zone 4 is the Ameren-area story

In the statewide reference case, nameplate capacity more than doubles by 2045. The model adds substantial renewables and storage, with firm contributions from sources including new nuclear later on and capacity purchases from outside Illinois. The slides report 89% of electricity generated within Illinois is clean in 2030, rising to 99.9% in 2045 under this specific measure.

The Ameren-area MISO Local Resource Zone 4 results are more revealing than the statewide headline. Its model increasingly relies on imported capacity over time and is no longer a net energy exporter after 2040. A state can export more MWh over a year and still need dependable MW at a particular zone and peak hour.

Key takeawayAnnual energy and zonal peak capacity are different questions. Statewide net export does not prove Zone 4 meets every peak hour.

5. Load futures change the scale

The gap in statewide installed capacity between low and high data center cases is about 25 GW in 2035 and 45 GW in 2045. These are scenario differences, not committed projects. Higher load selects more nuclear or long-duration storage, depending in part on the shape of demand. The CEJA extension case needs the fewest new resources through 2045 among the cases compared. Existing units reduce the model’s need for out-of-state capacity, new nuclear and long-duration storage.

A temporary bridge may draw support across interests only if the reliability need and community protections are credible.

Original plain-language debrief script (from the source page)308 words · about 123 sec spoken

The state held its fifth workshop on the resource adequacy mitigation plan and integrated resource plan. In plain language, they are trying to figure out what Illinois needs to keep the lights on as demand grows and existing plants retire under CEJA.

The modeling says we will need a lot more renewable energy and storage. But it also makes a distinction that matters for our work. Producing enough electricity over the course of a year is not the same as having enough dependable capacity during the hours when the system is stressed. The need for that capacity grows considerably if data center demand comes in at the high end of the forecast.

They looked at a case that follows today’s CEJA retirement assumptions and another that tests what happens if affected plants can remain available longer while meeting emissions limits. In that second case, existing plants provided reliability value even though they ran relatively infrequently. The model then needed fewer new resources, including less new nuclear, long-duration storage, and capacity from outside Illinois.

That is relevant to our gas plant work, but I want to be careful about what it means. The presenters did not make a recommendation about Venice or another individual Ameren plant. They also discussed a separate set of PJM plants with 2030 retirement dates that PJM asked to keep online through 2031. That does not automatically apply to Venice, which is in MISO.

The question now is what can realistically address the near-term reliability risk before major new resources are ready. We should look closely at the draft mitigation plan, especially the assumptions for the Ameren Illinois zone, the availability of imports, and how the agencies compare the cost and local impacts of keeping existing capacity available. Comments on this workshop are due October 8, and the IRP report is scheduled for November 16.

Skeptical stakeholder Q&A drill

Answer out loud, then open the card. Items in [brackets] need clearance before use.

Did the workshop say Venice should stay open?

No. Neither scenario grants Venice an extension, and the presenters made no Venice-specific recommendation. The CEJA Extension Case is a modeled policy scenario. [Confirm any Ameren position with Regulatory and Legal.]

Doesn’t PJM keeping units online prove the concept?

The PJM request covers identified PJM units with 2030 dates, about 5.7 GW, asked to stay through 2031. Slide 38 names them. It does not list Venice and does not automatically apply to MISO.

If Illinois exports power, why worry about Zone 4?

Annual energy and peak capacity are different. In the model Zone 4 relies more on imported capacity over time and is no longer a net energy exporter after 2040 in the reference case.

So extending the plants saves ratepayers money?

The model selected fewer new resources, which suggests a possible system-cost advantage. The preliminary slides do not establish customer bill savings or a plant-specific business case. Fuller cost and EJ work will accompany the IRP.

Why keep a plant that barely runs?

Availability in tight hours can matter even with low annual generation. Maintenance, fuel, emissions and compliance costs remain, and local communities may bear impacts.

Can we just import the power?

The model treated out-of-state gas CTs as an economical firm option, with limits based on zonal capability. The test is how the capacity is purchased and delivered during regional scarcity. ComEd’s Andrew questioned the practicality before 2030.

Won’t data center flexibility solve it?

The scenario removes data center demand in the 100 highest peak hours but does not say how. The test is enforceable customer arrangements, their emissions and actual availability.

Why not build only clean resources?

The model still builds renewables and storage substantially in both cases. The question is dependable MW in tight hours while longer-lead resources develop.

Where is carbon capture in all this?

Primary cases used a strict CEJA eligibility treatment; a separate policy case tests CCS. The legal assumptions should be explicit.

What do you want from the agencies?

Targeted answers on Zone 4, plant assumptions, near-term options, costs and EJ analysis, by the October 8 comment deadline. [Confirm what Ameren Illinois will file.]

Presentation walk-through (full detail)

The official deck lists E3 presenters Charlotte Fagan, Vignesh Venugopal, Nathan Miller, Arne Olson, Kevin Steinberger and Laura Shi. The supplied meeting notes identify IPA’s Jim Roland as opening the session and Richelle Sampson as facilitating. Some names in the automated notes differ from the deck; speaker-specific attribution below is used only where reasonably supported.

Slides 2-5 · Opening · 1. Why the agencies convened the workshop

Jim Roland introduced the fifth joint workshop for the Resource Adequacy Mitigation Plan and Integrated Resource Plan. Richelle explained the agenda and comment process. The slides explicitly say the material is for discussion and is not an agency policy decision or legal interpretation.

Plain English: Stakeholders were being asked to test preliminary assumptions and results before the agencies complete their reports.

Slides 6-9 · PLEXOS and requirements · 2. How the planning model makes choices

Charlotte Fagan explained that PLEXOS chooses a least-cost portfolio within each scenario’s rules. It weighs investment, operating, fuel and other modeled costs while deciding what resources to build and how they operate. The portfolio must meet load, reliability needs, the renewable standard, CEJA emissions limits and transmission constraints. The reserve margin adds a buffer above peak demand. Accredited capacity is the portion of a resource counted as dependable. The reference case follows CEJA retirement assumptions from the 2025 study with targeted updates. In particular, PJM asked for identified units totaling about 5.7 GW with 2030 dates to stay through 2031 for reliability. Slide 38 names those PJM units.

Why it matters: A model can find a cheaper portfolio under an extension scenario, but the comparison depends on how it counts existing plant costs, emissions, capacity value and realistic construction dates.

Slides 10-15 · Scenario design · 3. What the model varied

The load cases test reference, low and high data center demand, and faster electrification. Policy cases test CEJA unit extensions, a clean energy standard, data center flexibility during the 100 highest peak hours, and greater demand response. Other cases test faster or cheaper advanced technology, more import capability and specific interregional lines. The workshop showed results for five cases: reference, CEJA extension, low data center, high data center and electrification. The other cases remained part of the wider study. The SOO Green and Grain Belt cases add those lines as no-cost resources in the model, so their results cannot be treated as full real-world project economics.

Slides 16-19 · Reference case · 4. What happens with current retirement assumptions

In the statewide reference case, nameplate capacity more than doubles by 2045. The model adds substantial renewables and storage, with firm contributions from sources including new nuclear later on and capacity purchases from outside Illinois. The slides report that 89% of electricity generated within Illinois is clean in 2030, rising to 99.9% in 2045 under this specific measure. The Ameren-area MISO Local Resource Zone 4 results are more revealing than the statewide headline. Its model increasingly relies on imported capacity over time and is no longer a net energy exporter after 2040. A state can export more MWh over a year and still need dependable MW at a particular zone and peak hour.

Slides 20-23 · CEJA extension case · 5. What changes if existing CEJA units stay available

This hypothetical case keeps CEJA-affected plants available through the modeling horizon while requiring interim emissions reductions. The state still needs major new investment, but the model selects fewer new resources than in the reference case. Existing gas plants provide a large share of firm capacity while running relatively infrequently. In-state generation is 92% clean by 2045 by the deck’s measure. In Zone 4, imports still become more important over time even with these units available. The case demonstrates possible capacity value; it does not prove that any individual CTG qualifies or that retaining it is the preferred outcome after customer cost, local emissions and legal review.

Slides 24-28 · Scenario comparison · 6. How different load futures change the scale

The gap in statewide installed capacity between low and high data center cases is about 25 GW in 2035 and 45 GW in 2045. These are scenario differences, not committed projects. Higher load selects more nuclear or long-duration storage, depending in part on the shape of demand. The CEJA extension case needs the fewest new resources through 2045 among the cases compared. Existing units reduce the model’s need for out-of-state capacity, new nuclear and long-duration storage.

Slides 29-33 · Conclusions and participation · 7. What the presenters wanted stakeholders to take away

The closing slides describe a persistent capacity challenge and say a reliable clean system needs both clean energy and firm capacity. The agencies urged near-term action that preserves options while longer-lead resources develop. They also highlighted who bears costs and receives benefits. Comments on this workshop are due October 8.

Plant-name distinction. Slide 38 lists individual PJM units associated with the 2030-to-2031 reliability requests, including Calumet, Rockford, Zion and University Park units. It does not list Venice or Ameren Missouri’s other Illinois CTGs as recipients of those requests. The broad CEJA extension case is a separate modeled policy scenario. Ameren Missouri’s published plan expects Venice to retire by January 1, 2030, and its other Illinois CTGs by January 1, 2040; that is background from Ameren’s planning materials, not a workshop finding.

Questions and answers from the discussion

These exchanges are paraphrased from the supplied meeting summary. The official deck describes the scenarios but does not transcribe this live Q&A.

How will Illinois meet needs before 2030?

Andrew from ComEd challenged the practicality of obtaining out-of-state capacity quickly. The team said it would release an updated 2027-2030 load and resource balance. Test: Compare proposed actions with actual lead times, contracts and delivery paths.

Could demand response and efficiency do more?

Scott Bernstein pressed for stronger demand-side solutions. The team said existing programs are included in forecasts and a high load flexibility case is being run. Test: How much dependable peak reduction can be enrolled before 2030, not merely assumed by 2045?

Why was carbon capture absent from the main cases?

The presenters described the strict CEJA eligibility treatment used in the primary cases and said a separate policy case would test CCS. Test: Make the legal assumptions and comparison with other state studies explicit.

Can the state depend on imports?

The team said import limits were based on zonal capability, while out-of-state gas CTs represented an economical firm-capacity option in the model. Test: Show how the capacity would be purchased and delivered during regional scarcity.

What is 100 hours of data center flexibility?

The model removes data center demand during the 100 highest system peak hours. It does not specify whether that happens through reduced computing load or on-site generation. Test: An enforceable customer arrangement, its emissions and its actual availability.

When will customer cost and environmental justice impacts be known?

The meeting summary says fuller cost and EJ work will accompany the IRP. Test: Assess bill impacts and local consequences before declaring an extension scenario preferable.
