Part 1. Orient

Executive Brief

Bottom line

The 2028-2031 MYIGP is Ameren Illinois’ integrated case for how distribution investments, operations, customer programs, data capabilities, and equity commitments should work together as the grid changes. It is both an engineering roadmap and a regulatory proof document.

The plan is built around safe, reliable, affordable, and equitable service while preparing the distribution system for greater electrification, distributed energy resources, large-load growth, changing customer expectations, and more complex system operations. The filing repeatedly uses data, benefit-cost analysis, affordability review, equitable-benefits analysis, and a new right-sizing reliability framework to explain not only what should be built, but why, when, and at what pace.

Ten things to know

  1. The MYIGP covers the electric distribution system. It is not a generation plan and does not replace MISO transmission or resource-adequacy processes.
  2. The filing period is 2028 through 2031, but many forecasts, asset decisions, and risk analyses look beyond that four-year window.
  3. The plan responds to the Public Utilities Act as amended by CEJA and reflects CRGA’s added emphasis on affordability, reliability, and customer benefits.
  4. The filing links traditional reliability work with grid modernization, data systems, DER integration, electrification, energy efficiency, demand response, innovation, and economic development.
  5. The filing treats affordability as a planning constraint and an analytical lens that shapes project choices.
  6. Cost-effectiveness asks whether expected benefits justify costs. It does not by itself answer who pays, when costs appear on bills, or whether benefits reach the same customers who bear the cost.
  7. Equitable-benefits analysis is designed to test whether a meaningful share of benefits reaches Equity Investment Eligible Communities and low-income customers.
  8. The right-sizing reliability framework is intended to connect spending pace and project selection to quantified system risk and customer outcomes.
  9. Large-load and economic-development growth can create both opportunity and pressure, especially where load timing, interconnection needs, cost responsibility, and stranded-investment risk are uncertain.
  10. Few dispute that the grid needs investment. The real debate is over whether each investment is proven, well timed, affordable, and fairly paid for.
Say it out loud

“This plan lays out what our distribution grid needs from 2028 through 2031. The real test is showing that each piece is needed, timed right, and affordable.”

Grid Plan 101: The Mental Model

What a multi-year integrated grid plan is

A MYIGP is a coordinated plan for the distribution system. Instead of reviewing reliability projects, technology platforms, DER readiness, customer programs, and equity commitments as isolated requests, the plan is intended to show how they form one portfolio and how that portfolio advances statutory objectives.

What it is not

  • Not a MISO generation interconnection queue study.
  • Not a wholesale-market capacity plan.
  • Not a transmission-only investment plan.
  • Not automatic approval of every forecasted project or dollar.
  • Not proof that every projected benefit will occur exactly as modeled.

The chain of logic

StepQuestionEvidence expected
1. Policy objectiveWhat public purpose must be served?Statutory objectives and Commission direction
2. System conditionWhat is changing or at risk?Asset condition, loading, outage, DER, climate, customer, and economic-development data
3. NeedWhat problem requires action?Identification of deficiency, risk, constraint, or opportunity
4. AlternativesIs wires investment the best tool?Non-wires alternatives, timing options, operational solutions, pilots, or deferral
5. Portfolio decisionWhy this project and this year?Prioritization, right-sizing, risk reduction, readiness, and dependencies
6. Customer valueWhat benefits result?Reliability, resilience, safety, avoided cost, access, service, and economic value
7. FairnessWho pays and who benefits?Affordability and equitable-benefits analysis
8. AccountabilityHow will results be checked?Metrics, project controls, reporting, and Commission oversight
Bottom line for a lobbyist

When a debate becomes overly technical, bring it back to the chain of logic: objective, condition, need, alternatives, timing, customer value, fairness, and accountability.

Part 2. Understand the Tests

Five tests decide whether a grid investment is persuasive: affordability, cost-effectiveness, equity, reliability and right-sizing, and the quality of evidence behind each claim. Large loads and non-wires alternatives are where those tests are pressed hardest.

Affordability: The Governing Constraint

The plan describes an affordability framework intended to evaluate how the investment portfolio affects customers, with particular attention to EIECs and low-income customers. The core challenge is that an investment can be prudent, reliable, equitable in location, and cost-effective over its life while still creating near-term bill pressure.

Four different affordability questions

QuestionWhy it matters
How much will bills change?Customers experience annual and monthly bills, not lifecycle net benefits
Who is most exposed?The same increase can be manageable for one household and destabilizing for another
When do costs and benefits occur?Capital costs may enter rates before all modeled benefits are realized
What else is on the bill?Distribution investment interacts with supply, transmission, riders, taxes, and broader economic conditions

Affordability challenge checklist

  • Is the investment mandatory, discretionary, or timing-dependent?
  • Can it be staged, scaled, deferred, or paired with external funding?
  • What is the incremental revenue requirement by year, not only total capital?
  • What portion is driven by replacement, growth, modernization, policy, or a specific customer class?
  • What happens if load growth, DER adoption, electrification, or federal funding differs from forecast?
  • What protections exist for customers least able to absorb increases?
Context from CUB (public, June 2026)

CUB says electric delivery costs alone are projected to rise nearly 4 percent a year, above the 3.4 percent annual inflation it cites, and that the plan is a 48 percent increase over the current grid plan. These are CUB’s claims. Expect legislators to hear them.

Say it out loud

“Customers depend on this grid work getting done. Our job is to prove it’s needed, pace it carefully, and show people what it means for their bill.”

Cost-Effectiveness and Equitable Benefits

What the cost-effectiveness framework is trying to answer

The basic question is whether expected benefits justify expected costs. Benefits can include avoided outages, reduced risk, operational savings, avoided or deferred infrastructure, customer savings, improved service, or other monetized value. The strength of the answer depends on assumptions, discounting, useful lives, avoided-cost values, probability estimates, and whether benefits overlap across categories.

Caution

A positive benefit-cost result is evidence, not a blank check. It should be read alongside affordability, uncertainty, distribution of benefits, project readiness, and alternative options.

What the filing reports on equitable benefits

For total Grid Plan investments, the filing states that estimated total benefits are approximately $8.64 billion, with approximately $4.08 billion expected to flow to EIECs and low-income customers, an anticipated share of 47 percent. This is a modeled allocation of benefits, not a guarantee of realized cash savings to individual customers.

TestGood question to ask
DefinitionWhat counts as a benefit and what does not?
AllocationHow is a shared system benefit assigned geographically or by customer group?
AdditionalityWould the benefit have occurred without the proposed investment?
TimingWhen is the benefit expected, and how sensitive is it to delay?
MeasurementCan actual outcomes be observed after the project is in service?
Avoiding double countAre the same benefits counted in more than one project or framework?
Bottom line for government relations

Equity lands best when it can be explained as service improvement, access, risk reduction, or savings that people can actually see in their community.

Reliability, Resilience, and Right-Sizing

The plan distinguishes work that sustains current performance, strengthens the system, and prepares for future risks. Right-sizing is meant to address a recurring regulatory concern: even where a category of work is legitimate, the utility must still demonstrate the amount, pace, and sequencing of investment.

LensQuestion
ConditionWhat does the asset or circuit data show today?
ConsequenceWhat happens if the asset fails or demand exceeds capability?
ProbabilityHow likely is that outcome during the planning horizon?
ExposureHow many customers, critical facilities, or communities are affected?
AlternativesCan operations, maintenance, DER, load management, or a smaller project reduce the risk?
TimingWhy 2028-2031 rather than later?
OutcomeWhich reliability or resilience metric should improve?

The filing’s right-sizing analysis uses quantified risk to compare investment and no-investment paths over time. It reports that annual system risk grows from approximately $572.9 million to more than $3 billion by 2050 under a no-investment assumption. That is a modeled risk trajectory and should be read with attention to the underlying probability, consequence, asset, and escalation assumptions.

Say it out loud

“Right-sizing is how we show we’re building the right amount, at the right pace, for the risk we actually face.”

Capacity Planning, Large Loads, and Economic Development

Capacity planning converts customer and technology forecasts into circuit, substation, and system needs. The political importance of this section is growing because data centers, manufacturers, fleet electrification, distributed resources, and new community development can change load quickly and unevenly.

OpportunityRisk to manage
Jobs, property tax, and supplier activityPublic claims may exceed enforceable commitments
New delivery revenueRevenue depends on actual load, timing, load factor, and tariff design
Improved asset utilizationLocalized upgrades may be needed before broad system value appears
Community investmentBenefits and construction impacts may not fall on the same communities
Flexible load or onsite resourcesOperational and market rules determine whether flexibility is dependable

The large-load decision tree

  1. Is the project real enough to include in the base forecast, or should it be modeled as a scenario?
  2. What facilities are required solely because of the project, and which upgrades provide broader system value?
  3. Who advances or guarantees the cost?
  4. What minimum-demand, security, collateral, or exit protections apply if the project is delayed, downsized, or canceled?
  5. Which costs belong in a customer-specific agreement or tariff, and which are common distribution investments?
  6. Could the project improve system utilization or revenue contribution, and under what assumptions?
  7. What is the consequence for existing customers if expected load does not materialize?
Bottom line for a lobbyist

Support growth, but insist on cost protection, credible milestones, transparent assumptions, and a clear explanation of which risks stay with the large customer rather than residential and small-business customers.

Non-Wires Alternatives, DER, and Data

Why non-wires alternatives matter

A non-wires alternative can be valuable when it solves a defined need at lower cost, avoids or delays construction, can be deployed in time, and performs reliably when the system needs it. Being nontraditional doesn’t make it the better choice on its own. Delivery certainty, persistence, dispatchability, customer participation, and measurement all matter.

Why data modernization is central

Advanced planning and operations require more granular customer, asset, outage, voltage, DER, and load information. Better data can improve forecasting, hosting-capacity analysis, investment targeting, situational awareness, and customer programs. It also raises governance questions involving cybersecurity, privacy, system integration, vendor dependence, and whether new capabilities produce measurable operating or customer value.

CapabilityPotential valueProof point
Granular load forecastingBetter timing and location of capacity investmentForecast error and avoided or deferred work
Advanced outage analyticsFaster restoration and targeted reliability workObserved outage and restoration outcomes
DER visibilitySafer integration and more accurate hosting capacityInterconnection time, curtailment, voltage, and capacity results
Customer analyticsBetter program targeting and supportParticipation, savings, burden reduction, and customer experience
Asset health analyticsRisk-based replacement instead of age-only replacementFailure rates, avoided consequences, and maintenance results

Investment Governance and Regulatory Proof

The filing describes tiered internal project governance, with escalating approvals for projects above $500,000, $10 million, $25 million, $50 million, and $100 million and different senior-leader and corporate review expectations at each level. Internal approval is important, but ICC review asks a different question. Is the investment prudent, useful, timely, supported, and fairly recovered from customers?

Internal governance asksRegulatory review adds

Are risks, schedule, and budget controlled?Are alternatives and timing sufficiently evaluated?
Has the proper executive body approved it?Is the cost reasonable and allocated appropriately?
Does it align with corporate strategy?Does it advance statutory objectives and customer benefits?

Evidence hierarchy

Regulatory lesson

The farther the evidence moves down the hierarchy, the more important staging, off-ramps, customer protections, and scenario analysis become.

  • Lead with customer outcome before technology or capital category.
  • Distinguish mandatory replacement from discretionary modernization.
  • State clearly when a number is a forecast, modeled benefit, or approved amount.
  • Do not imply that cost-effectiveness eliminates bill impact.

  • When a conversation drifts, bring it back to the chain of logic in Grid Plan 101.

Tough Questions and Recommended Responses

Each answer has three parts: what to say, what to know, and what to do if pressed. Question 11 is new.

1. Why should customers pay for this much grid investment?

What to say. Customers only pay for investments the ICC reviews and finds prudent and useful. This plan shows what each investment fixes, why the timing makes sense, what else we looked at, and how we worked to keep the bill impact in check.

What to know. The Commission decides what is recoverable, so avoid implying approval is assured. Expect follow-up on the annual revenue-requirement path, since that is the number critics will use.

If pressed. Offer to walk through one specific program and the condition data behind it, rather than defending the total dollar figure in the abstract.

2. Is this gold-plating?

What to say. The right-sizing framework was built to answer that question. It ties the amount and pace of spending to measured system risk, so we build what the data supports and hold back where it doesn’t.

What to know. Technology and data platforms draw the most gold-plating criticism because their benefits are harder to see. Know which capabilities have measurable proof points and which rest mostly on modeled value.

If pressed. Have one example ready, from the regulatory team, of work the plan staged, scaled back, or deferred. A concrete example of restraint lands better than a general assurance.

3. Will bills go up?

What to say. Grid investment does affect the delivery portion of the bill, and we’re upfront about that. The actual impact depends on what the ICC approves and when projects go into service, and we can walk through the year-by-year estimate.

What to know. Delivery is only one part of the bill. Supply, transmission, riders, and taxes move too, so keep distribution impacts separate from the total-bill changes constituents may be describing.

If pressed. Give the annual residential impact from the filing rather than the total capital figure, and offer to follow up in writing with the numbers by customer class.

4. Are low-income customers subsidizing modernization?

What to say. The plan includes a specific analysis of how benefits and costs reach low-income customers and equity communities. The filing estimates that about 47 percent of total modeled benefits flow to those customers, and it looks closely at how bill impacts land on those households.

What to know. The 47 percent figure is a modeled allocation of benefits and does not mean individual bill relief. Advocates will ask about actual bill burden and program access, so don’t lean on the percentage alone.

If pressed. Shift to tangible outcomes, such as reliability improvements on circuits serving equity communities, and offer to connect the office with the team that runs customer programs.

5. Why build for data centers before they arrive?

What to say. Work for a large customer is tied to real milestones and contract protections. Where facilities serve only that customer, the customer carries the cost and the risk to the fullest extent our tariffs and ICC policy allow.

What to know. Economic development announcements are not connected load. Know which large-load projects sit in the base forecast and which are scenarios, and where tariff protections may not fully cover exit risk.

If pressed. Explain the protections in plain terms, like collateral and minimum-demand commitments, and make clear that residential and small-business customers shouldn’t be left holding the cost.

6. Can batteries or demand response replace wires?

What to say. Sometimes, yes. The plan screens for non-wires alternatives, and where a battery, demand response, or another option can meet the need reliably and on time, it’s a real option.

What to know. Advocates may argue alternatives were screened too narrowly. Know the screening criteria and whether any alternatives were actually selected in this plan.

If pressed. Be honest about the tradeoff. A battery has to be in the right spot, last long enough, and perform on the hottest day, and sometimes a wires fix is the cheaper and surer answer.

7. Why not wait until equipment fails?

What to say. Waiting for equipment to fail usually costs customers more. Emergency replacements take longer, cost more, and mean longer outages. We use condition data to replace equipment before it fails, at a pace the evidence supports.

What to know. The fair challenge here is pace. Critics may accept planned replacement in principle but question whether the proposed volume is backed by condition and consequence data.

If pressed. Use a relatable example, like a substation serving a hospital or school, to show what an unplanned failure means for the people who depend on it.

8. How do we know benefits will happen?

What to say. The plan ties major investments to measurable indicators, sets baselines, and reports on results after deployment. The ICC keeps oversight the whole way through.

What to know. Some modeled benefits, such as avoided outage costs, are hard to observe directly. Know which benefits can be measured after the fact and which rely on estimates.

If pressed. Name the specific reliability metrics being tracked and explain that results come back before the ICC, so programs that underperform can be corrected.

9. What did the prior grid-plan case teach us?

What to say. We took the Commission’s feedback seriously. This plan does more to show why each investment is needed, why the timing works, and what customers get, including a new right-sizing framework built for that purpose.

What to know. On December 14, 2023, the ICC rejected the first 2024-2027 grid plan by a 4-1 vote. It found the plan fell short on affordability and did not show how 40 percent of benefits would reach low-income and environmental justice communities. Ameren refiled in March 2024 and deferred about $400 million in spending. On December 19, 2024, the ICC approved the refiled plan with modifications, allowing $83 million of the $333 million proposed. Be ready to point to where this filing responds to each of those findings.

If pressed. Ask the regulatory team for a short side-by-side of the ICC’s key findings and how this filing responds to each. It’s the most useful handout for this question.

10. What should legislators watch?

What to say. We’d suggest watching the year-by-year bill impact, how low-income customers are protected, who pays for large-load growth, how quickly projects move, outside funding, and whether results are reported after approval.

What to know. Each of these is a point where a legislator could push for statutory changes. Track which ones are gaining attention in committee and in stakeholder conversations.

If pressed. Offer a standing briefing as the case moves through the ICC. It keeps the office informed and positions us as a reliable source.

11. (New) A consumer advocate says hundreds of millions in this plan are questionable. What is our response?

What to say. The ICC will weigh every party’s position in the record. We’re glad to walk through any single item, what need it addresses, and the evidence behind it. [Draft for regulatory team review before use]

What to know. CUB’s public post names six items totaling $341.7 million. The September edition did not prepare readers for them. See the Critic’s Brief below. The portfolio was updated in the March 2026 supplemental filing, so confirm the current dollar amounts before quoting any figure.

If pressed. Do not argue CUB’s numbers from memory. Ask the regulatory team for an item-by-item response and point to the record rather than characterizing the advocate.

Affordability

  • What is the annual revenue-requirement path by customer class?
  • Which investments create near-term cost before long-term benefit?
  • What assumptions have the greatest influence on bill impact?
  • How are EIEC and low-income impacts measured beyond modeled benefit shares?

Reliability

  • Which asset classes drive the largest quantified risk?
  • How are probability and consequence calibrated?
  • How do proposed investments change outage frequency, duration, or restoration capability?
  • What investments remain justified under a low-load-growth case?

Large loads

  • What load is in the base forecast versus scenarios?
  • What contractual milestones must occur before construction starts?
  • What collateral, minimum-demand, and exit protections apply?
  • How are common-system and customer-specific costs separated?

Benefit-cost and equity

  • Which benefits are most difficult to monetize?
  • How is double counting prevented?
  • Which benefits are directly observable after deployment?
  • How will underperformance change future investment decisions?

Meeting Scripts

Opening a discussion with regulatory SMEs

Suggested script

“Thanks for walking us through the 2028-2031 Grid Plan. I’ve read through the structure. What I really need is what changed from the last plan, how you set the pace of spending, where customers feel it most, and where you expect pushback. Help us see the parts we’ll need to explain in plain English.”

Transition to affordability

Suggested script

“Before we get into project categories, can we stop on customer impact for a minute? I want to know when these costs show up on bills, who feels them most, and what options you looked at to pace or soften them.”

Transition to large loads

Suggested script

“On large loads, can you split out what’s signed from what’s forecast? Then walk us through what protects other customers if a project slips, shrinks, or never shows up.”

Closing summary

Suggested script

“Here’s where I land. Our strongest public case ties each major investment to a clear need, explains the timing, shows the bill impact, and names how we’ll measure results. Where do you think that story is weakest? Let’s start there.”

Deck A. Terms

MYIGP
Multi-Year Integrated Grid Plan: a coordinated plan for the distribution system, shown as one portfolio
Distribution grid
Poles, wires, substations, transformers, and controls that deliver power from the bulk grid to customers
Reliability vs. resilience
Reliability: how consistently service arrives (outage frequency and duration). Resilience: how well the system prepares for, withstands, and recovers from disruption
Hosting capacity
How much more DER a location can take without major upgrades
DER
Smaller resources on or near the distribution system: rooftop solar, batteries, some controllable loads
Non-wires alternative
A solution other than poles, wires, or substations that solves or defers a grid need
Right-sizing
Choosing the amount, pace, and timing of investment that matches demonstrated risk
Rate base
Utility investment recovered over time with depreciation and an authorized return, subject to approval
Revenue requirement
The annual amount a utility may collect to cover approved costs, taxes, depreciation, and return
Stranded investment
Infrastructure customers keep paying for even if expected demand never arrives
Cost causation
Assign costs, where feasible, to whoever causes them
EIEC
Equity Investment Eligible Community: an area prioritized to receive plan benefits
CEJA / CRGA
CEJA (2021) created the grid plan requirement. CRGA added emphasis on affordability, reliability, and customer benefits
Beneficial electrification
Replacing direct fossil-fuel use with electricity where it gives customer, system, or environmental value

Knowledge Check

Twenty concept questions and six scenario questions. Write your answers first, then use the answer key. Rate your confidence (low, medium, high) for each answer. The questions you got wrong with high confidence are the ones to rehearse first.

1. The MYIGP primarily covers which part of the electric system?

The MYIGP covers the distribution system. Generation, transmission, and wholesale markets are handled elsewhere.

2. Which concept asks whether spending pace matches demonstrated risk?

Right-sizing ties the amount and pace of spending to quantified system risk.

3. A positive benefit-cost result means:

A positive result is modeled evidence under stated assumptions. It does not remove bill impact or guarantee approval.

4. Which is the strongest large-load protection?

Enforceable milestones and financial commitments protect other customers if a project slips or is canceled.

5. A non-wires alternative is useful when it:

An alternative only works if it solves the defined need reliably and in time. Lower upfront cost alone is not enough.

6. Hosting capacity concerns:

Hosting capacity estimates how much DER a location can take without major upgrades.

7. Affordability analysis should focus on:

Customers feel annual bills, and the same increase affects households differently.

8. Equity analysis is strongest when tied to:

Modeled shares and spending locations matter, but measurable service and burden outcomes are what hold up.

9. Which evidence is generally strongest?

Observed data and documented condition sit at the top of the evidence hierarchy. The other options follow in order.

10. Cost causation asks:

Cost causation assigns costs, where feasible, to whoever creates the need.

11. Reliability is best described as:

Reliability is everyday consistency of service. Storm recovery and restoration speed are resilience measures.

12. Resilience is best described as:

Resilience covers preparing for, withstanding, and recovering from disruptive events. No system avoids all outages.

13. About what share of total modeled benefits does the plan report flowing to EIECs and low-income customers?

The filing estimates about 47 percent. The 40 percent figure is the benchmark the ICC cited when it rejected the first plan in 2023.

14. Why use scenarios for uncertain large loads?

Scenarios keep uncertain load from being treated as a firm need.

15. Internal project approval proves:

Internal approval shows corporate authorization. Prudence, compliance, and recovery are decided by the ICC.

16. What is the central regulatory proof question?

The ICC looks at the whole chain, not just one test like the benefit-cost ratio.

17. Which can lower stranded-investment risk?

Milestones, collateral, and staging limit what customers carry if load doesn’t show up.

18. What should accompany modeled benefits?

Baselines and post-deployment measurement show whether modeled benefits actually happen.

19. Why distinguish mandatory from discretionary work?

Separating mandatory from discretionary work shows where pacing and affordability choices exist.

20. The best public explanation of grid investment starts with:

Leading with customer outcome and demonstrated need keeps the story grounded and relatable.

21. SCENARIO. A legislative aide asks: “If it’s cost-effective, bills won’t go up, right?” Best reply:

Separate lifetime value from the annual bill, label the number as an estimate, and offer written follow-up. Never imply approval is assured.

22. SCENARIO. A press release promises a large campus in the territory. Treat it as:

Announcements are not connected load. They sit low on the evidence hierarchy, so staging and protections matter.

23. SCENARIO. An SME says a project’s benefit-cost ratio is above 1.0. Best next question:

A positive result is evidence, not a blank check. Measurement and double counting are two of the guide’s tests.

24. SCENARIO. A critic says a pilot has no firm cost estimate. Best first move:

The guide advises against defending totals in the abstract. Specific program, specific evidence.

25. SCENARIO. You plan to cite the 47 percent figure. What is its weakness?

The figure is modeled. Advocates will ask about bill burden and program access, so pair it with measurable outcomes.

26. SCENARIO. A legislator asks: “Is this gold-plating?” Best reply:

Concede the real weak spot (technology and data platforms), and offer a concrete example of restraint, as the guide advises.

A strong Grid Plan story is not “we need to modernize.” It is “here is the condition, here is the risk, here are the alternatives, here is why the timing and scale are right, here is the customer impact, and here is how we will measure the result.”
