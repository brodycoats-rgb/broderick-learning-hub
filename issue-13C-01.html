Grid reliability and plant closures

More on Reliability and retirements

What was said

Five plants are involved, and Venice is the closest worry. Speaker 1 said Venice is in an Environmental Justice zone, is a major producer, and has a target on its closure.

Baldwin shows how an extension can fail. Speaker 2 referred to talks with Ameren about exhausting every option before reversing course. The scenario was that the state decides it needs Baldwin for three more years, makes the call, and is told no. Speaker 2 also asked whether Doug Scott has any workable answer if the owner of Baldwin will not run it.

Imports are not a safe fallback. Speaker 3 said surrounding zones were not in a good position to supply power. Speaker 2 added that generators are reluctant to put capital into older assets, and raised blackout risk during stress events. A phrase in the notes, “X Gen”, is unclear.

About 3 GW could leave with no replacement. Speaker 2 worried that roughly three gigawatts of capacity are retiring without an immediate replacement. The distributed generation figures that followed are garbled in the notes.

The timeline is argued over. Speaker 2 debated whether coal shutdowns under CEJA fall in 2027, 2028 or 2030, with larger gas issues after 2030.

The public will not see it until it happens. Speaker 3 said people will not understand the crisis until the power goes out and bills rise at the same time. Speaker 2 wondered whether that could become a political crisis for state leaders.

Say it in plain words

Five plants are in play, and Venice, a gas peaking plant in an environmental justice area, is the nearest concern. Baldwin shows a different problem. Even if the state decides a plant is needed for three more years, the owner may simply say no, and nobody could name a way to force it. Neighbors cannot be counted on for imports, and generators do not want to spend on old units. The worry is that about three gigawatts retire with nothing dependable to take their place, and that the public will only notice when bills rise and the lights flicker.

Background General context I added. It was not said in the meeting.

Energy and capacity are different

Energy is how much power is produced over time. Capacity is how much can be counted on at the hardest moment. A grid can have plenty of energy and still run short at one hot evening. That is why the question is dependable capacity, not just installed megawatts.

Why a peaker can matter more than its hours suggest

A peaking plant may run only a few hours a year. Those can be the hours when everything else is stretched, so removing it can matter more than its yearly output implies. A strong case for keeping one rests on documented emergency use and what happens without it.

Terms on this page

Capacity Background
How much power can be counted on at the hardest moment. It is different from energy, which is how much is produced over time.
Accredited capacity Background
The share of a resource's rated size that planners count on during stress. It differs by resource type and market rules.
CEJA Background
Illinois's Climate and Equitable Jobs Act. It sets retirement requirements for certain fossil units, including some in environmental justice communities.
Peaking plant From the meetings
A plant built to run during short periods of very high demand or when other units or lines are out.
Environmental justice community From the meetings
A community given special attention under CEJA. Venice is in one.
Black start From the meetings
The ability to restart a generator with no power from the outside grid, which helps rebuild the system after a major blackout.
