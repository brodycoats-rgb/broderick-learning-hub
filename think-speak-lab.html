Think and Speak Lab

The leaders I admire most do three things well. They synthesize a messy situation, formulate a clear idea, and articulate it so people move. This lab builds those three skills, and the steadiness to use them when the pressure is on.

Everything here is practice. Speak out loud, use the timer, and let the filler-word counter show your progress over time.

Steady under pressure

Nerves are not a flaw. They are adrenaline, the same chemistry that sharpens athletes before a game. The goal is not to make them disappear. It is to keep them from taking over, and to recover fast when they spike.

Before

Script the first 30 seconds

Nerves peak at the start. Know your opening word for word so your mouth is moving before your mind catches up. The rest can be bullet points.

Rehearse out loud three times

Silent review does not count. Saying it out loud builds the path your brain will use under stress.

Call it excitement

Research by Alison Wood Brooks at Harvard found that people who told themselves "I am excited" before speaking performed better than people who tried to calm down. The racing heart is the same. The label changes how you use it.

Arrive early and own the room

Stand where you will speak, test the setup, and greet people as they come in. Talking to someone before you start lowers the stakes of the first sentence.

In the moment

The physiological sigh

Two short inhales through the nose, then one long, slow exhale through the mouth. A 2023 Stanford study found a few minutes of this cyclic sighing lowered stress and improved mood more than mindfulness meditation. One or two sighs work in a hallway.

Plant your feet and slow down

Feel both feet on the floor. Speak about 20 percent slower than feels natural. It will sound normal to the room.

Find one friendly face

Talk to one person who is nodding, then move to another. A room is easier one face at a time.

Use the pause

Silence feels long to you and thoughtful to them. A two-second pause before answering signals confidence, not panic.

Breathing pacer

Cyclic sighing. Follow the circle for one to five minutes.

Ready

If your mind goes blank

Have one of these ready. Each buys you a few seconds and sounds composed.

  • "Let me think about that for a second."
  • "The short answer is…"
  • "Let me come at that from a different angle."
  • "There are really two parts to that. The first is…"
  • "I want to make sure I answer the question you're actually asking. Do you mean…?"
  • "I don't know that number offhand. I'll get it to you today."

The exposure ladder

Confidence comes from reps at rising stakes. Climb one rung at a time and check it off.

Many executives work with a speaking coach. If nerves regularly shut you down, a coach or a counselor who works on performance anxiety can help a lot, and it is common at senior levels. Toastmasters is a low-cost place to get weekly reps.

Skill 1: Synthesize

Synthesis is finding the one thing that matters in a pile of facts, and saying it first. A good rule is the answer first, then no more than three supporting points, in the order the listener needs them.

Impromptu frameworks

When you get a question you did not expect, pick a frame in the first two seconds and let it carry you.

PREP

Point, Reason, Example, Point

The all-purpose answer. State your view, give one reason, prove it with an example, restate the point.

"We should require deposits for large loads. They protect families from paying for projects that never get built. One utility's pipeline is larger than its record peak. So deposits are basic customer protection."

WSN

What, So what, Now what

Best for updates and synthesis. What happened, why it matters, what we do next.

"The capacity auction cleared at the cap. That means supply costs stay high next year. Now we need to get members an explainer before constituents call."

PPF

Past, Present, Future

Best for "tell me about" questions and introductions.

"Ten years ago reliability was the top complaint. Today it's affordability. The next challenge is growth, and paying for it fairly."

PSB

Problem, Solution, Benefit

Best for pitching an idea.

"Members rarely get local facts. A quarterly district brief fixes that. It makes us the most trusted source in the building."

ABC

Acknowledge, Bridge, Communicate

Best for hostile or loaded questions. Acknowledge the concern, bridge to what matters, deliver your message.

"I understand why people are angry about their bills. What's driving most of the increase is supply. Here's what we're doing to help families right now."

Your progress

Hostile question simulator

Five people who will not make it easy. Answer out loud, then compare.

▸Legislator "Your company raised rates while families are struggling. Why should I believe anything you tell me?"

Approach. Acknowledge the frustration without arguing. Bridge to what drives bills. Offer something concrete for their district.

Model answer

"I understand, and you should hold us to the facts. Most of the recent increase is the supply side, set by the regional auction, and we pass that through without markup. What we control is delivery, and I'd like to show you what it's bought in your district. I can also set up an assistance enrollment event for your constituents."
▸Reporter "Isn't this data center push just a way for utilities to grow their profits?"

Approach. Do not repeat the loaded word. Bridge to customer protection.

Model answer

"Data centers bring jobs and tax base, and we want Illinois to win them. Our focus is making sure the projects pay for the grid they need, so families don't carry the risk. That's why we support deposits and collateral for large projects."
▸CEO "Why didn't we see this bill coming?"

Approach. Own it, give the facts, and say what changes.

Model answer

"You're right that we should have flagged it sooner. It was filed late and moved fast. Here's where it stands, here's our plan for the next 48 hours, and here's the change I'm making so late filings get a same-day review."
▸Advocate "Utilities say they care about low-income customers but shutoffs keep happening."

Approach. Agree on the shared goal and offer a specific next step.

Model answer

"We share the goal of keeping people connected. I'd like to understand which neighborhoods you're seeing it in, so we can target outreach for assistance programs there. Can we set up a working meeting?"
▸Panel "You've never managed a large team. Why should we trust you with ours?"

Approach. Acknowledge, then prove with evidence and a plan.

Model answer

"It's a fair question. I led a team of seven for seven years, and for the last several years I've led cross-functional groups and contract lobbyists without formal authority, which is harder. My first 90 days would be listening to every person on the team and setting clear coverage and priorities. I'm ready for it."

Learning from great communicators

A leader I learn from up close

Matt Tomc

Synthesize, formulate, articulate. He takes a messy set of facts, finds the one idea that matters, and says it so clearly that the room moves with him.

Use it in GR

  • Watch how he summarizes a discussion in one or two sentences before deciding
  • Notice how he frames an idea as a choice between options
  • Listen for how few words he uses
Drill. After your next meeting, write the one-sentence summary he would have given. Compare it to what you would have said.
Teacher

Jesus

Taught with short stories drawn from his listeners' daily lives, such as farming, fishing and lost coins. When asked "Who is my neighbor?" he answered with the parable of the Good Samaritan (Luke 10). He often answered a question with a question.

Use it in GR

  • A story about one person beats a statistic about a million
  • Use images from the listener's world, not yours
  • A good question can move someone more than an answer
Drill. Answer a member's question with a 30-second story about one customer in their district.
Investor and writer

Warren Buffett

Writes his annual shareholder letters in plain English and has said he imagines writing to his sisters, who are smart but not experts. He reports his own mistakes openly and early.

Use it in GR

  • Write for a smart person outside your field
  • Use a homespun analogy instead of jargon
  • Lead with your mistakes and you earn trust for everything else
Drill. Rewrite your last executive brief as if your smartest non-utility friend had to understand it.
President and speaker

Barack Obama

Known for deliberate pauses, a measured pace, and stating the other side's view fairly before making his own case. He drafted and edited his major speeches heavily.

Use it in GR

  • Slow down. Pauses carry authority
  • Steelman the opposing view first
  • Prepare hard so you can sound relaxed
Drill. Practice a 60-second answer with three deliberate two-second pauses.
CEO

Jack Welch

Pushed for candor, facing reality as it is, and simplicity. He argued that insecure managers create complexity, and that confident leaders make things simple.

Use it in GR

  • Say the hard thing plainly
  • Make it simpler than feels comfortable
  • Differentiate: say what matters most
Drill. Take a complex issue and say it in one sentence a ninth grader would understand.
Preacher and civil rights leader

Martin Luther King Jr.

Grounded arguments in values his audience already held, used repetition to build rhythm and memory, and painted concrete pictures. His deep preparation let him improvise. Part of his best-known 1963 speech was delivered off script.

Use it in GR

  • Start from shared values, not your position
  • Repeat a key phrase so it sticks
  • Preparation is what makes improvisation possible
Drill. Pick one phrase for your next talk and repeat it three times on purpose.
