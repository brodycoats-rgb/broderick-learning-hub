Venice follow up, October 14
Oct 13

About 3,000 MW of existing generation could retire without timely replacement. Participants asked whether renewables, batteries, distributed resources, and imports would show up fast enough. The worry is losing dependable capacity before equal reliability resources exist. The meeting tied this to higher capacity prices, more reliance on imports, possible rolling blackouts, and political heat on state leaders. These were scenarios, not predictions. Participant estimate

Oct 13

Venice is the nearest concern among five plants mentioned. It is a 562 MW gas peaking plant in Madison County. It sits in an environmental justice community, and the CEJA framework sets a January 1, 2030 retirement for certain covered units. No extension has been granted, and the meeting did not show that law automatically allows one.

Oct 13

Baldwin shows that an extension alone may not work. A speaker asked what happens if the state decides Baldwin is needed for three more years but the owner refuses to run it. Regulators may find a plant important and still face an owner with real operating, maintenance, and environmental costs. No way to force a plant to stay open was identified, and Doug Scott's role came up without detail.

Oct 13

The IEPA letter process is treated differently in PJM and MISO. Participants said existing law lets certain units keep running under defined reliability conditions. PJM took part in extension arrangements and some plants got more time. MISO was described as more reluctant, partly because many MISO utilities carry resource planning duties themselves. That matters most for plants in the Ameren Illinois territory. Neighboring regions may also be tight on supply, which limits how much Illinois can lean on imports.

Oct 13

Southern Illinois may have very few black start resources left. The transcript is unclear, so the number and location are not established.

Oct 13

Plant closures hit schools and local governments. Large plants are major local taxpayers, and closure or teardown can lower assessments. Jobs, contractors, and nearby businesses are affected too. The suggestion was a plant by plant economic profile that is kept apart from the reliability argument so each stands on its own evidence.

Oct 14

PJM's 2028 to 2029 auction came in 6,831 MW below the projected reliability requirement. PJM reported buying 138,318 MW of unforced capacity in its July 14, 2026 auction, with prices at the approved cap. That is a warning about future adequacy, not an announcement of blackouts.

Venice follow up, October 14

Oct 14

The group agreed it is time to engage the Venice community. An external affairs call on October 13 reached that view. The approach starts low key, with the election calendar in mind, and is meant to open with an investor involved in local economic development before it reaches municipal leaders. A short, non technical plan about community impact is due October 21. Participants also agreed to plan around the law as written today. They noted that several generators have spent years on closure plans and would likely close even if the law changed late.

Oct 14

The tax base is the human story. A Venice closure would hit local school funding, parks, and public safety. The plan is to explain the CEJA requirement, why Venice is covered, and why a timeline extension should be considered, including that it is a peaking plant and not a constant source of pollution. Participants noted that local officials may not yet understand the effect.

Oct 14

A Missouri planning call added a regional angle. On October 12, a presentation on resources and coal retirements led to a question about how Venice changes under the Illinois plan would affect the Missouri plan. The answer was that keeping the plant open saves Ameren Missouri customers a large amount by avoiding new construction or market purchases. The notes say hundreds of millions of dollars, which needs checking. A replacement inventory was said to be unavailable until at least 2035, and Venice was described as buffer supply for the MISO Zone 4 overlap. Participants reported that a maximum generation event on October 13 was handled with peaking plants. How power was split between Missouri and Illinois during that event is unknown.

Oct 14

A draft mitigation plan is expected October 15 or 16. The group will watch whether it hints at more time for Venice. One open question is whether the group can map potential blackout areas against legislative district offices. Estimates of people affected in a blackout, from about 300 to half a million, were called unproven.

Check before using
  • The Missouri savings figure and the 2035 inventory date.
  • The names mentioned for the outreach, and who is responsible for each step.
  • Whether the draft mitigation plan was released and what it says.

Say it in plain words

The biggest worry on October 13 was that about three thousand megawatts of existing generation could retire before anything dependable replaces it. That number is a participant estimate, so it has to be checked against real retirement notices and accredited capacity. Venice, a 562 megawatt gas peaking plant in Madison County, was the nearest concern. It sits in an environmental justice community, and the law sets a January 1, 2030 retirement for covered units. A peaker may run only a few hours a year, but those can be the hours that matter most. Baldwin raised a different problem. Even if the state decides a plant is needed, the owner may not want to keep running it. And in MISO, where Ameren Illinois operates, the grid operator was described as less willing to use the extension letters that PJM has used. On October 14 we added the PJM auction, which came in about 6,831 megawatts short of its reliability target. That is a warning, not a blackout forecast.

Go deeper, the ideas behind reliability
Energy and capacity are different things

Background

Energy is how much electricity is produced over time. Capacity is how much can be counted on at the hardest moment. A system can make enough energy over a year and still fall short on the coldest morning or hottest afternoon.

Why accredited capacity exists

Accredited capacity is the share of a resource's rated size that grid planners count on during stress. It differs by resource type and market rules. That is why 3,000 MW of retirements and 3,000 MW of new builds are not automatically a wash.

What a peaker is

A peaking plant is built to run during short periods of very high demand or when other units or lines are out. Judging it by yearly run hours alone misses its job.

What CEJA is

CEJA is Illinois's Climate and Equitable Jobs Act. It sets retirement requirements for certain fossil units, including some in environmental justice communities.

Questions worth asking in any briefing
  • Which retirement notices exist, and what are the exact dates?
  • What is the accredited value of each retiring unit and each replacement?
  • Does the owner want to keep operating, and what would that cost?
  • What does the emergency dispatch record show?
  • Which transmission limits would bind if the unit were gone?

Check before using

Check before using
  • The 3 GW figure, against retirement notices, accredited values, replacement plans, and IRP modeling.
  • Whether any Venice extension exists or is legally available.
  • How many black start resources Southern Illinois has, and where.
  • Doug Scott's role and any way to compel a plant to run.
  • Only Venice and Baldwin were named among the five plants.
