LIHEAP, PIPP, and data sharing

Payments tied to income, federal funds applied first, and talks about matching eligible customers.

More on LIHEAP and PIPP

What was said

Low income bills move toward a share of income. Under a Percentage of Income Payment Plan, or PIPP, a household's utility cost is capped at 3 percent of income for electric and 3 percent for gas, or 6 percent for a household with one utility.

Federal money is applied first. Utilities negotiated to credit federal LIHEAP funds to an account first, and only then work out what is left against the 6 percent cap. The aim was to avoid losing federal money that a lower starting bill could have caused.

Tariff filings are done, with one exception. All participating utilities have filed tariffs to carry this out except ComEd, which is on a separate regulatory track.

CEDA secured $5 million. CEDA, the Cook County administrator, secured $5 million in benefits for 2025 and 2026.

Utilities want automatic eligibility. They are pursuing categorical eligibility, where a household that qualifies for SNAP, for example, is treated as qualifying here too. Community action agencies resist because their administrative payments are tied to processing traditional paper applications.

A meeting with Mr. Workman is planned. It is meant to address data sharing barriers and find a way to reach eligible customers without relying on flat block funding.

Why it matters

My reading

Two quiet design choices decide whether this program helps people. One is the order in which credits are applied, which determines how much federal money gets used. The other is whether eligible households are found automatically or must apply on their own. The agencies that run intake have a financial stake in the second choice.

Say it in plain words

Low income customers are moving to a plan where the bill is a fixed share of income, three percent for electric and three percent for gas, or six percent if a home has only one utility. Utilities negotiated to apply federal LIHEAP money first, so that federal dollars get used and are not lost. All the utilities have filed their tariffs except ComEd. The next hurdle is finding eligible households. Utilities want people who already qualify for programs like SNAP to qualify automatically. Community action agencies worry about losing payments tied to paper applications. A meeting with the program administrator is planned to work through data sharing.

Why the order of credits matters

If the household share is figured first and federal money is applied second, a lower bill can shrink the federal claim. Applying federal money first uses the full grant and leaves less for other funds to cover. The second calculator below lets you test this with your own numbers.

Why intake is a money question

Agencies that are paid per processed application lose income when eligibility becomes automatic. A fair design either pays for outreach and support in another way or keeps a role for the agencies. That is the question the notes leave open.

Terms on this page

PIPP From the meetings
The Percentage of Income Payment Plan, which bases a qualifying customer's required payment on a share of income.
LIHEAP From the meetings
The Low Income Home Energy Assistance Program, a federally funded program that helps qualifying households with energy costs.
Categorical eligibility From the meetings
A shortcut that lets participation in one qualifying program, such as SNAP, help establish eligibility for another.
CEDA From the meetings
The Community and Economic Development Association of Cook County.
Community action agency Background
A local nonprofit that delivers anti poverty programs, often including energy assistance applications. CEDA is one.
SNAP Background
The Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program.
Block grant Background
A lump sum from Congress to a state, which writes program rules within federal limits. LIHEAP works this way.
