Executive Recap

Illinois is no longer debating data centers only as economic-development prospects. The issue now sits at the intersection of affordability, reliability, water use, land use, environmental review, local control, customer choice, and regional market rules. The strategic path is to welcome credible investment while making sure the projects that create new costs and risks are the ones responsible for them.

Recommended Ameren Illinois posture: Support responsible data-center growth, but insist on enforceable project-readiness standards, customer-specific cost responsibility, risk-based financial assurance, coordinated reporting, and a full ICC process for any large-load tariff. Avoid taking a position that sounds either anti-growth or overly promotional before the technical record is clear.

Core message: Illinois can compete for data-center investment without asking existing customers to underwrite speculative infrastructure.

Policy standard: Legislation should set clear principles, while the ICC and other proper agencies should handle technical implementation through evidence-based proceedings.

Negotiating posture: Support the objective when the mechanism is workable; seek modifications when the bill creates cost-shift, jurisdictional, timing, confidentiality, or customer-choice problems.

GR discipline: Lead with verified facts, identify what is known versus assumed, and always offer a constructive alternative rather than simply opposing a proposal.

For leadership and external conversations, the simplest frame is this: Illinois should be open for credible large-load investment, but not open-ended financial exposure. The strongest Ameren Illinois position is practical, customer-focused, and institutionally disciplined.

Executive Orientation

The issue in one sentence
Data centers have shifted from a specialized economic-development topic to a statewide affordability, reliability, water, land-use, privacy, and governance issue because proposed load is arriving faster and at a greater scale than traditional utility planning, local siting, and legislative processes were designed to manage.

Illinois should move toward a large-load framework that welcomes serious projects, protects existing customers, and keeps technical decisions with the agencies best positioned to make them. For Ameren Illinois, the practical position is clear: support project-readiness standards, customer-specific cost responsibility, risk-based financial assurance, coordinated reporting, and full ICC review of any tariff design. The company should resist proposals that move speculative risk to existing customers, ignore Illinois customer choice, duplicate agency authority, or impose statutory mandates that cannot be implemented through evidence.

National policy is moving toward separate large-load tariffs, minimum payment obligations, financial assurance, longer contract terms, exit protections, project-readiness screening, and more transparent environmental reporting.

Illinois entered the debate as an aggressive incentive state, but the Governor paused new incentive agreements beginning July 1, 2026 and called for rate, water, clean-energy, and transparency guardrails.

Illinois does not have a single settled data-center framework. The 2026 bills instead form a policy menu covering cost recovery, reporting, water, community impacts, clean-energy procurement, heat reuse, foreign ownership, and privacy.

For Ameren Illinois, the defensible position is responsible growth, correct cost allocation, protection from speculative-project risk, clear agency authority, and consistency with Illinois customer choice and regional market rules.

Your GR role is to translate across policy domains, separate verified SME positions from preliminary analysis, connect sponsor objectives to workable mechanisms, and keep the conversation constructive.

5. Illinois Tax Benefit and the Governor's Stance

Current program architecture

Qualifying projects must make at least $250 million in capital investment over the applicable 60-month period.

Projects must create at least 20 new full-time or equivalent operations or maintenance jobs, with compensation at or above 120% of the county average wage.

Projects must demonstrate carbon neutrality or qualifying green-building certification within the program requirements.

Certificates may provide exemptions for up to 20 years in renewable five-year increments.

Projects in qualifying underserved areas may receive a construction-employment tax credit equal to 20% of eligible construction wages.

7. Ameren Illinois Position: The Thin Line to Walk

Recommended posture
Support responsible data-center growth and the policy objectives of affordability, reliability, transparency, and environmental stewardship. Advocate for mechanisms that assign costs to the projects that create them, preserve ICC cost-of-service review, protect existing customers from speculative-project risk, and respect Illinois customer choice, regional market rules, and federal authority.

What Ameren Illinois can credibly support

What requires modification

What should be resisted

Project-readiness standards, cost causation, financial security, transparent reporting, workable local engagement, ICC-led tariff review.

Prescriptive renewable obligations, one-size thresholds, overlapping reporting, unrealistic implementation dates, broad NDA prohibitions.

Building major infrastructure without enforceable payment protection; arbitrary tariff multipliers; state direction of FERC/RTO matters; assigning the utility responsibility for customer-owned backup generation.

The central institutional distinction

Ameren Illinois is principally a delivery utility in a customer-choice state. That means a large customer may take distribution delivery service from Ameren Illinois while buying electric supply from a competitive provider. Any data-center legislation should keep those roles distinct by separating delivery infrastructure, competitive energy supply, regional transmission, and environmental requirements. Internal SME review of SB3830 and SB4016 flagged this structural issue, along with FERC, ARES, timing, and utility-role concerns.

7A. Utility Perspective: Defining a Defensible Ameren Illinois Path

Utility perspective in one sentence
The utility objective is not simply to connect large load or slow it down. It is to convert a proposed project into a credible, financeable, operable, and fairly allocated service commitment without transferring speculative, stranded-cost, reliability, or jurisdictional risk to existing customers.

Economic-development risk if Ameren Illinois gets the balance wrong

If economic development and regulatory teams use different assumptions, external stakeholders will receive inconsistent answers about timing, cost, and responsibility.

C. Large-load tariff perspective

A large-load tariff is a risk-allocation framework, not simply a higher electric rate. It should turn a proposed project into enforceable commitments that match the utility investments, reserved capacity, and system obligations created by the request.

Purpose

Benefit

Potential drawback

Eligibility threshold

Identify loads whose scale or characteristics create unusual planning risk.

Proportional treatment and administrative clarity.

A single MW threshold may miss aggregated campuses or capture lower-risk industrial loads.

Study and application fees

F. Best-practice positions Ameren Illinois could lead on

1. One verified load vocabulary: Require consistent reporting of inquiry, studied, contracted, energized, and operating MW. This would improve legislative, regulatory, and public understanding.

2. Project-readiness before major utility commitment: Support site control, paid studies, credible development milestones, and a realistic load ramp before material construction exposure begins.

3. Risk-based financial assurance: Lead on a transparent methodology that considers creditworthiness, project stage, customer-specific investment, asset reuse, and step-downs as risk declines.

4. Full ICC process for large-load tariffs: Support statutory customer-protection principles while preserving evidence, stakeholder participation, cost-of-service review, and updating authority at the Commission.

5. Technology-neutral large-load treatment: Where the risk is comparable, consider whether rules should apply to large loads generally rather than only to data centers. Preserve separate provisions only where an end-use characteristic creates a documented difference.

6. Coordinated state reporting: Advocate one reporting architecture with clear agency ownership, consistent definitions, and protections for trade secrets, customer information, and critical infrastructure.

7. Source-specific water policy: Support measurement and planning while distinguishing withdrawal from consumption and potable, reclaimed, surface, and groundwater sources.

8. Contracted flexibility, not rhetorical curtailment: Support voluntary or negotiated flexible-load products with explicit triggers, verification, compensation, and consequences.

9. Reuse and mitigation credits: Ensure exit obligations reflect actual unrecovered cost after reasonable mitigation and credit for facilities repurposed for other customers.

10. Regional and federal savings clauses: Make clear that state requirements do not override FERC, MISO, NERC, transmission-owner, or other applicable authority.

11. Predictable paths for mid-sized and hyperscale projects: Use graduated obligations so requirements rise with scale, infrastructure exposure, clustering, and environmental impact.

12. Public-benefit evidence: Support transparent reporting of investment, construction labor, permanent jobs, infrastructure payments, taxes, incentive value, and realized community commitments, with each measure reported on the correct timeline.

10. What Mainstream Discussion Often Misses

The denominator problem: Project counts are political shorthand, but MW by status and time horizon are more useful. Requested load is not operating load.

The campus-versus-building problem: One campus may contain many buildings. Counts can rise without representing distinct grid interconnections.

The speculative queue problem: A large request can change planning assumptions before a project has land, financing, tenants, permits, or a realistic ramp.

The reuse-value problem: Not every canceled-project asset is stranded. Some substations or lines may serve future customers; tariff design should credit reuse.

The electric-supply versus delivery problem: Illinois customer choice makes bundled-utility language especially risky.

The regional displacement problem: A project moving across a state line may still affect the same RTO market and regional prices.

The firmness problem: Nameplate MW, contract MW, peak MW, average MW, and firm MW are not interchangeable.

The water-source problem: Potable water, reclaimed water, groundwater, and surface water have different public impacts.

The backup-generation problem: Installed backup MW does not necessarily equal permitted, fueled, synchronized, or dispatchable grid support.

The tax-benefit timing problem: Upfront exemptions, construction activity, operating jobs, property taxes, and infrastructure costs occur on different timelines.

The cybersecurity and national-security problem: Large campuses are critical digital and energy infrastructure; transparency rules need security safeguards.

The workforce sequencing problem: Construction labor demand can be significant, but permanent staffing and local workforce pipelines should be analyzed separately.

The BIPA boundary problem: Physical access, hosted data, cloud processing, and tenant systems involve different legal facts.

The governance problem: Multiple agencies collecting similar water or energy information can create burden without producing a coherent public record.

The exit-ramp problem: Good legislation needs transition rules, cure periods, variances, grandfathering standards, and a process for projects already in development.

F. Difficult-question responses

Question

Response

Are data centers raising rates?

They can create costs and they can add revenue. The answer depends on the infrastructure required, tariff terms, contract commitments, market effects, and whether the project actually operates as forecast. The policy goal is to assign costs and risks correctly before investment occurs.

Why should data centers receive tax incentives?

That is now an accountability question. Illinois should compare verified investment, jobs, property tax, and community benefits against the exemption value and infrastructure impacts, then condition any future incentive on measurable performance.

Should data centers bring their own power?

The phrase captures a legitimate concern but is too imprecise for statute. We need to define whether the objective is new energy, accredited capacity, on-site generation, storage, flexible load, or full cost responsibility.

Why not curtail data centers first in an emergency?

Emergency operations are controlled through tariffs and reliability protocols involving utilities and regional operators. A flexible or interruptible service option can be designed, but the trigger, notice, compensation, and performance rules must be explicit.

Is BIPA driving data centers out of Illinois?

BIPA creates compliance and litigation considerations, especially for biometric access systems and hosted biometric information. Some stakeholders cite it as an investment concern, but project-location claims should be supported project by project rather than assumed.

Is Ameren trying to slow data centers?

The objective is to serve credible growth while protecting existing customers. Reasonable readiness standards and financial commitments can speed serious projects by separating them from speculative requests.

Why not let the legislature set the tariff?

The legislature should set the consumer-protection objective. The ICC is better positioned to test evidence, allocate costs, review contracts, and update technical terms through an open proceeding.

Lesson 1 of 9

Understand the large load

Start with what the project asks the power system to do, not the size of the announcement.

A data center houses computing equipment, networking and supporting systems. Electricity runs the servers, but also cooling, power conversion and other equipment. A campus may contain several buildings. That is why a count of buildings, campuses or service requests can describe different things.

The central government relations question is how to welcome credible investment while assigning the costs and risks it creates fairly. A project can bring construction activity, tax revenue and demand for utility service. It can also require infrastructure before its promised load arrives. Those benefits and obligations occur on different timelines.

Four distinctions that make you more precise

Power versus energy
MW measures power at a point in time. MWh measures energy over a period. A constant 50 MW load running for 24 hours uses 1,200 MWh. A 50 MW announcement alone does not tell you annual consumption.
IT load versus facility load
IT load describes computing equipment. Total facility load also includes supporting systems. Power usage effectiveness, or PUE, compares total facility energy with IT equipment energy over the same period and boundary. Lower PUE means less overhead, not necessarily lower total energy or water use.
Requested versus operating load
A service request is an input to planning. It is not proof that a facility is financed, built or consuming electricity. Several possible locations can describe the same prospective project.
Energy versus capacity
Energy is electricity produced or consumed over time. Capacity concerns the ability to meet demand under the applicable planning and market rules. Annual renewable purchases do not by themselves establish dependable power in every hour.

Worked example

Two campuses each announce 200 MW. One has site control, permits, signed service milestones and a phased construction plan. The other has a press release and several possible locations. Their headline size is equal; their planning certainty is not.

Lesson 3 of 9

Read the Illinois policy landscape

Learn the incentive framework, then read proposed changes without turning them into law.

Illinois established a data center incentive framework centered on investment and job commitments. The public debate also addresses infrastructure costs, water, energy procurement and community effects. That creates a useful learning frame: economic development and customer protection have to be evaluated together.

The current incentive baseline

The statutory framework includes $250 million of investment over 60 months, at least 20 qualifying new jobs, and compensation of at least 120 percent of the county average full-time wage as specified in the law. Environmental certification and other conditions also apply. This is a learning summary, not a complete eligibility checklist. Read 20 ILCS 605/605-1025.

DCEO states that it stopped processing applications beginning July 1, 2026, following the Governor's June 5 directive. The announcement says existing agreements will be honored. A processing pause is different from repealing the statute. DCEO's webpage also uses “median” where the statute uses “average”; use the legal text for the statutory requirement and confirm administrative application with the agency. DCEO program page · Governor's announcement.

How to read a proposal

  1. Identify the General Assembly and bill number. Numbers repeat across sessions.
  2. Read the version: introduced text, filed amendment, adopted amendment or enrolled bill.
  3. Check the action history. A filed amendment is not automatically operative.
  4. Extract the facility definition, threshold, covered activity, deadline and enforcing agency.
  5. Separate the sponsor's objective from the mechanism and identify an implementable alternative when needed.

Lesson 4 of 9

Understand Ameren large load tariffs

Follow a 100 MW project from service request to bill to changed plans.

A tariff is a filed schedule of rates and service rules. A rider adds provisions for a particular charge or service. A customer agreement records applicable project-specific commitments. Read them together: the name of a rate class alone will not tell you who pays for every facility or what happens after cancellation.

Start with the correct company and jurisdiction

Ameren Illinois DS-4 is Large General Delivery Service. Ameren Missouri has a distinct Large Load Customer Service rate within Service Classification 11(M). Illinois supply choice and Missouri's service framework differ. An 80 percent contract-demand floor described for Missouri must not be presented as an Illinois DS-4 requirement.

Verified DS-4 basics: eligibility generally starts at 1,000 kW of maximum monthly demand with demand metering, subject to qualification rules. Before January 2027, distribution billing demand is the higher of on-peak maximum kW or half the off-peak maximum kW. The posted tariff changes that basis to kVA in January 2027, with related power-factor provisions. Customers using company supply take it under HSS; alternative supply is also addressed. DS-4's minimum-bill provision is not Missouri's 80 percent contract-capacity floor. DS-4 sheets 14 through 14.003.

A simple demand example

Assume a 2026 billing period has an 80 MW on-peak maximum and a 100 MW off-peak maximum. Under the stated DS-4 distribution-demand comparison, use the higher of 80 MW or 50 percent of 100 MW: 80 MW, or 80,000 kW. Multiply that billing quantity by the applicable delivery rate to find that component. This is not the total bill; energy, other charges and actual service details remain separate.

Power factor describes how effectively apparent power supports real power. kW measures real power; kVA measures apparent power. That distinction matters when reading a future billing period, so always check both the effective date and the billing-period language.

Follow the hypothetical 100 MW project

  1. Request: Confirm the parcel, serving entity, proposed voltage, phased load and supply choice. A 100 MW announcement is not a guaranteed service date.
  2. Study: Identify required delivery and transmission work, who owns it and which costs are project-specific. Distinguish ordinary extensions from additional or special facilities.
  3. Commit: Read the actual tariffs and agreements governing contributions, security, construction authorization and changes. Do not assume one universal deposit percentage.
  4. Operate: Separate demand-based delivery charges from energy consumed and other applicable components. Compare measured load with the projected ramp.
  5. Change or cancel: Identify costs incurred, contractual obligations, prior payments and any relevant asset reuse. The answer comes from applicable provisions and agreements, not simply from the original megawatt announcement.

What Rider EFC illustrates

EFC addresses facilities the company agrees to provide beyond normal provision. It uses defined contribution and rental treatment depending on the facilities and costs; it is not a single universal data center surcharge. For a real project, identify the covered equipment and applicable agreement before estimating the obligation. Read EFC applicability and charges.

Missouri comparison

Missouri's posted Large Load Customer Service tariff applies to qualifying new facilities forecast at 75 MW or more and specified expansion loads. Its billing-demand rule includes an 80 percent contract-capacity minimum, and the tariff has separate provisions for ramp, commitments and creditworthiness. For a simplified 100 MW contract-capacity illustration, an 80 percent floor is 80 MW; it is not a statement that the customer consumes 80 MW at all times or pays 80 percent of a hypothetical total bill. Read all relevant exceptions and agreement provisions before applying it. Missouri tariff, sheets 61.7 onward.

The lobbyist's question

“What does the existing tariff already require, what risk remains, and which provision would this proposal change?” Ask regulatory staff to identify the exact sheet and explain the practical effect. Ask legal and commercial teams which obligations arise in the actual agreement.

Explain: Why can two customers with the same total energy use have different demand-related charges?

Lesson 5 of 9

Follow the money and the risk

Connect each protection to the exposure it is intended to address.

Cost causation asks which customer or activity creates a cost. It is the starting point for a fair allocation, not an automatic answer to every shared-network question. A dedicated connection and a broader upgrade may have different beneficiaries, reuse value and regulatory treatment.

The biggest risk often appears between the utility's spending decision and the customer's firm commitment. If a project is delayed, reduced or cancelled, facilities may be underused. Some assets can serve another customer; others cannot. Count residual exposure after appropriate reuse and recovery rather than labeling every dollar “stranded.”

A protection is not a slogan

“Pay your own way” still needs a cost definition and a recovery process. A deposit, minimum bill and exit charge can overlap. Review them together so the same exposure is not recovered twice. The enforceability of a mechanism depends on the tariff, contract and governing authority.

Worked example

Suppose a project causes $100 million of committed spending. For this simplified exercise, $20 million is already paid and $30 million is recoverable through asset reuse. That leaves $50 million before financial assurance. If collectible security covers $25 million of that same exposure, the remaining amount is $25 million. This is an illustration of exposure accounting, not a utility tariff or legal damages calculation.

Change the assumptions

Hypothetical millions of dollars. Treat the offsets as distinct and collectible; do not count the same recovery twice.

Remaining exposure: $25 million

Remaining exposure = maximum of zero or committed cost minus paid contributions, reuse recovery and collectible security. Excludes timing, financing, legal priority and other real-world factors.

Lesson 6 of 9

Connect reliability water and community impacts

Learn to ask a site-specific question rather than repeat a statewide generalization.

Reliability and clean energy are connected but different

A project may purchase renewable energy attributes for annual consumption while still relying on the grid during particular hours. Hourly matching, accredited capacity, transmission access and emergency performance are distinct questions. “Bring your own power” is incomplete until it specifies whether the objective is new energy, dependable capacity, on-site generation, flexible load or cost responsibility.

Backup generation is not automatically available grid support. Ask about fuel, permits, synchronization, control, operating limits and the service agreement. Similarly, a flexible workload is useful only if it can deliver the agreed reduction when needed.

Water questions should name the source and the measure

Withdrawal is water taken from a source. Consumption is the portion not returned for the relevant use and accounting boundary. Potable water, reclaimed water, groundwater and surface water can pose different constraints. Ask about peak seasonal demand, drought conditions, treatment and discharge as well as annual totals.

Community benefits need a timeline

Construction jobs, permanent jobs, property taxes, exemptions, road costs and emergency-service needs should be compared over their actual periods. An announced investment is not proof that every promised benefit has arrived. A practical local discussion identifies the obligation, responsible party, measurement and remedy.

Privacy is its own issue

Biometric access systems and other biometric activities can raise Illinois BIPA questions. The 2024 amendment changed specified damages treatment for repeated conduct; it did not erase the law's substantive duties. Do not infer that a project will leave Illinois because of BIPA without project-specific evidence. BIPA section 20.

Case to discuss

A developer offers “100 percent clean power” and “low water use.” Before endorsing the claim, ask: matched over what period, supplied under what arrangement, measured at which boundary, and compared with what baseline? Then ask what happens during an extreme-weather event.

Teach it back: Explain one reason why lower PUE does not settle a community's water concern.

Lesson 7 of 9

Compare models and build a position

Borrow a mechanism only after you understand the problem it solves.

Other jurisdictions offer useful examples, but their utility structures, statutes and tariffs differ. Compare the objective, eligible customers, cost definition, contract duration, enforcement and implementation venue before recommending an Illinois adaptation.

Ohio and Virginia as learning cases

AEP Ohio's data center tariff uses detailed minimum billing provisions, including a graduated contract-capacity calculation and other tests. It should not be summarized as every customer always paying for 85 percent of all contracted demand. The tariff is the source for applicability and billing details. AEP Ohio tariff reference.

The Virginia SCC describes Dominion's separate large-load class and long-term service and financial commitments. Treat this as a utility-specific regulatory example rather than a universal rule for every Virginia utility. SCC February 2026 fact sheet.

Regional and federal developments

FERC's June 18, 2026 orders directed six regional grid operators to justify or reform relevant large-load rules. The action opened proceedings; it did not instantly create one completed national tariff. Track the resulting orders and filings before treating a possible reform as an implemented requirement. FERC announcement.

Build a defensible recommendation

  1. Objective: State the customer or community outcome you want.
  2. Evidence: Identify the actual risk, cost or impact.
  3. Mechanism: Explain how the proposal changes that outcome.
  4. Authority: Identify who can establish and enforce the rule.
  5. Conditions: Name what must be confirmed before commitment.

Model recommendation

“I recommend tying the project's financial commitments to the costs incurred on its behalf, with credit for appropriate reuse and recovery, provided the applicable tariff and legal authority support the mechanism. The next question is what spending must occur before the first firm customer milestone.”

Stakeholder listening prompts

  • Sponsor: What outcome are you trying to guarantee for constituents?
  • Consumer advocate: What evidence would demonstrate that existing customers are protected?
  • Developer: What commitment can you make when infrastructure spending begins?
  • Local official: Which site-specific cost or impact is not yet addressed?
  • Technical expert: Which assumption would change your recommendation?

Apply: Explain why supporting a bill's objective can still require changing its mechanism.

Environmental and Ameren perspectives

Understand the interests, then distinguish a proposed approach from an official position.

The environmental position in the playbook

The original recommends responsible development with measurable environmental safeguards. It emphasizes water source and consumption, generator emissions, community effects and implementation by the appropriate authority. That is a proposed policy approach. It does not establish the formal position of every environmental organization.

From an environmental participant's perspective, useful questions include whether energy claims represent additional resources, whether water impacts are measured during constrained periods, and whether affected communities have enforceable protections. The playbook proposes supporting credible investment while protecting existing customers, matching commitments to project risk, preserving ICC review, respecting customer choice and maintaining clear agency responsibilities. Lesson 9 of 9

Contribute in a working group

Offer useful ideas without taking over the meeting or committing beyond your authority.

Prepare your contribution

Know whether the meeting is for learning, option development, negotiation or a decision. Bring one useful idea, one clarifying question and one material concern. Identify what you can explain, what you can explore and what requires internal confirmation. Define a useful outcome for the session, such as separating readiness requirements from financial security.

Timing and conduct

  • Listen until the purpose and question are clear. Contribute early enough to help shape the discussion.
  • Enter at a natural pause or use the chair's process. Do not interrupt an explanation to correct a minor detail.
  • Keep an initial contribution to about 30–60 seconds: idea, reason, question. These are practice targets, not rigid meeting rules.
  • Offer one point, then invite a response. Acknowledge the specific idea another participant contributed.
  • Raise material concerns before a decision closes. If the issue requires detail, request a follow-up rather than consuming the meeting.
  • Once your point is understood, stop. Repetition can crowd out the discussion you need to hear.
