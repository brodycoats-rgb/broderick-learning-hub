Lesson 1. Understand the large load

Key takeawayStart with what the project asks the power system to do, not the size of the announcement.
Retrieve first. Why is a project announcement different from a load forecast?

A data center houses computing equipment, networking and supporting systems. Electricity runs the servers but also cooling, power conversion and other equipment. A campus may contain several buildings, which is why a count of buildings, campuses or service requests can describe different things.

The central government relations question is how to welcome credible investment while assigning the costs and risks it creates fairly. A project can bring construction activity, tax revenue and demand for utility service. It can also require infrastructure before its promised load arrives, and those benefits and obligations occur on different timelines.

Power versus energy

  • MW measures power at a point in time. MWh measures energy over a period.
  • A constant 50 MW load running for 24 hours uses 1,200 MWh.
  • A 50 MW announcement alone does not tell you annual consumption.

IT load versus facility load

  • IT load is the computing equipment. Total facility load also includes supporting systems.
  • Power usage effectiveness (PUE) compares total facility energy with IT equipment energy over the same period and boundary.
  • Lower PUE means less overhead, not necessarily lower total energy or water use.

Requested versus operating load

  • A service request is an input to planning. It is not proof that a facility is financed, built or consuming electricity.
  • Several possible locations can describe the same prospective project.

Energy versus capacity

  • Energy is electricity produced or consumed over time.
  • Capacity concerns the ability to meet demand under the applicable planning and market rules.
  • Annual renewable purchases do not by themselves establish dependable power in every hour.
Worked example. Two campuses each announce 200 MW. One has site control, permits, signed service milestones and a phased construction plan. The other has a press release and several possible locations. Their headline size is equal; their planning certainty is not.

Your next question: What evidence supports the load ramp, and what investment is needed before each milestone?

Lesson 2. Know who decides what

Key takeawayA strong briefing sends each problem to the right decision maker.
Retrieve first. Name three Illinois bodies that could each touch a single data center project.

Illinois data center policy crosses economic development, utility regulation, environmental review and local land use. No single agency controls the whole project. Start by separating the requested decision: a retail rate, a procurement requirement, a permit, a local approval or a regional transmission question.

Delivery and supply are different

In Illinois, retail supply choices matter. The company delivering electricity and the entity supplying it may differ. A request that the delivery utility guarantee a customer’s clean energy procurement therefore raises a different question from funding its substation. Identify the actual supply arrangement and applicable tariff before making a commitment (ICC customer choice reference).

Try the distinction. A sponsor asks for a single law to set a deposit, require new generation and approve a water source. First separate the three outcomes. Then identify the authority, evidence and implementation process for each. One policy objective can require several coordinated decisions.

Teach it back: Explain why DCEO approval does not answer every electric service or permitting question.

Lesson 3. Read the Illinois policy landscape

Key takeawayLearn the incentive framework, then read proposed changes without turning them into law.
Retrieve first. A bill says "effective immediately." What still has to happen before that becomes a legal requirement?

Illinois established a data center incentive framework centered on investment and job commitments. The public debate also addresses infrastructure costs, water, energy procurement and community effects. That creates a useful frame: economic development and customer protection have to be evaluated together.

The current incentive baseline

The statutory framework includes $250 million of investment over 60 months, at least 20 qualifying new jobs, and compensation of at least 120 percent of the county average full-time wage as specified in the law. Environmental certification and other conditions also apply. This is a learning summary, not a complete eligibility checklist (20 ILCS 605/605-1025).

DCEO states that it stopped processing applications beginning July 1, 2026, following the Governor’s June 5 directive, and that existing agreements will be honored. A processing pause is different from repealing the statute. DCEO’s webpage also uses “median” where the statute uses “average”; use the legal text for the statutory requirement and confirm administrative application with the agency (DCEO program page · Governor’s announcement).

How to read a proposal

  • Identify the General Assembly and bill number. Numbers repeat across sessions.
  • Read the version: introduced text, filed amendment, adopted amendment or enrolled bill.
  • Check the action history. A filed amendment is not automatically operative.
  • Extract the facility definition, threshold, covered activity, deadline and enforcing agency.
  • Separate the sponsor’s objective from the mechanism and identify an implementable alternative when needed.

Lesson 4. Understand Ameren large load tariffs

Key takeawayA tariff is a filed schedule of rates and service rules; read the tariff, riders and agreement together. Do not carry a Missouri provision into Illinois.
Retrieve first. What is the difference between a tariff, a rider and a customer agreement?

A tariff is a filed schedule of rates and service rules. A rider adds provisions for a particular charge or service. A customer agreement records applicable project-specific commitments. Read them together: the name of a rate class alone will not tell you who pays for every facility or what happens after cancellation.

Start with the correct company and jurisdiction

Ameren Illinois DS-4 is Large General Delivery Service. Ameren Missouri has a distinct Large Load Customer Service rate within Service Classification 11(M). Illinois supply choice and Missouri’s service framework differ. An 80 percent contract-demand floor described for Missouri must not be presented as an Illinois DS-4 requirement.

The Illinois service documents to read together

DocumentWhat it helps answer
DS-4Who qualifies; delivery billing demand; metering, transformation and supply references.
PBR-R rate appendixThe applicable year's delivery prices by service characteristics. Use the current appendix, not an old example rate.
Rider HSS and Rider TSCompany hourly supply and applicable transmission treatment; DS-4 also describes the alternative supplier arrangement.
Rider EFCRecovery for agreed facilities outside normal provision, including defined installation and ongoing costs.
Standards and Qualifications; Customer Terms and ConditionsApplicable extensions, service conditions, billing and other obligations. Follow the Illinois links on the rates page.

Links: DS-4 · Illinois business rates page (PBR-R appendix, Standards and Qualifications, Customer Terms and Conditions) · Rider EFC.

Verified DS-4 basics: eligibility generally starts at 1,000 kW of maximum monthly demand with demand metering, subject to qualification rules. Before January 2027, distribution billing demand is the higher of on-peak maximum kW or half the off-peak maximum kW. The posted tariff changes that basis to kVA in January 2027, with related power-factor provisions. Customers using company supply take it under HSS; alternative supply is also addressed. DS-4’s minimum-bill provision is not Missouri’s 80 percent contract-capacity floor (DS-4 sheets 14 through 14.003).

A simple demand example. Assume a 2026 billing period has an 80 MW on-peak maximum and a 100 MW off-peak maximum. Under the stated DS-4 distribution-demand comparison, use the higher of 80 MW or 50 percent of 100 MW: 80 MW, or 80,000 kW. Multiply that billing quantity by the applicable delivery rate to find that component. This is not the total bill; energy, other charges and actual service details remain separate.

Power factor describes how effectively apparent power supports real power. kW measures real power; kVA measures apparent power. That distinction matters when reading a future billing period, so always check both the effective date and the billing-period language.

Follow the hypothetical 100 MW project

RequestConfirm the parcel, serving entity, proposed voltage, phased load and supply choice. A 100 MW announcement is not a guaranteed service date.
StudyIdentify required delivery and transmission work, who owns it and which costs are project-specific. Distinguish ordinary extensions from additional or special facilities.
CommitRead the actual tariffs and agreements governing contributions, security, construction authorization and changes. Do not assume one universal deposit percentage.
OperateSeparate demand-based delivery charges from energy consumed and other applicable components. Compare measured load with the projected ramp.
Change or cancelIdentify costs incurred, contractual obligations, prior payments and any relevant asset reuse. The answer comes from applicable provisions and agreements, not the original megawatt announcement.

What Rider EFC illustrates

EFC addresses facilities the company agrees to provide beyond normal provision. It uses defined contribution and rental treatment depending on the facilities and costs; it is not a single universal data center surcharge. For a real project, identify the covered equipment and applicable agreement before estimating the obligation (EFC applicability and charges).

Missouri comparison

Missouri’s posted Large Load Customer Service tariff applies to qualifying new facilities forecast at 75 MW or more and specified expansion loads. Its billing-demand rule includes an 80 percent contract-capacity minimum, and the tariff has separate provisions for ramp, commitments and creditworthiness. For a simplified 100 MW contract-capacity illustration, an 80 percent floor is 80 MW; it is not a statement that the customer consumes 80 MW at all times or pays 80 percent of a hypothetical total bill. Read all relevant exceptions and agreement provisions before applying it (Missouri tariff, sheets 61.7 onward).

The lobbyist’s question: “What does the existing tariff already require, what risk remains, and which provision would this proposal change?” Ask regulatory staff to identify the exact sheet and explain the practical effect. Ask legal and commercial teams which obligations arise in the actual agreement.

Explain: Why can two customers with the same total energy use have different demand-related charges?

Lesson 5. Follow the money and the risk

Key takeawayMatch each protection to the exposure it addresses. A protection is not a slogan: define the cost, the trigger and the recovery.
Retrieve first. If a project is delayed or canceled, who is exposed between the utility's spending decision and the customer's firm commitment?

Cost causation asks which customer or activity creates a cost. It is the starting point for a fair allocation, not an automatic answer to every shared-network question. A dedicated connection and a broader upgrade may have different beneficiaries, reuse value and regulatory treatment.

The biggest risk often appears between the utility’s spending decision and the customer’s firm commitment. If a project is delayed, reduced or cancelled, facilities may be underused. Some assets can serve another customer; others cannot. Count residual exposure after appropriate reuse and recovery rather than labeling every dollar “stranded.”

Match the tool to the risk

ToolPurposeQuestion that tests the design
Readiness milestonesTest whether a request is credible before escalating commitments.What proves site control, financing and a realistic ramp?
Customer contributionFund attributable facilities or costs under the applicable terms.Which costs are covered, and how are shared benefits treated?
Financial assuranceProvide collectible protection against a defined default or exposure.How much, from whom, for how long, and under what trigger?
Minimum billing commitmentSupport recovery when realized demand is below a contracted level.What demand definition, ramp and duration apply?
Exit obligationAddress eligible unrecovered costs after early termination.How do reuse value and prior collections reduce the obligation?
Flexible serviceOffer defined reductions in load when conditions require them.Are triggers, notice, performance and compensation workable?

A protection is not a slogan

“Pay your own way” still needs a cost definition and a recovery process. A deposit, minimum bill and exit charge can overlap. Review them together so the same exposure is not recovered twice. The enforceability of a mechanism depends on the tariff, contract and governing authority.

Worked example. Suppose a project causes $100 million of committed spending. For this simplified exercise, $20 million is already paid and $30 million is recoverable through asset reuse. That leaves $50 million before financial assurance. If collectible security covers $25 million of that same exposure, the remaining amount is $25 million. This is an illustration of exposure accounting, not a utility tariff or legal damages calculation.

Change the assumptions

Hypothetical millions of dollars. Treat the offsets as distinct and collectible; do not count the same recovery twice.

Remaining exposure: $25 million

Remaining exposure = maximum of zero or committed cost minus paid contributions, reuse recovery and collectible security. Excludes timing, financing, legal priority and other real-world factors.

Explain the mechanism: Why might a larger security requirement be appropriate at a later construction milestone than at the first inquiry?

Lesson 6. Connect reliability, water and community impacts

Key takeawayAsk a site-specific question rather than repeat a statewide generalization.
Retrieve first. Name one thing "bring your own power" could mean, and one it does not settle.

Reliability and clean energy are connected but different

A project may purchase renewable energy attributes for annual consumption while still relying on the grid during particular hours. Hourly matching, accredited capacity, transmission access and emergency performance are distinct questions. “Bring your own power” is incomplete until it specifies whether the objective is new energy, dependable capacity, on-site generation, flexible load or cost responsibility.

Backup generation is not automatically available grid support. Ask about fuel, permits, synchronization, control, operating limits and the service agreement. Similarly, a flexible workload is useful only if it can deliver the agreed reduction when needed.

Water questions should name the source and the measure

Withdrawal is water taken from a source. Consumption is the portion not returned for the relevant use and accounting boundary. Potable water, reclaimed water, groundwater and surface water can pose different constraints. Ask about peak seasonal demand, drought conditions, treatment and discharge as well as annual totals.

Community benefits need a timeline

Construction jobs, permanent jobs, property taxes, exemptions, road costs and emergency-service needs should be compared over their actual periods. An announced investment is not proof that every promised benefit has arrived. A practical local discussion identifies the obligation, responsible party, measurement and remedy.

Privacy is its own issue

Biometric access systems and other biometric activities can raise Illinois BIPA questions. The 2024 amendment changed specified damages treatment for repeated conduct; it did not erase the law’s substantive duties. Do not infer that a project will leave Illinois because of BIPA without project-specific evidence (BIPA section 20).

Case to discuss. A developer offers “100 percent clean power” and “low water use.” Before endorsing the claim, ask: matched over what period, supplied under what arrangement, measured at which boundary, and compared with what baseline? Then ask what happens during an extreme-weather event.

Teach it back: Explain one reason why lower PUE does not settle a community’s water concern.

Lesson 7. Compare models and build a position

Key takeawayBorrow a mechanism only after you understand the problem it solves.
Retrieve first. What would you need to know about another state before recommending its tariff for Illinois?

Other jurisdictions offer useful examples, but their utility structures, statutes and tariffs differ. Compare the objective, eligible customers, cost definition, contract duration, enforcement and implementation venue before recommending an Illinois adaptation.

Ohio and Virginia as learning cases

AEP Ohio’s data center tariff uses detailed minimum billing provisions, including a graduated contract-capacity calculation and other tests. It should not be summarized as every customer always paying for 85 percent of all contracted demand. The tariff is the source for applicability and billing details (AEP Ohio tariff reference).

The Virginia SCC describes Dominion’s separate large-load class and long-term service and financial commitments. Treat this as a utility-specific regulatory example rather than a universal rule for every Virginia utility (SCC February 2026 fact sheet).

Regional and federal developments

FERC’s June 18, 2026 orders directed six regional grid operators to justify or reform relevant large-load rules. The action opened proceedings; it did not instantly create one completed national tariff. Track the resulting orders and filings before treating a possible reform as an implemented requirement (FERC announcement).

ObjectiveState the customer or community outcome you want.
EvidenceIdentify the actual risk, cost or impact.
MechanismExplain how the proposal changes that outcome.
AuthorityIdentify who can establish and enforce the rule.
ConditionsName what must be confirmed before commitment.
Model recommendation. “I recommend tying the project’s financial commitments to the costs incurred on its behalf, with credit for appropriate reuse and recovery, provided the applicable tariff and legal authority support the mechanism. The next question is what spending must occur before the first firm customer milestone.”

Stakeholder listening prompts

  • Sponsor: What outcome are you trying to guarantee for constituents?
  • Consumer advocate: What evidence would demonstrate that existing customers are protected?
  • Developer: What commitment can you make when infrastructure spending begins?
  • Local official: Which site-specific cost or impact is not yet addressed?
  • Technical expert: Which assumption would change your recommendation?

Apply: Explain why supporting a bill’s objective can still require changing its mechanism.

Lesson 8. Environmental and Ameren perspectives

Key takeawayUnderstand the interests, then distinguish a proposed approach from an official position.
Retrieve first. What is the difference between a verified fact, a proposed position and an official advocacy position?

The environmental position in the playbook

The original recommends responsible development with measurable environmental safeguards. It emphasizes water source and consumption, generator emissions, community effects and implementation by the appropriate authority. That is a proposed policy approach. It does not establish the formal position of every environmental organization.

From an environmental participant’s perspective, useful questions include whether energy claims represent additional resources, whether water impacts are measured during constrained periods, and whether affected communities have enforceable protections. Listen for differences among organizations rather than assuming they share one demand.

The Ameren Illinois perspective in the playbook

The playbook proposes supporting credible investment while protecting existing customers, matching commitments to project risk, preserving ICC review, respecting customer choice and maintaining clear agency responsibilities. Those are recommended positions for evaluation, not a representation of a leadership-approved company platform.

Lesson 9. Contribute in a working group

Key takeawayOffer useful ideas without taking over the meeting or committing beyond your authority.
Retrieve first. What can you explain, what can you explore, and what needs internal confirmation before your next meeting?

Prepare your contribution

Know whether the meeting is for learning, option development, negotiation or a decision. Bring one useful idea, one clarifying question and one material concern. Identify what you can explain, what you can explore and what requires internal confirmation. Define a useful outcome for the session, such as separating readiness requirements from financial security.

Timing and conduct

  • Listen until the purpose and question are clear. Contribute early enough to help shape the discussion.
  • Enter at a natural pause or use the chair’s process. Do not interrupt an explanation to correct a minor detail.
  • Keep an initial contribution to about 30 to 60 seconds: idea, reason, question. These are practice targets, not rigid meeting rules.
  • Offer one point, then invite a response. Acknowledge the specific idea another participant contributed.
  • Raise material concerns before a decision closes. If the issue requires detail, request a follow-up rather than consuming the meeting.
  • Once your point is understood, stop. Repetition can crowd out the discussion you need to hear.

Practice three moments

Scenario 1: you are pressed for endorsement

A participant asks, “Can we tell the chair Ameren supports this?”

Try: “I can confirm we discussed the option, but I cannot confirm endorsement. Let me take the specific language through the appropriate review.”

Evaluate: Did you distinguish discussion from approval?

Scenario 2: an environmental participant challenges your idea

They say a phased approach could postpone meaningful protections.

Try: “That is a useful concern. Which protection must be in place before site work starts? We may be able to separate early environmental requirements from later spending-related security.”

Evaluate: Did you address the concern rather than defend every part of your first idea?

Scenario 3: the group is about to decide with a missing fact

The proposed requirement assumes a Missouri tariff provision already applies in Illinois.

Try: “Before we close this, I want to separate the two jurisdictions. Could we record this as an option pending confirmation of the Illinois tariff, rather than an existing Illinois requirement?”

Evaluate: Did you surface the material issue in time and offer a practical next step?

After the meeting

Record what was agreed, what remains open, who owns the next step and what you personally promised. Separate an idea discussed from a decision made. A follow-up can say: “My takeaway is that we will compare a fixed requirement with a milestone-based approach. I will confirm the tariff considerations before our next discussion.”

Self-evaluation: Did I help the group understand or resolve something? Identify one specific contribution rather than counting how often you spoke.

The playbook in plain language

Appendix A in one breathThe playbook’s own summary: Illinois recruited data centers with incentives, AI-scale demand changed the questions, no comprehensive bill passed in spring 2026, and the Governor paused new incentive agreements beginning July 1. The credible path is not a blank check and not a blanket ban.
Retrieve first. From memory, list the five phases states moved through, from recruitment to federal and regional intervention.

What has become mainstream

  • A separate process for the largest loads.
  • Evidence of site control and credible ramp schedules.
  • Customer-funded studies and customer-specific facilities.
  • Minimum contract terms, minimum demand payments, collateral and exit fees.
  • Transparent water and energy information, with security and trade-secret protection.
  • State policy principles implemented through full commission proceedings.

What remains contested

  • Whether data centers should supply new generation or merely pay their full cost of service.
  • Whether renewable-energy requirements should be annual, hourly, regional or in-state.
  • Whether flexibility should be voluntary and compensated or mandatory in emergencies.
  • How shared transmission and generation costs should be allocated.
  • Whether statewide rules should preempt, supplement or preserve local siting authority.
  • Whether tax incentives remain justified once infrastructure and resource impacts are counted.

Ameren Illinois: what to support, modify, resist

Can credibly support

  • Project-readiness standards, cost causation, financial security, transparent reporting, workable local engagement, ICC-led tariff review.

Requires modification

  • Prescriptive renewable obligations, one-size thresholds, overlapping reporting, unrealistic implementation dates, broad NDA prohibitions.

Should be resisted

  • Building major infrastructure without enforceable payment protection; arbitrary tariff multipliers; state direction of FERC/RTO matters; assigning the utility responsibility for customer-owned backup generation.

2 minutes: sponsor or colleague conversation223 words · about 89 sec spoken

[Sponsor or colleague meeting.] I agree with the goal of protecting existing customers from costs created by a project that is delayed, downsized or canceled. The question is how to make that protection work in Illinois.

Here is the setting. Illinois recruited data centers with incentives. AI-scale requests changed the questions: who pays, how a project proves it is real, how reliability is protected and what happens if the load never arrives. A lot of bills were introduced this spring. No comprehensive framework passed. On June 5 the Governor paused new incentive agreements beginning July 1.

Three things I would test in any proposal. First, the harm: what exactly are we protecting against, and who bears it? Second, the mechanism: a deposit, a minimum bill and an exit charge can each cover a different exposure, but they have to fit together so the same cost is not recovered twice. Third, the authority: our utilities deliver power, many large customers buy supply competitively, and transmission is governed through regional and federal rules.

So my suggestion is that the statute set the customer-protection standard and direct the ICC to set tariff, assurance, minimum commitment and exit terms in a full proceeding. [Confirm company position before saying this externally.] Could we start with the specific harm you want to prevent, then compare mechanisms that reach it?

5 minutes: briefing outline305 words · about 122 sec spoken

[Five-minute briefing outline: internal or committee-style. Facts are from the playbook; confirm status and numbers before external use.]

1. Why now (30 seconds). Illinois built its policy around attracting investment. AI-scale projects, from tens to hundreds of megawatts and sometimes gigawatt campuses, raise separate questions about grid, supply, water, local impact and customer risk.

2. What we know and do not know (45 seconds). There is no single authoritative public count of Illinois data centers. Trackers differ: one lists 36 operating centers and 66 planned projects, another tracks 278 centers. Say which dataset and date. A requested megawatt is not an operating megawatt.

3. The state of play (45 seconds). The 2019 program offers exemptions for qualifying projects. Fourteen data center measures were tracked in the 2025-2026 General Assembly. None settled the issue. The Governor paused new agreements beginning July 1, 2026, and called for rate, water, clean-energy and transparency guardrails. The fall veto session is the next opportunity.

4. How other states handled it (45 seconds). Separate large-load tariffs, minimum payment, financial assurance, longer terms, exit protection and readiness screening. Illinois should borrow the problem each mechanism solves, not copy the number.

5. The proposed path (60 seconds). Welcome credible projects. Require site control, paid studies and a credible ramp before major utility construction. Match financial assurance and minimum commitment to the exposure. Credit reusable assets. Keep technical terms at the ICC. Keep delivery, supply, transmission and environmental obligations distinct. Preserve federal and regional authority. [Company position: confirm.]

6. The ask and the uncertainty (60 seconds). Say the action you recommend, why, the condition, and the next decision. Name one thing you still need to confirm, such as the current bill version or the Illinois tariff provision.

7. Close (15 seconds). Illinois should be open for credible large-load investment, but not open-ended financial exposure.

Skeptical Q&A drill

Answer out loud first, then open the model. Structure: acknowledge, verified fact, tradeoff, decision test. [Bracketed] items need clearance.

Are data centers raising rates?

They can create costs and they can add revenue. It depends on the infrastructure required, tariff terms, contract commitments, market effects and whether the project operates as forecast. The policy goal is to assign costs and risks correctly before investment occurs.

Why should data centers receive tax incentives?

That is now an accountability question. Illinois should compare verified investment, jobs, property tax and community benefits against the exemption value and infrastructure impacts, then condition any future incentive on measurable performance. The reported figure is more than $983 million for 27 data centers from 2020 through 2024, which is not the program lifetime cost.

Should data centers bring their own power?

The phrase captures a legitimate concern but is too imprecise for statute. We need to define whether the objective is new energy, accredited capacity, on-site generation, storage, flexible load or full cost responsibility.

Why not curtail data centers first in an emergency?

Emergency operations are controlled through tariffs and reliability protocols involving utilities and regional operators. A flexible or interruptible service option can be designed, but the trigger, notice, compensation and performance rules must be explicit.

Is BIPA driving data centers out of Illinois?

BIPA creates compliance and litigation considerations, especially for biometric access systems and hosted biometric information. Some stakeholders cite it as an investment concern, but location claims should be supported project by project rather than assumed.

Is Ameren trying to slow data centers? [clearance]

The objective is to serve credible growth while protecting existing customers. Reasonable readiness standards and financial commitments can speed serious projects by separating them from speculative requests.

Why not let the legislature set the tariff?

The legislature should set the consumer-protection objective. The ICC is better positioned to test evidence, allocate costs, review contracts and update technical terms through an open proceeding.

Can you tell the chair Ameren supports this?

I can confirm we discussed the option, but I cannot confirm endorsement. Let me take the specific language through the appropriate review.

Doesn't a deposit on every project just slow down investment?

Risk-based forms, step-downs and periodic review matter; a deposit that fits the exposure protects customers without tying up more developer capital than needed. Overly restrictive or unpredictable rules can send credible projects elsewhere, so the path should be predictable. [Company position: confirm.]

Do you know how many data centers Illinois has?

There is no single authoritative public count. Cleanview lists 36 operating centers and 66 planned projects; Aterio tracks 278; USDataMap lists 60 facilities. They define facilities differently, so I would name the dataset and date and focus on MW by status.

Load Explain to a new colleague why a 700 MW announcement is not 700 MW of operating demand.

Glossary

MW / MWhPower at one moment versus energy over time. 50 MW for 24 hours is 1,200 MWh.
IT loadPower used by computing equipment, as opposed to total facility load.
PUEPower usage effectiveness: total facility energy over IT energy, same boundary.
Large loadA customer whose scale or characteristics create unusual planning risk for the utility.
HyperscaleVery large campuses; no clear MW threshold in internal review of SB4016.
Load rampThe schedule by which a customer's demand grows after energization.
CapacityAbility to meet demand under planning and market rules; different from annual energy.
Nameplate / firm MWRated, contract, peak, average and firm MW are different measures.
Cost causationThe principle of assigning costs to the customer or activity that creates them.
Stranded costUnrecovered cost of facilities left underused; count only the residual after reuse and recovery.
Financial assuranceCollateral or deposits protecting against default or exposure.
Minimum billing demandA billing floor that recovers fixed costs when actual demand is below reserved capacity.
Exit chargeCharge to recover prudent unrecovered costs when a customer terminates early.
Capacity reassignmentReleasing or transferring unused reserved capacity to another use.
TariffA filed schedule of rates and service rules.
RiderAdded provisions for a particular charge or service attached to a tariff.
DS-4Ameren Illinois Large General Delivery Service.
Rider EFCRecovers agreed facilities outside normal provision; defined contribution and rental treatment.
ICCIllinois Commerce Commission; regulates relevant utility rates, tariffs and service.
IPAIllinois Power Agency; statutory power procurement and planning.
DCEODepartment of Commerce and Economic Opportunity; administers the incentive program.
IEPAIllinois Environmental Protection Agency; environmental permits.
ARESAlternative retail electric supplier; competitive supply under Illinois customer choice.
MISO / PJMRegional transmission organizations whose rules interact with state policy.
FERCFederal Energy Regulatory Commission; federal jurisdiction over wholesale and interstate transmission matters.
NERCNorth American Electric Reliability Corporation; reliability standards authority named in the savings clause.
Customer choiceIllinois retail framework in which delivery and supply may be provided by different entities.
BIPABiometric Information Privacy Act, with a private right of action.
AdditionalityWhether a clean-energy commitment adds a new resource rather than relabeling existing supply.
Hourly matchingMatching clean energy to consumption hour by hour rather than over a year.
Savings clauseBill language preserving federal, regional or agency authority.
Withdrawal / consumptionWater taken from a source versus the portion not returned.
Behind-the-meterGeneration on the customer's side of the meter (noted in the Wyoming model).
