Capacity, Retirements and the IRP

Why the supply picture is the most pressing issue

Why it matters

Illinois sits in two grid markets. The northern part is in PJM and the southern part is in MISO. Both are tightening at once. State clean energy law sets retirement dates for some plants, while demand from data centers is rising. If supply leaves faster than replacement arrives, prices rise first and reliability risk follows.

What the meetings said

  • Plants at risk. Several gas fueled peaking plants face retirement deadlines under the state clean energy law. One plant in an environmental justice zone was described as the most immediate concern.
  • Owner willingness. A recurring worry is that even if the state wants a plant to stay, the owner may not be willing to keep it running. One example described a plant that owners might not agree to keep operating for three more years.
  • The letter process. After session ended, the Illinois EPA issued a letter saying RTOs have independent authority under existing state law to extend the life of needed plants. PJM extended several facilities, including dual fuel units. MISO has been slower to join, taking the view that resource adequacy in vertically integrated markets is a state job.
  • Coal timelines. As summarized, one coal plant secured an extension through the letter process, remaining units at two plants are scheduled to close by the end of 2027, and two others received extensions through 2045. A university pilot is testing carbon capture at one of them. Verify these dates before quoting them.
  • IRP numbers. The state plan appears to count about 800 MW of upgrades at an existing nuclear fleet. PJM reportedly says most of those megawatts are already committed. Models also showed out of state imports growing three to four times over five to ten years.
  • Timing. The IRP and mitigation plan are expected the day before veto session, with no preview for working groups and no immediate legislative action expected.
  • PJM backstop. A backstop procurement is expected in roughly five to six months, with large cost allocations to big Illinois consumers.
  • Local impact. Retirements remove property tax base for towns and school districts, especially in southern Illinois. A summer max gen event on October 13 was cited as proof that peakers still matter.
  • Public understanding. One participant predicted the public will not feel this until prices rise and power is hard to get.

Worked example, the 800 MW question

Say a state plan counts 800 MW of new capacity from upgrades. The grid operator then says 700 MW of that is already promised to other buyers. The plan has double counted 700 MW, and the true new supply is only 100 MW.

That is why one number can turn a comfortable looking plan into a shortfall. The first question to ask of any plan is whether each megawatt is counted once.

Scripts

Thirty seconds

Illinois is losing firm generation faster than it is adding it. About three gigawatts are scheduled to leave without a clear replacement, and demand from data centers is climbing. Before anyone votes on anything, we should make sure every megawatt in the state plan is counted once and that the owners of key plants are actually willing to keep them running.

Two minutes

Let me give you the short version. Under current law, several plants have retirement dates. Some owners have already spent years planning those closures, so a late change in the law may not change what they do. PJM has used a letter process to extend plants it needs, and the Illinois EPA has said RTOs have that authority. MISO has been more cautious and says supply adequacy is a state matter.

The state plan is expected the day before veto session, which leaves almost no time to read it. One early concern is about 800 megawatts of upgrades that may be counted by the state while the grid operator says they are already committed elsewhere. If that holds, the gap is larger than the plan suggests.

The people who feel this first are local. Towns and school districts that depend on plant property taxes lose revenue when a plant closes. So my ask is simple. Let us look at the numbers together, count each megawatt once, and talk about timelines that protect reliability and local budgets.

Questions you may get

Why not just build new plants?

New supply takes years to permit, finance and connect. One participant said replacement inventory may not be fully built until about 2035. The near term question is how to bridge the gap.

Will a change in administration or law save the plants?

Not necessarily. Several owners have already executed closure plans and may retire units regardless. Plan on the law as written today.

Is this a utility problem?

It is a system problem that touches customers, local governments and the grid. A policy designed years ago now meets new demand, and the two need to be reconciled.

What should legislators ask for first?

The underlying data, with each megawatt counted once, and a clear view of who has authority to extend a plant and who pays.

Verify before using

  • Exact retirement dates and extension terms for each coal plant.
  • The 800 MW figure and which entity counted it.
  • The veto session date and the IRP release date.
  • The backstop procurement schedule and cost allocation method.
