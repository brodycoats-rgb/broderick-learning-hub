Enough power, and what it costs

Four lessons about supply. How the grid counts dependable power, what batteries can and cannot do, why a rarely used plant can matter, and how long range planning turns into headlines.

What this part is for

Part 1 was about getting power through the wires. Part 2 is about whether there will be enough power to send. The big idea is that a megawatt on a label and a megawatt you can count on in the hardest hour are different things. Once that clicks, the Venice debate, the battery numbers and the planning scenarios all start to make sense.

Reminder, the three reliability questions

Distribution

Can power reach the customer on local poles and wires? (Part 1)

Resource adequacy

Is there enough dependable supply when it is needed? (This part)

Transmission security

Can the high voltage system carry it where it is needed? (Parts 1 and 2)

4

Enough dependable power, and why megawatts differ

You will be able to separate three kinds of reliability, and explain nameplate versus accredited capacity with a worked example.

In plain words

A power plant's label tells you its best day. The grid operator asks what you can count on in the hardest hours. Those can be very different numbers, especially for solar and short duration batteries.

Full explanation

Three ideas that get blurred together

People say "reliability" and mean three different things. All three have to work at once. A failure in any one of them can leave customers in the dark.

Model 4.1

Where each kind of reliability lives

How to read itPower travels left to right, from where it is made to where it is used. Tap a button to light up one kind of reliability and see where a failure would sit.
What it showsThe same blackout can come from three different places, and each has a different fix. Poles and wires cannot fix a supply shortage. New plants cannot fix a fallen feeder.
So whatWhen a legislator says "reliability," ask which one. Naming the layer is the fastest way to get to the right solution.
IdeaQuestion it asksExample of a failure
Distribution reliabilityCan power get to the customer on local poles, wires and transformers?A fallen tree takes down a neighborhood feeder
Resource adequacyWill enough dependable generation, storage, imports and demand response exist when needed?A cold snap hits and there is not enough supply
Transmission securityCan the high voltage system carry power where it is needed within limits?Lines overload even though supply exists somewhere

Two pressures at once

Leaders said Illinois faces two pressures together. Dispatchable plants, which can be told to raise or lower output, are scheduled to retire under CEJA while new demand grows from data centers, manufacturing and electrification. Wind and solar depend on the weather. Batteries are controllable but limited by what is stored. Leaders also described a net swing of about 4 GW within two hours earlier that week as solar output and behind the meter solar changed. That is a lot for operators to balance in a short time.

What accreditation means

Accreditation is the amount of capacity the grid operator credits toward reliability. It is the nameplate rating times a credit factor. The credit reflects how much a resource can be counted on in the hours when the system is most at risk. The speakers gave rounded numbers, below. These are speaker reported assumptions, not official MISO factors.

ResourceCredit as described
NuclearAbout 93 percent
Natural gasAbout 85 percent
Battery storageAbout 64 to 65 percent now, possibly falling toward 50 percent as more are added
SolarAbout 40 to 50 percent in summer and 25 to 30 percent in winter now, possibly falling to about 5 percent by 2028 to 2029

A rate is not an amount

MW is how fast power flows, like a faucet. MWh is how much has flowed, like water in the tub. Set both and see the amount.

How to read itEnergy in MWh equals power in MW times hours. The same MWh can come from a big rate for a short time or a small rate for a long time.

So whatClean energy totals (MWh) and reliability contribution (accredited MW) answer different questions. Say which one you mean.

Common mix-ups

  • A 5 percent credit does not mean solar makes only 5 percent of its energy. It means it contributes little in the riskiest hours.
  • MW is a rate. MWh is an amount. A plant can produce a lot of clean MWh and still help little at the worst hour.
  • Accreditation is not a judgment about whether a resource is good. It is a measure of how it helps in one specific job.

Check before using outside

The solar decline to 5 percent should be matched to the exact E3 scenario and MISO method. The study year and season matter.

Say it

Full version, about 45 seconds

There is a difference between how much power a plant can make on its best day and how much the grid can count on in its worst hour. Grid planners give each resource a credit for that. A gas plant gets a high credit. Solar gets a lower and falling one, because the hardest hours often come when the sun is down. That is why adding megawatts and adding reliability are not the same thing.

Hallway version, about 30 seconds

A plant's label is its best day. The grid counts what it can rely on in the worst hour. A gas plant counts most of its label. Solar counts much less and falling, because the toughest hours are often after sunset. More megawatts is not always more reliability.
Legislator asksWe are adding thousands of megawatts. Why does anyone say we might be short?
You sayBecause the megawatts on the label are not the ones the grid can count on in the hardest hour. Planners credit each resource for what it provides then, and for some resources that credit is much lower. The mix matters as much as the total.

Batteries, duration and what they cost

You will be able to do the battery duration math, read the storage cost numbers correctly, and describe batteries and peakers as partners, not rivals.

In plain words

A four hour battery is like a four hour tank. It is great for moving power around the day. It cannot keep running for ten hours on that tank. A gas plant keeps going as long as it has fuel. They solve related but different problems.

Full explanation

The math first

Energy equals power times time. A 500 MW battery with four hours of storage holds about 2,000 MWh. It could give 500 MW for four hours, 250 MW for eight hours or about 83 MW for 24 hours. Those are ideal numbers before losses and operating limits.

Why duration matters in an emergency

In a July emergency, a peaker at Venice ran about ten hours straight. To cover 10 hours at 500 MW without recharging, you would need about three four hour battery blocks. The speakers also described a peaker in PJM running 26 days in a row, which shows the limit of short duration storage in a long emergency. Missouri, by contrast, uses batteries to cover the 4 to 5 PM drop in solar while gas plants ramp up, which is a job batteries do well. And batteries cannot recharge on a summer evening without extra solar built to charge them during the day.

Why battery credit can fall

If the riskiest hours were 6 to 9 PM, the first batteries cover that window well. Once the window is covered, the remaining risk moves to longer stretches, so each new battery adds less. That is why the accreditation table in lesson 4 shows battery credit possibly falling toward 50 percent.

Model 5.1

Each square is one 500 MW, four hour block. Teaching model only. It ignores charging, efficiency, cost and grid limits.

How to read itEvery square is one 2,000 MWh tank. A partly filled square is a tank you only need part of.
What it showsThe number of blocks grows in a straight line with hours. Ten hours takes three. A multi day event takes dozens.
So whatBatteries are excellent for hours. Long events are a different problem, which is why "batteries or gas" is the wrong framing.

The tank trade off

For a fixed tank of stored energy, the longer you want it to run, the less power it can give at once.

How to read itThe curve shows the power the tank can deliver for each run time. The dot is your pick. Moving right means longer and lower.
What it showsPower times hours stays the same. A battery rated for a big number of MW is not a promise of a long run.
So whatWhen someone quotes battery megawatts, the next question is always "for how many hours?"

This is a shape, not a forecast. The first batteries cover the evening peak. Later ones chase a longer, flatter need.

So what"Batteries have a high credit" is true for the first ones. Cost estimates that assume the credit holds can understate the gap, which is why the meeting said costs could be higher if credit falls.

The storage cost numbers, kept straight

  • The average strike price discussed was about $106 per MWh. That is a contract benchmark, not a household rate.
  • Offsets come from energy arbitrage, which means charging cheap and discharging when power is worth more, and from capacity market value.
  • Procurement results. Ameren's area got 520 MW against a target near 450 MW. ComEd's area got 80 MW against 588 MW, which the meeting attributed to PJM's interconnection queue.

The fair way to frame it

The honest question is not whether batteries are good or bad. It is how much storage the system needs, where, and what else must come with it for reliability at a reasonable cost.

Common mix-ups

  • Gross contract exposure is not what customers pay on day one.
  • Awarded megawatts are not operating megawatts.
  • A battery's megawatt rating says nothing about how long it can run.

Check before using outside

Contract settlement mechanics, the market offset assumptions, and whether four or five projects were awarded in Ameren's area, since the accounts differ. Many of these figures are company analysis.

Go deeper on the hub

Say it

Full version, about 45 seconds

Batteries are valuable, and they do some jobs better than anything else. But a four hour battery has a four hour tank. When a peaker plant has to run ten hours in an emergency, you would need about three battery blocks to match it, and in a multi day event many more. So the right question is not batteries versus gas. It is how much of each the system needs, and what the total bill looks like once you count the contracts honestly.

Hallway version, about 30 seconds

A four hour battery has a four hour tank. Great for the evening ramp. Not great when a plant has to run ten hours straight. We need both, and the real question is how much of each and what it costs.
Legislator asksDoesn't storage save customers money?
You sayIt can create value by storing cheaper power and delivering it when power costs more. The question is whether that value covers the contract cost. In the figures presented to us, the first 520 megawatts still left about 890 million dollars in modeled net cost over 20 years. We need transparent modeling and updated assumptions.

Venice, CEJA and the five plant question

You will be able to explain why a rarely used plant can matter, why a reliability finding does not change a law, and what a retirement means for a community.

In plain words

Venice is a fast starting gas plant near St. Louis that runs only some hours each year. Operators say it helped stabilize a recent emergency. State law schedules it to close. The honest question is what will do its job after that, and what legal path exists if the answer is nothing.

Full explanation

The plant and the numbers

Venice Energy Center is in Madison County in the Metro East area. It is described as a peaker with a rating of about 562 MW. Other figures in the meetings, about 500 to 526 MW, describe operating output, so keep rating and output apart. Under CEJA it faces an early retirement date because of its location in an Environmental Justice community, plus annual operating limits. The meetings gave the retirement date as 2030, and the first day's notes said the end of 2029. Those are consistent, since the plant would stop at the turn of the year, but confirm the exact statutory wording.

The July emergency

In the recent emergency, heavy west to east flows into St. Louis stressed regional lines. Operators started Venice to supply power locally. The account says that operators were also getting close to the plant's annual run limits, which is the tension in one picture. Reliability said run it. The law said it had a limited number of hours.

Environmental objective

Cut emissions and address impacts on communities that have carried a heavy pollution burden. The group described Venice as close to homes and in an Environmental Justice area.

Reliability objective

Keep enough fast, dependable capability to handle sudden shortages and transmission emergencies.

Model 6.1

The four gates before a retirement

Answer each question for a plant you are discussing. The model shows what posture the evidence supports. This is a thinking tool, not a legal opinion.

How to read itTurn each switch to Yes or No. The message below changes. Notice how many Yes answers it takes before a request sounds credible.
What it showsA credible position needs evidence of need, a lawful path, a replacement plan and a plan for the community. Missing any one weakens the whole.
So what"Keep the plant" is a weak ask. "Prove the replacement first, through a lawful path, with community effects planned" is a strong one that people can say yes to.

The legal distinction to remember

A study can find a need. It cannot by itself change a law. An IRP or a reliability finding may identify a risk, but extending a statutory retirement or relaxing a run limit needs actual legal authority, whether in the statute, in an agency process or in new legislation. A credible Venice plan names that authority, the evidence, and the environmental conditions that would come with any extension.

What retirement actually involves

It is more than turning the plant off. Retirement can involve notice to the grid operator, reliability review, regulatory compliance, decisions about the equipment and the site, employee planning, fuel and interconnection arrangements, and community coordination. Preparation may need to begin long before the date. The exact grid operator notice rules for gas plants were left as an open question.

Model 6.2

How to read itThe line runs from this year to 2040. Each marker is a retirement timeframe discussed in the meetings. The red line is today.
What it showsVenice is near. The other four are a decade or more away, but the planning and replacement work has long lead times.
So what"There is plenty of time" and "we are already late" can both be argued. Lead times for replacement plants and transmission are the deciding fact.

The community side

Plants pay property taxes that support schools, fire protection and local government. When a plant closes, the assessed value falls and local budgets can fall with it. Venice's 2024 property tax payment was about $666,526, and about $10.1 million has been paid since 2006 as cited. The wider group looked at five plants, Venice, Goose Creek, Pinckneyville, Raccoon Creek and Kinmundy. A fair profile of each covers rating, accredited capacity, run hours, emergency runs, location limits, taxes, jobs, emissions, legal requirements and alternatives. Doing this respects Environmental Justice concerns instead of treating them as an obstacle.

Common mix-ups

  • A peaker's value is not measured by hours run. It is measured by what it does in the hours that matter.
  • Rating, output and accredited capacity are three different numbers.
  • Saying keep the plant is weaker than saying prove the replacement first.

Check before using outside

The exact CEJA run limit and exceptions, remaining permitted hours, the rating versus output, and every property tax figure. Some names and numbers in the notes were garbled.

Say it

Full version, about 60 seconds

Illinois has set important goals to cut power plant emissions, and the community near Venice has carried a real burden. At the same time, some older gas plants still help when the grid is under stress. Venice helped manage a recent Metro East emergency, and it is scheduled to close in 2030. Before that happens, we want confidence that something reliable can do the same job, that local tax and job effects are understood, and that any change fits state law. That is not about delaying the goals. It is about getting the transition right.

Hallway version, about 30 seconds

The goals matter, and so does the community near Venice. The plant helped in a recent emergency and it is set to close in 2030. We just want to be sure something dependable can do that job first, and that the local effects are planned for. That is getting the transition right, not slowing it.
Local official asksAre you asking us to choose jobs and taxes over cleaner air?
You sayNo. Both matter. We want to understand the environmental impact, the reliability need and the effect on local services clearly, and look at responsible options well before the retirement date.
Advocate asksIsn't this just a way to keep a polluting plant open?
You sayWe are asking for evidence and a lawful path before any change, and for the community effects to be part of the plan. If a dependable replacement is ready, that is a good outcome.

The IRP and the modeled shortfalls

You will be able to describe how an IRP works, and tell an import requirement from a capacity deficit.

In plain words

An IRP is a long range checkup. It asks what power the state will need, what will be available, and what has to be added. The headline numbers from it are scenarios, not today's shortages, so how you word them matters.

Full explanation

An IRP looks at several possible futures instead of one. It combines demand forecasts, existing and retiring plants, renewables, transmission, fuel costs, storage, reserve needs and environmental limits, then compares reliability, cost, emissions, deliverability and buildability.

1 Forecast demandHomes, industry, data centers, electrification
2 Count resourcesExisting and retiring
3 Model futuresNormal, high demand, extreme weather
4 Weigh tradeoffsReliability, cost, emissions, feasibility
5 Name needsNew capacity, transmission, storage, timing

The E3 modeling discussed for MISO Zone 4, which is Ameren Illinois territory in the central and southern part of the state, showed two notable scenarios.

Invented teaching numbers for an imaginary zone. The same gap can be an import requirement, a deficit, or both, depending on how much power can actually be brought in.

How to read itThe gap inside the zone is the import requirement. If imports can cover it, there is no deficit. If they cannot, what is left over is the deficit.
What it showsThe two words are not interchangeable. An import requirement is a dependence. A deficit is a shortfall after imports.
So whatIf you say "deficit" when you mean "import requirement," someone will correct you, and you will lose the room. Say which one, and say it is a scenario.
Import requirement

A region needs qualifying power from outside its borders. Imports might be available if lines and neighboring supply allow. It is not automatically a shortage.

Capacity deficit

Credited resources fall below a planning requirement. Do not use the two terms as if they mean the same thing.

The concern raised was that Illinois could retire plants inside its borders while buying power from plants in neighboring states. That may satisfy some state accounting, yet not cut regional emissions, and it leaves Illinois leaning on neighbors during the same weather. It can also move jobs and tax revenue out of state. The counterpoint to keep in mind is that regional trade has real benefits. The test is whether imports are dependable, deliverable, affordable and available under stress.

The calendar after the filing

  • Late October 2026 A joint mitigation plan by state agencies, following a December 2025 state finding of a reliability shortfall. A draft was expected near the end of October.
  • November 16, 2026 The IRP filing is due, moved a day because the 15th falls on a weekend.
  • January 2027 A docketed implementation proceeding was expected, taking six months or more, and could stretch toward November 2027.

Common mix-ups

  • A scenario is not a forecast of what will happen, and not a current measurement.
  • An IRP finding does not by itself change a retirement date.
  • "Import dependence" and "deficit" are different words with different consequences.

Check before using outside

The study year, season, geography and exact metric for each number. Also confirm the filing dates and what follows each one.

Say it

Full version, about 45 seconds

The state's resource planning work looked at many possible futures, and in some of them central and southern Illinois ends up needing thousands of megawatts it does not have inside its own borders. If we retire plants here, will the power we buy from elsewhere be there, deliverable and affordable on the coldest night? That is the question this plan should answer with evidence.

The state's planning models show scenarios where downstate Illinois would depend on thousands of megawatts from outside. Those are scenarios, not today's numbers. The fair question is whether that outside power will be there and affordable on the coldest night.
Legislator asksIs Illinois running out of power?
