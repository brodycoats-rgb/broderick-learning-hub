BUSINESS ACUMEN PLAYBOOK

MISO ERAS and the
Generation Development Cycle

How reliability need becomes a project, an interconnection agreement, accredited capacity, and ultimately service to customers

Purpose
Build practical business fluency for government relations, economic development, utility planning, and policy discussions involving new generation, storage, large loads, and MISO reliability processes.

Prepared for Broderick T. Coats
September 2026

How to Use This Playbook

Use this playbook as a practical briefing guide, not an engineering manual. It is designed to help government relations, economic development, utility planning, and policy teams explain how reliability need becomes dependable capacity, where the process can break down, and which decisions require statutory, regulatory, commercial, or stakeholder action.

The plain-English through-line is this: ERAS can help compress one important step in the development chain, but it does not by itself build generation, secure fuel, finance a project, obtain permits, qualify capacity, or protect customers from bad assumptions.

The core idea
There is no single “generation cycle.” Business acumen comes from seeing several cycles operating at once: the physical project-development cycle, MISO’s interconnection study cycle, the annual resource-adequacy and capacity-market cycle, the transmission-planning cycle, and the political and regulatory decision cycle.

Reader’s Map

The first half of the playbook builds the operating model: what ERAS is, how interconnection differs from resource adequacy, and why project development moves through several overlapping cycles. The second half applies that model to Illinois, Ameren Illinois, large-load growth, and the government-relations work needed to keep reliability, affordability, and economic development in balance.

Learning Outcomes

  • Explain ERAS in plain language without treating it as permission to build.

  • Distinguish nameplate MW, interconnection service, accredited capacity, energy output, and deliverability.

  • Recognize where a project can stall and which party controls the next decision.

  • Connect large-load attraction to resource adequacy, transmission, tariffs, cost allocation, and customer risk.

  • Ask sharper questions of developers, planners, regulators, legislators, and economic-development partners.

1. Executive Orientation

What ERAS Is

The Expedited Resource Addition Study, or ERAS, is MISO’s temporary expedited pathway for reliability-critical generation and storage. In practical terms, it is meant to move qualifying projects more quickly through the interconnection study and agreement process while broader queue reforms continue. MISO describes the process as a path to issue a Generator Interconnection Agreement in three months, subject to tariff requirements, program limits, and cycle timing. [1]

Use ERAS as a Timing Tool, Not a Build Guarantee

  • ERAS is not a state siting permit, air permit, construction authorization, fuel-supply agreement, financing commitment, or customer-rate approval.

  • An executed interconnection agreement is an important milestone, but it is not proof that the project will reach commercial operation on time.

  • It does not make every MW equally dependable. Resource accreditation and seasonal performance still matter.

  • It does not eliminate needed network upgrades or transfer limits. It accelerates study and agreement work for qualifying projects.

Business takeaway
ERAS is a response to a timing mismatch. Load can materialize faster than generation, transmission, and ordinary interconnection studies. The expedited study can shorten one portion of the chain, but it cannot compress every commercial, regulatory, construction, and supply-chain dependency.

The Vocabulary That Prevents Bad Decisions

Term Practical meaning Why it matters
Nameplate capacity The maximum rated output shown for a facility or resource. It is not the same as dependable seasonal capacity.
Interconnection service The service studied and documented for connecting to the transmission system. A GIA defines connection obligations; it is not a construction guarantee.
Accredited capacity The amount of a resource’s capacity that MISO allows to count toward reliability needs, based on seasonal performance rules, technology type, availability, and resource-specific characteristics. A 400 MW resource may contribute something other than 400 MW to adequacy.
Energy MWh produced or discharged over time. Capacity answers “available when needed”; energy answers “how much over time.”
Deliverability The ability to move output through the network under studied conditions. Local generation can still face network constraints.
Resource adequacy Enough available supply to meet demand with a defined reliability standard. This is the planning problem ERAS is meant to help address.

The main takeaway is simple: do not let a single MW number carry more meaning than it should. For policy and business decisions, the meaningful question is whether the resource is deliverable, accredited, financeable, and available in the season and location where risk actually exists.

2. The Five Overlapping Generation Cycles

Cycle A: Need Identification and Commercial Origination

  1. A utility, load-serving entity, state, regulator, or customer identifies a credible need. The trigger may be load growth, retirement, declining accreditation, transfer limitations, policy requirements, or a local deficiency.

  2. The sponsor tests whether the need is durable. For large loads, that means scrutinizing schedule, ramp, creditworthiness, operating profile, and the consequences of delay or cancellation.

  3. A resource concept is matched to the need. Technology choice depends on timing, operating duty, fuel, emissions, land, transmission, contractability, and policy fit.

  4. Commercial structure begins. The parties determine who develops, owns, contracts for, pays for, and bears risk if the project or load changes.

GR question
What evidence converts a promising economic-development prospect into a planning-grade load assumption, and who is accountable if that assumption changes?

Cycle B: Site, Permit, Fuel, and Community Development

  • Site control: land rights, zoning compatibility, access, water, and environmental conditions.

  • Local approvals: zoning, land use, community agreements, road and construction impacts.

  • Environmental approvals: air, water, waste, wetlands, species, and other applicable reviews.

  • Fuel and logistics: pipeline capacity and firm transportation for gas; charging source and duration for storage; equipment supply for all technologies.

  • Community legitimacy: tax base, jobs, local burdens, emergency response, land use, and trust.

These activities often proceed in parallel with interconnection work. That creates development risk because money is committed before every approval or network cost is known.

Cycle C: MISO Generator Interconnection

Under the standard Generator Interconnection Queue, requests progress through MISO’s three-phase Definitive Planning Phase. MISO’s published sequence includes preliminary, revised, and final system-impact work, facility studies, decision points, milestones, and then negotiation and execution of the GIA. MISO states that it uses reasonable efforts to complete DPP system-impact and facility studies in approximately 355 calendar days, followed by an estimated 150-day GIA stage. [3]

Stage Primary question Commercial consequence
Application and readiness Is the request complete, technically defined, and supported by required deposits and site control? Weak readiness creates delay or withdrawal risk.
DPP Phase 1 What preliminary system impacts and upgrade needs appear? First major test of cost and feasibility.
Decision Point 1 Does the customer continue after preliminary results? Capital becomes more exposed.
DPP Phase 2 How do revised models change impacts, facilities, and costs? Cost allocation and assumptions sharpen.
Decision Point 2 Does the customer continue again? Withdrawal becomes more consequential.
DPP Phase 3 What are the final system impacts and network-upgrade facilities? Forms the technical basis for the agreement.
GIA What must each party construct, fund, and complete? Contractualizes interconnection responsibilities.
Construction and testing Can facilities and upgrades be placed in service safely? Schedule and execution risk remain after signing.

The interconnection table should be read as a risk screen, not just a process map. Each stage either narrows uncertainty or exposes a new decision point that can affect cost, schedule, credibility, and public confidence.

Cycle D: Resource Adequacy and the Annual PRA

MISO’s resource-adequacy construct operates seasonally. MISO sets requirements for summer, fall, winter, and spring within a June 1 through May 31 planning year. The annual Planning Resource Auction provides a mechanism for market participants with capacity to sell to participants expecting a shortfall, while respecting zonal and transmission constraints. [4]

Recurring step What happens Business lens
Demand forecasting LSE and distribution-company forecasts inform requirements. Large-load forecasts can increase obligations before new supply arrives.
Reliability studies MISO evaluates reserve needs and loss-of-load risk. The standard is probabilistic, not “zero risk.”
Accreditation Resources receive seasonal capacity values based on applicable rules and performance. Technology and availability affect what counts.
Bilateral/self-supply decisions LSEs may secure capacity before the auction or self-supply. The PRA is not the only procurement pathway.
Planning Resource Auction Offers and requirements clear by season and zone. Prices reveal scarcity and transmission limitations, but are not a complete long-term development contract.
Seasonal assessment and operations MISO reassesses risk as weather, outages, imports, and load evolve. Planning adequacy does not eliminate operational emergencies.
Important distinction
The interconnection cycle asks whether and how a resource can connect. The resource-adequacy cycle asks how much dependable capacity counts and whether enough capacity is available. A project can advance in one cycle while remaining uncertain in the other.

Cycle E: Policy, Regulation, and Cost Recovery

  • State policy defines permissible ownership, procurement, emissions pathways, siting authority, and customer protections.

  • Regulators determine which costs may be recovered and how risk is allocated among customers, shareholders, developers, and large-load customers.

  • Tariffs translate policy into service terms, contribution requirements, security, ramp milestones, and exit protections.

  • Legislation may be needed when existing authority cannot coordinate generation, large-load commitments, long-term procurement, or reliability mitigation.

  • Political timing rarely matches construction timing. Credible strategy begins before a crisis or filing deadline.

3. ERAS in Depth

Eligibility Logic

MISO’s published materials identify ERAS as a pathway for resources tied to a demonstrated reliability need, including a verified new load addition or resource-adequacy deficiency. Public descriptions indicate that the relevant electric retail regulatory authority, or RERRA, verifies the need; interconnection service is limited relative to the verified need and tied to the same Local Resource Zone; and projects must meet a defined commercial-operation window. The controlling tariff and current MISO guidance should be checked for any transaction. [1][5]

A Practical ERAS Sequence

Step What must align Failure mode to watch
1. Establish need Forecast, location, timing, magnitude, and regulatory verification. Speculative or changing load.
2. Match resource Technology, size, location, operating profile, and development maturity. A resource that is quick to study but slow to permit or fuel.
3. Assemble application Technical data, site readiness, commercial documents, and required verification. Incomplete or inconsistent assumptions.
4. Enter a study cycle Submission timing and MISO sequencing determine cycle placement. Missing cutoff or limited program capacity.
5. System study MISO evaluates impacts, constraints, and required facilities. Unexpected network upgrades or affected-system issues.
6. Execute GIA Parties accept responsibilities, milestones, and costs. Agreement without financeable risk allocation.
7. Build in parallel Generation, interconnection facilities, network upgrades, permits, and fuel advance. One critical path slips while others appear on track.
8. Qualify as capacity Registration, testing, commercial operation, and accreditation requirements are met. Nameplate MW arrives but dependable value is lower or late.

Why a Three-Month GIA Does Not Mean Three-Month Generation

ERAS targets the study and agreement portion of the process. Physical delivery still depends on equipment, engineering, procurement, construction, fuel infrastructure, transmission-owner work, network upgrades, permitting, financing, and testing. The commercial-operation target should therefore be evaluated as an integrated critical path, not as an interconnection date alone.

ERAS Governance Questions

  • Who owns the underlying load forecast and verifies that it is still valid?

  • What happens if the associated load is delayed, downsized, or canceled?

  • Is project size reasonably proportional to the need?

  • Which costs are directly assigned, socialized, or recovered through retail rates?

  • Does the technology provide the operating characteristics that the identified need requires?

  • Are fuel, duration, start capability, emissions, water, and extreme-weather performance credible?

  • Can the resource qualify for the intended seasonal accreditation by the needed planning year?

  • What public reporting is needed to preserve confidence in an expedited pathway?

4. What the ERAS Cycles Reveal

ERAS “cycles” are study cohorts, not the operating life cycle of a power plant. Each cohort is a snapshot of projects considered under the temporary pathway at a point in time. The portfolio can change as projects withdraw, agreements are completed, or new cycles begin.

Cycle Publicly reported pattern Business-acumen lesson
Cycle 1 Public analyses describe a portfolio dominated by natural gas, with a small storage share. Earliest use emphasized dispatchable additions tied to identified needs.
Cycle 2 Public analyses show a broader mix that included gas, storage, solar, and wind. Technology mix can shift with the sponsoring need and project readiness.
Cycle 3 15 projects totaling about 8.8 GW; public analysis reported roughly 6.4 GW gas and 2.3 GW storage. Large load and manufacturing needs drove substantial gas and utility-scale storage activity.
Cycle 4 Seven projects totaling about 3.7 GW; public analysis reported 83% gas, one 230 MW storage project, and 400 MW of Illinois solar. Cycle composition can swing sharply and withdrawals matter as much as initial applications.
Cycle 5 15 resources totaling approximately 7.3 GW, combining gas, storage, solar, and wind; expected in service by 2029. The portfolio broadened, but gas and batteries still represent most listed MW.

Sources: MISO fifth-cycle release and public cycle analyses. Cycle 1 and Cycle 2 are described directionally because the reviewed sources did not provide a complete authoritative cycle-by-cycle table. Use MISO’s current ERAS Interconnection Requests register for transaction-level diligence. [2][6][7]

How to Read a Cycle Without Being Misled

  1. Separate applications, selected study participants, executed GIAs, projects under construction, and projects in commercial operation. They are different milestones.

  2. Count resources carefully. A single development name can include more than one resource, as with the DOUBLEC solar and storage components.

  3. Reconcile stated portfolio totals with project-level MW, while allowing for rounding and updates.

  4. Track withdrawals and replacements. An initial cycle announcement is not a final built portfolio.

  5. Classify by function, not fuel label alone. A combined-cycle plant, combustion turbine, four-hour battery, solar facility, and wind project solve different operational problems.

  6. Map each project to its Local Resource Zone and identified need. System totals can obscure local constraints.

The right metric
Do not ask only, “How many gigawatts are in ERAS?” Ask, “How many MW have executed GIAs, remain financeable, have permits and fuel, can meet the need date, and will receive useful seasonal accreditation in the right zone?”

5. Project Economics and Technology Tradeoffs

Resource Commercial strength Key exposure Best question
Combined-cycle gas High output and efficient operation relative to simple-cycle gas; can serve sustained load. Long lead equipment, gas infrastructure, emissions, capital intensity, and utilization risk. Is the expected duty cycle sufficient to recover fixed costs?
Combustion turbine Fast-start capability and peaking flexibility. Lower efficiency, fuel cost exposure, emissions, and limited run hours. Is the value primarily capacity, energy scarcity, reserves, or local reliability?
Battery storage Fast response, flexible charging/discharging, and modular deployment. Duration, charging economics, degradation, accreditation, augmentation, and interconnection limits. What need duration must it cover, and what is the charging assumption?
Solar Low fuel and operating cost; modular and policy-aligned. Weather-dependent output and limited contribution during some risk hours. What is its seasonal accredited value and curtailment exposure?
Wind Energy production and fuel diversity. Variable output, transmission dependence, and siting constraints. Does its output profile align with regional risk hours?
Hybrid Can share interconnection and combine complementary attributes. Complex controls, charging rules, shared limits, and contract design. How do components compete for the interconnection limit?
Demand response Can reduce obligations without building equivalent supply. Measurement, customer participation, dispatch limits, and performance. Is it dependable under prolonged or repeated events?

The Revenue Stack

  • Energy revenue: payment for MWh based on market dispatch or contract terms.

  • Capacity value: compensation associated with accredited availability for resource adequacy.

  • Ancillary services: products supporting balancing, reserves, and system performance where qualified.

  • Contract revenue: tolling, power purchase, capacity, or utility procurement arrangements.

  • Customer-backed value: direct alignment with a large-load service commitment or special contract.

  • Tax and incentive value: applicable federal, state, and local provisions, subject to current law and qualification.

A financeable project usually needs a credible answer to revenue sufficiency, not merely a favorable interconnection position. For business discussions, ask which revenue elements are contracted, which are merchant, and who absorbs rule changes.

The Core Risk Allocation

Risk Possible bearer Policy question
Load fails to materialize Large-load customer, developer, utility customers, or shareholders Should deposits, minimum bills, collateral, or exit payments protect other customers?
Generation delay Developer, contractor, utility, or customer Are milestones and replacement-power obligations enforceable?
Network-upgrade increase Interconnection customer, transmission customers, or allocated beneficiaries Does cost allocation match cost causation and regional benefit?
Fuel constraint Generator, pipeline shipper, customers, or market Is firm fuel or alternate capability needed for the reliability claim?
Accreditation change Resource owner, counterparty, or customers Is the contract for nameplate MW or qualified accredited capacity?
Policy change Developer, utility, customers, or state Who bears compliance and stranded-cost risk?

The practical lesson is that technology choice is never just a fuel debate. The better question is what problem the system is trying to solve: fast-start peaking, sustained energy, seasonal capacity, local reliability, flexibility, customer-backed growth, or policy compliance.

6. Illinois and Ameren Illinois Lens

Why Illinois Is Structurally Different

Illinois is structurally different because responsibility for supply is spread across wholesale markets, load-serving entities, alternative retail suppliers, municipal systems, cooperatives, and state policy institutions. That makes reliability strategy less direct than it would be in a fully vertically integrated environment. The state’s 2025 Resource Adequacy Study notes that new electric supply resources typically take five to seven years to develop, while some transmission can require ten years or more. [8]

Implication
Illinois can identify a statewide reliability concern without having a single vertically integrated utility that is automatically responsible for building the next plant. Policy design must therefore answer who procures, for whom, through what authority, and with what customer protections.

Ameren Illinois Role Clarity

For Ameren Illinois, the central message is role clarity. As a distribution utility, Ameren Illinois does not own or operate generation, but it still has a major role in making large-load growth workable. That includes forecasting, service planning, tariff administration, customer protections, system coordination, and clear communication about what must happen before load, transmission, and supply can move together. [9]

Ameren Illinois can help Ameren Illinois does not automatically control
Develop credible load forecasts and service requirements. Whether an independent generator reaches financial close.
Plan distribution and coordinate subtransmission and transmission impacts. MISO’s final interconnection study results or market rules.
Design and administer approved tariffs and customer protections. State siting, environmental permits, or legislative outcomes.
Provide operational and customer information to relevant processes. The commercial decisions of every ARES, developer, or large-load customer.
Explain system needs and implementation consequences. A universal statewide generation-procurement obligation absent authority.

Large-Load Integration: The Whole Arc

  1. Economic development identifies the opportunity and tests site fit.

  2. The customer provides a load profile, schedule, ramp, redundancy needs, and credit support.

  3. Utility planners determine service facilities, system impacts, and sequencing.

  4. Transmission and generation implications are identified, including the need for MISO studies.

  5. Tariff and contract terms allocate upgrade costs, minimum obligations, security, delay risk, and exit risk.

  6. A credible supply and resource-adequacy pathway is aligned with the customer energization date.

  7. Permits, construction, equipment, transmission, and generation advance against shared milestones.

  8. Commission, legislative, and stakeholder communications explain costs, benefits, protections, and contingencies.

From a utility perspective, the strongest position is not simply to welcome every large-load prospect or resist new obligations. It is to insist on a disciplined pathway: credible load commitments, transparent cost causation, enforceable customer protections, realistic supply assumptions, and enough flexibility to phase service if the customer or resource timeline changes.

7. Government Relations Playbook

Your Role: Integrator, Not Substitute Engineer

The highest-value government relations role is not to become the engineer, market monitor, or tariff lawyer. It is to connect their work into a position that elected officials, regulators, executives, customers, and community stakeholders can understand and act on. Credibility comes from being precise about the facts, honest about tradeoffs, and clear about who owns each decision.

Five Moves for Credible Engagement

Move What to do Evidence of credibility
Learn Build fluency in interconnection, resource adequacy, accreditation, tariffs, planning, and technology duty cycles. You use terms precisely and can explain them plainly.
Map Identify the decision, authority, affected parties, timeline, and unresolved dependencies. You know who can say yes, who pays, and what must happen next.
Frame Connect reliability, affordability, economic development, customer protection, and clean-energy obligations. You avoid presenting one objective as costless.
Negotiate Trade across timing, risk allocation, transparency, milestones, and safeguards. You bring options, not just objections.
Influence Use validated facts, local benefits, constituent impact, and a realistic implementation pathway. Your proposal survives technical and political scrutiny.

Stakeholder Likely priority Productive line of inquiry

7. Message Architecture

A strong explanation can be built in four sentences:

  1. Need: State the verified reliability or load problem, including location and timing.

  2. Constraint: Explain why the ordinary process or existing resource mix may not meet the date.

  3. Solution: Describe the resource, interconnection pathway, and supporting tariff or procurement structure.

  4. Protection: Identify who pays, what milestones apply, what happens if assumptions change, and how the public can evaluate performance.

Example
“The issue is not simply adding megawatts. The need is dependable capacity in the right location and season, synchronized with a credible customer schedule. ERAS can accelerate the interconnection agreement for a qualifying project, but permits, fuel, transmission construction, financing, and accreditation still determine whether it becomes useful capacity. Any policy response should therefore pair speed with milestone discipline, transparent verification, and protection for customers if the load or project does not materialize.”

This structure keeps the message balanced. It starts with the problem, explains the constraint, avoids overselling the remedy, and ends with accountability.

Negotiation Levers

  • Milestones: tie service, procurement, or approvals to measurable load and project progress.

  • Security: deposits, collateral, letters of credit, minimum bills, or exit payments.

  • Phasing: energize load in steps as network and supply capability becomes available.

  • Flexibility: interruptible service, demand response, backup arrangements, or operational curtailment where appropriate.

  • Cost allocation: separate directly caused facilities from broader regional facilities and document the rationale.

  • Sunset and review: require periodic reassessment of temporary or experimental authority.

  • Transparency: publish aggregate status without disclosing protected customer information.

  • Fallbacks: specify replacement capacity, delayed service, or alternative configurations before a failure occurs.

Red Flags in Briefings

  • “The project is approved” when only a study or GIA milestone has been completed.

  • Using nameplate MW as if it equals seasonal accredited capacity.

  • Treating an executed GIA as proof of financing or construction readiness.

  • Assuming a data center announcement is equivalent to a planning-grade load commitment.

  • Describing batteries without duration, charging source, degradation, or accreditation context.

  • Describing gas as firm without addressing pipeline and winter conditions.

  • Treating the PRA clearing price as the full cost of new generation.

  • Claiming “no customer impact” without explaining direct, network, stranded, and risk costs.

  • Using regional MW totals to answer a local-zone or transmission-constraint problem.

Glossary and Reference Terms

Term Working definition and example
Accredited capacity The amount of a resource’s capacity that MISO allows to count toward reliability needs after applying seasonal, performance, availability, and technology rules. Example: a 400 MW battery may not count as 400 MW of dependable summer capacity if its duration or expected risk-hour performance supports a lower value.
Accreditation The process of assigning a seasonal reliability value to a resource. Example: a solar project receives a capacity value based on expected contribution during risk hours, not simply its installed MW.
Adequacy Having enough dependable supply, demand response, imports, and reserves to meet forecasted demand under reliability standards. Example: if new data center load is expected in a zone, adequacy asks whether enough dependable capacity will be available before the load arrives.
Affected system Another transmission system, neighboring utility, or higher-queued facility that could be affected by a new interconnection request. Example: a generator connecting in one part of MISO may require review by a neighboring transmission owner if power flows could affect that system.
Availability Whether a resource is actually ready and able to perform when needed. Example: a gas unit may be accredited, but it is not available during an emergency if it is on forced outage or lacks fuel transportation.
BESS Battery Energy Storage System, a facility that charges from the grid or a paired resource and later discharges electricity. Example: a four-hour BESS can help cover an evening peak if it has enough charge and the event duration fits its capability.
COD Commercial Operation Date, the date when a resource is approved to operate commercially after construction, testing, and required approvals. Example: a project may execute a GIA in 2026 but not reach COD until 2029.
Deliverability The ability to move a resource’s output through the transmission system under studied conditions. Example: a project can be physically near load but still face deliverability limits if transmission constraints prevent output from reaching the needed area.
DPP Definitive Planning Phase, MISO’s phased standard generator-interconnection study process. Example: a project in DPP moves through study phases and decision points before it can negotiate a Generator Interconnection Agreement.
ELCC Effective Load Carrying Capability, a method for estimating how much reliability value a resource or portfolio provides during risk hours. Example: ELCC may show that adding more solar has diminishing capacity value if system risk shifts to hours after sunset.
Energy Electricity produced, consumed, stored, or discharged over time, usually measured in MWh. Example: a 100 MW battery discharging at full output for four hours delivers 400 MWh of energy.
ERAS Expedited Resource Addition Study, MISO’s temporary expedited pathway for qualifying reliability-critical resources. Example: a project tied to a verified load addition may seek ERAS treatment to move more quickly through interconnection study and agreement steps.
ERIS Energy Resource Interconnection Service, a type of interconnection service that allows a resource to inject energy when the system can accept it but does not by itself provide capacity deliverability rights. Example: a wind project with ERIS may sell energy when available but may need separate qualification to count as capacity.
GIA Generator Interconnection Agreement, the contract that identifies interconnection facilities, network upgrades, cost responsibilities, milestones, and operating requirements. Example: a signed GIA means the parties have agreed on interconnection obligations, but it does not mean the plant is financed, permitted, or built.
IC Interconnection Customer, the developer, owner, or sponsor requesting to connect a generating resource to the transmission system. Example: the IC submits technical data, deposits, site information, and milestone materials during the interconnection process.
Interconnection vs. accreditation Interconnection determines whether and how a resource can connect to the grid. Accreditation determines how much of that resource counts toward reliability needs. Example: a 300 MW solar project may be interconnected, but its accredited capacity could be much lower during evening risk hours.
ISAC / SAC Initial or seasonal accredited capacity concepts used to describe how much capacity counts under seasonal resource-adequacy rules. Example: a resource may carry one accredited value in summer and a different value in winter because risks and performance differ by season.
LOLE Loss of Load Expectation, a probabilistic reliability metric estimating how often demand may exceed available resources. Example: the common “one day in ten years” concept is a planning standard, not a guarantee that outages can never happen.
LRZ Local Resource Zone, a geographic capacity zone used in MISO resource-adequacy planning. Example: capacity that helps one LRZ may not fully solve a shortage in another LRZ if transfer limits restrict deliverability.
LSE Load-Serving Entity, the entity responsible for serving retail load and meeting applicable resource-adequacy obligations. Example: an LSE may secure capacity through contracts, self-supply, or the Planning Resource Auction to meet its seasonal requirement.
Load factor A measure of how consistently a customer or system uses electricity over time compared with its peak demand. Example: a data center with steady 24-hour usage usually has a high load factor, while a customer that peaks briefly on hot afternoons has a lower load factor.
MTEP MISO Transmission Expansion Plan, MISO’s regional transmission planning process and portfolio. Example: an MTEP project may be needed to relieve congestion, enable resource deliverability, or support long-term reliability needs.
MW / MWh Megawatt measures instantaneous capacity; megawatt-hour measures energy over time. Example: a 100 MW battery discharging for four hours produces 400 MWh.
Nameplate capacity The maximum rated output of a generator or storage device under specified conditions. Example: a 500 MW plant has 500 MW of nameplate capacity, but its accredited capacity may be lower depending on performance, availability, and seasonal rules.
Network upgrade A transmission-system addition or modification identified as necessary through interconnection or planning studies. Example: a new generator may require a substation upgrade, breaker replacement, reconductoring, or new line work before it can interconnect reliably.
NRIS Network Resource Interconnection Service, interconnection service designed to support deliverability for capacity-resource purposes under applicable rules. Example: a project seeking to count toward resource adequacy may need NRIS rather than energy-only interconnection treatment.
PRA Planning Resource Auction, MISO’s annual capacity auction for the June-through-May planning year, clearing by season and zone. Example: if an LSE is short capacity for summer, it may buy capacity through the PRA from a participant with surplus accredited capacity.
PRM / PRMR Planning Reserve Margin and Planning Reserve Margin Requirement, the reserve level above forecasted demand needed to meet reliability standards. Example: if expected peak demand is 10,000 MW and the planning reserve requirement is 15%, the system needs more than 10,000 MW of dependable capacity.
Qualified capacity The capacity a resource is eligible to offer or count after satisfying applicable registration, testing, operational, and market requirements. Example: a new unit may be physically complete but not yet qualified capacity until it has completed the required market and operational steps.
RERRA Relevant Electric Retail Regulatory Authority, the state or retail regulator with authority related to retail electric service and verification issues. Example: for an Illinois-related ERAS request, the relevant authority may need to verify the underlying need before MISO treats the resource as eligible.
Resource adequacy Sufficient available power to meet demand under an established reliability criterion. Example: resource adequacy asks whether the system has enough dependable capacity across seasons, not merely whether enough generation nameplate MW exists on paper.

Source Discipline

This playbook separates published facts from practical interpretation. Public rules, tariff requirements, accreditation methods, project status, and cycle rosters can change. Before using the material in an external position, filing, or legislative proposal, verify the current MISO tariff, business-practice manuals, ERAS request register, relevant public-utility commission orders, and project-specific documents.

Bottom line
ERAS can accelerate an important interconnection decision. The business challenge is everything around that decision: credible load, financeable projects, workable tariffs, permits, fuel, transmission, accreditation, customer protections, and a political story that can withstand scrutiny.
