Purpose and government relations takeaway. This paper is an internal decision and preparation tool. It explains the Peoples Gas record, identifies transferable ICC standards, tests the implications for Ameren Illinois, and recommends government relations actions. The central conclusion is that the Peoples Gas proceeding should be treated as evidence of broader scrutiny of utility capital plans, not as an isolated gas utility controversy.

What is established. Recent ICC orders show sustained scrutiny of capital need, timing, pace, alternatives, affordability, and measurable customer benefits across multiple Illinois utilities.

What is emerging. Alternatives analysis, affordability metrics, and closer alignment between approved plans and recoverable investment are becoming practical expectations in major utility proceedings.

What should be monitored. Track Docket 26-0065, the Future of Gas final report, the 2026 Nicor proceeding, and substantive rulings in Ameren Illinois’s MYIGP for standards that may affect future filings or legislative strategy.

The one-sentence version. A financing mechanism outran a governance mechanism, and the correction that followed reset the burden of proof for every utility in the state.

Plain-language script. Chicago had a real safety problem: more than 1,000 miles of very old iron pipe needed attention. Federal safety pressure increased after the San Bruno explosion, and Illinois responded by creating Rider QIP, which let utilities recover qualifying investments through a surcharge without waiting for a full rate case. That made it easier to spend quickly, but spending and customer bills rose much faster than expected. The program moved from an original estimate of about $1.4 billion and a 2030 target toward projections of roughly $11 billion and completion as late as 2048. Regulators then paused and restructured the work, imposed a 2035 deadline and independent monitoring, and began applying the same questions to other utilities: Is the project needed, why now, why at this pace, and what alternatives were considered? For Ameren Illinois, the lesson is to build those answers into the MYIGP and future filings from the start.

Section 3. Genesis of the Program

The program did not begin as a climate fight. It began as an engineering and federal safety problem.

Chicago’s distribution system includes cast iron and ductile iron mains, some dating to the nineteenth century. Engineering studies flagged the risk repeatedly across four decades, beginning with the Zinder studies between 1981 and 2002 and continuing with the Kiefner study in 2007.

Two external events accelerated everything. The San Bruno pipeline explosion in 2010 and the resulting PHMSA Call to Action in 2011 pushed distribution integrity to the top of the national regulatory agenda.

The financing mechanism arrived in 2013. Public Act 98-57 created Rider QIP under Section 9-220.3 of the Public Utilities Act, allowing gas utilities to recover qualifying infrastructure investment through an automatic surcharge rather than waiting for a full rate case. The Third District Appellate Court later summarized the legislative purpose plainly: the General Assembly enacted the section to encourage gas utilities to invest in infrastructure to improve the safety and reliability of their systems.

That single legislative decision is the hinge of this entire story. QIP converted a slow, contested recovery process into a fast, formulaic one. Spending accelerated. Bills followed.

Legislators were told QIP would cost Chicago customers roughly $1.14 per month. Between 2017 and 2023 it reached as high as $30 per month, according to the Attorney General’s Office.

Plain-language script. This program began with a legitimate engineering and safety concern, not with the later climate and affordability debate. Chicago had aging cast iron and ductile iron pipe, federal regulators were pressing utilities to improve pipeline safety, and Illinois created Rider QIP in 2013 to speed investment by allowing recovery through a surcharge. The key turning point was removing the normal delay between spending money and collecting it from customers. That helped accelerate work, but it also reduced a natural check on spending. Customers were initially told the surcharge would be about $1.14 per month; it later reached as much as $30 per month.

Section 4. Purpose, Expectations, and Fallout

The original promise. Retire aging iron pipe posing leak and rupture risk. Complete by roughly 2030. Spend roughly $1.4 billion, with broader scope estimates near $2 billion. Modernize metering, relocate meters outside homes, improve inspectability. Reduce methane leakage.

What happened instead. By 2023 the program had missed quarterly targets for twenty-two consecutive quarters and had ended every year over budget since quarterly reporting began in 2018. Cost projections moved from roughly $2 billion toward $11 billion and in some analyses toward $13 billion. Scope expanded from iron pipe retirement into a broader System Modernization Program including work not directly tied to the highest risk pipe.

Sun-Times reporting in February 2025 captured the execution problem in a single line: the program had already cost $3.3 billion with roughly 38 percent of the work complete.

The affordability consequence. Peoples Gas customers were collectively $111 million behind on bills by July 2023. Disconnection notices went to 5 percent of customers in a single month, a higher rate than any other major Illinois utility.

The political consequence. The Chicago City Council passed a resolution in April 2020 calling the program troubled and urging the Governor and General Assembly to restore oversight. Legislation to sunset QIP early drew 36 supporting organizations. QIP ultimately sunset on schedule at the end of 2023 rather than early.

Plain-language script. The original goal was straightforward: replace dangerous old pipe, finish around 2030, and spend about $1.4 billion, with some broader estimates closer to $2 billion. Instead, the program repeatedly missed targets, ran over budget, expanded beyond the highest-risk pipe, and moved toward an $11 billion or higher projection. By early 2025, about $3.3 billion had been spent while only roughly 38 percent of the work was complete. At the same time, customer debt and disconnection pressure grew, which turned an engineering program into an affordability and political problem. The letter’s concern about cost escalation and customer impact is therefore grounded in the program’s actual history.

Section 5. ICC History and Regulatory Outcomes

2015 to 2018, QIP reconciliation. Docket 15-0209 produced a settlement reducing 2014 QIP recovery by $5.4 million, with roughly $4.7 million directed to low income assistance and customer refunds. The settlement also barred credit agency reporting of late payments through December 2023 and required Peoples Gas to solely fund Share the Warmth for a year. The ICC’s executive director described staff analyzing every work order, invoice, permit, and management decision.

2007 to 2020, audits and studies. The Liberty audits, the AMRP to SMP dockets in 2016 to 2018, additional workshops, and the second Kiefner study all documented execution problems.

November 2023, the rate case and the pause. The ICC cut the Peoples Gas request from $402 million to roughly $300 million, set ROE at 9.38 percent against a requested 9.90 percent, and paused most SMP spending pending investigation. Across four gas utilities the Commission cut roughly $240 million in combined requests. The same order created a low income discount of up to 83 percent for qualifying customers and launched the two-step Future of Gas proceeding.

December 2023. The Commission unanimously rejected an emergency motion to spend $134 million winding down program work, with Chairman Scott noting the utility has an enduring statutory responsibility to maintain a safe system.

January 2024 to February 2025, the investigation. Docket 24-0081 ran twelve months.

February 20, 2025, the landmark order. The Commission terminated the SMP construct and replaced it with a directive focused specifically on cast and ductile iron retirement. Key elements: a 2035 deadline aligned with PHMSA and engineering recommendations; an independent Safety Monitor reporting annually beginning 2027; notice of potential financial penalties for noncompliance; and an explicit statement that the order is not a green light for business as usual.

Commissioner Paradis framed the finding precisely: the record showed Peoples Gas was not prioritizing CI/DI removal but pursued it as one of several components of the broader program, and as a result the retirement rate slowed over time.

November 19, 2025, the comparative orders. On the same day the Commission cut Nicor by $146.5 million, or nearly 47 percent, and Ameren Illinois gas by $55.8 million, or about 43 percent, approving 9.60 percent ROE for both. The Commission stated that many disallowances were made where the utility failed to sufficiently articulate management decisions including the need, timing, and pace of proposed projects. For Ameren specifically, it found the transmission investment program was both overly accelerated and overly focused on replacement.

For Ameren Illinois, the phrase “overly accelerated and overly focused on replacement” deserves particular attention. It reflects a concern similar to the one raised in the Peoples Gas record and shows that the Commission’s scrutiny of timing, pace, and alternatives is already being applied to the company’s own proposals.

December 18, 2025, the reconciliation. The Commission struck $11.2 million from Ameren’s $59.6 million multi-year rate reconciliation in the first proceeding under the new performance-based evaluation process, and directed Ameren to include affordability data updates and cost benefit analyses of grid plan projects in future reconciliation dockets.

August 6, 2026. In Docket 16-0376 the Commission partially granted discontinuation of legacy Peoples Gas reporting while refusing to relieve reporting on SIPI, emergency, and HP work, and directed a separate actual cost column so regulators and stakeholders can see actual CI/DI retirement costs by category.

January 2026 to present, Docket 26-0065. Peoples Gas filed a $202.3 million request on January 5, 2026 with a 10.1 percent ROE, citing roughly $600 million in planned investment through 2027. In May 2026 CUB, PIRG, and the Attorney General filed testimony seeking cuts of at least $137 million, with the AG calling for eliminating the increase entirely and giving customers a $4 million reduction. PIRG projected bills rising an average of $581 annually by 2035 and total customer cost of $15.2 billion through 2070, with $2 billion in savings available from alternatives. In July 2026 Peoples Gas cut its request to roughly $144 million.

May 2026, the AG settlements. Separate from the rate case, settlements involving the AG, ICC Staff, CUB, and the companies delivered more than $130 million in bill credits over three years, roughly $50 in 2026 and $40 in each of 2027 and 2028. Peoples Gas also agreed to remove $130 million in capital costs from rate base, which the AG estimated at $350 million in lifetime consumer savings.

Plain-language script. The ICC’s response developed over time rather than through one decision. Regulators first reviewed individual QIP costs and required refunds and assistance. In 2023, they cut the rate request, lowered the authorized return, paused most modernization spending, and created a low-income discount. In February 2025, they ended the broader program structure, refocused the work on cast and ductile iron pipe, imposed a 2035 deadline, and required independent monitoring. Later orders cut Nicor and Ameren Illinois requests where the utilities did not adequately explain need, timing, and pace. In the current Peoples Gas case, the company reduced its request from $202.3 million to roughly $144 million, while advocates continue to seek larger cuts. The broader lesson is that the ICC is now demanding project-level justification and clearer affordability evidence.

Section 6. How the Utility Makes Money

This is the mechanism most stakeholders misunderstand, and the one you should be able to explain in ninety seconds.

The formula.

Revenue Requirement equals Operating Expenses plus Depreciation plus Taxes plus Rate Base multiplied by Weighted Average Cost of Capital.

Only the final term produces shareholder earnings. The utility does not profit on gas commodity, which passes through at cost. It does not profit on O&M. It profits on invested capital.

Rate base. Net original cost of plant in service, plus working capital, less accumulated depreciation and accumulated deferred income taxes. Every dollar of prudent capital placed in service enlarges it.

Weighted average cost of capital. A blend of debt cost and authorized ROE applied to the equity layer. Nicor’s approved structure in November 2025 was 9.60 percent ROE on a 50.00 percent equity ratio, which is the cleanest recent public reference point for how these components combine.

Why replacement is financially attractive regardless of intent. Replacing a main is capital and earns a return for forty to seventy years. Repairing or monitoring a main is expense and earns nothing. That asymmetry is structural in cost of service regulation. It does not prove bad faith. It explains precisely why intervenors demand alternatives analysis and why the Commission now requires it.

What QIP changed. Under a normal rate case, capital sits unrecovered until the next case, and that regulatory lag disciplines spending pace. QIP removed the lag through an automatic annual surcharge. Spending accelerated because the financial friction was gone. This is the clearest transferable lesson in the entire record.

How the Commission claws value back. Four levers, all visible in the benchmark table in Section 1.3: disallowance of imprudent cost, reduction of authorized ROE, removal of plant from rate base, and direct customer credits. All four have now been used against multiple Illinois utilities, and two produced SEC-disclosed earnings charges.

Plain-language script. A regulated utility earns for shareholders mainly by investing in assets that go into rate base. Routine operating expenses are generally recovered at cost, and the gas commodity itself is passed through without profit. A replacement main can earn a return over decades, while repairing or monitoring an existing main is usually an operating expense and does not earn a return. That does not prove improper intent, but it explains why regulators want utilities to compare replacement with less expensive alternatives. Rider QIP made capital recovery faster by removing normal regulatory lag. The ICC can later protect customers by disallowing costs, lowering the authorized return, removing plant from rate base, or ordering credits.

Section 7. Press and Narrative

Coverage has been sustained, statewide, and consistently framed around affordability rather than safety.

The recurring media frame is over budget, behind schedule, record profits. That triad appears across ABC7, CBS Chicago, the Sun-Times, Capitol News Illinois, Crain’s, NPR Illinois, and Block Club.

Consumer advocates have maintained exceptional message discipline. CUB and PIRG have used the same numbers, framing, and spokespeople for years. Polling released in 2023 showed Chicagoans opposed the rate hike 61 to 32, rising to 73 to 25 after hearing arguments against it.

The utility’s counter-message is equally consistent. Peoples Gas says the Commission directed the work, the work is necessary for safety, delay increases both risk and cost, and postponing essential infrastructure work is not a solution.

Notably, the Commission itself has become a quoted actor. Chairman Scott and Commissioner Paradis have both given on-the-record statements characterizing utility conduct, which is unusual and worth tracking.

Plain-language script. The public story has been simple and effective: the program is over budget, behind schedule, and producing strong utility earnings while customers struggle with bills. Consumer advocates have repeated the same figures and themes for years, and polling shows those messages resonate. Peoples Gas answers that the work is required for safety, delay raises both risk and cost, and essential infrastructure cannot simply be postponed. The important communications lesson is that safety alone has not displaced the affordability narrative. A credible response needs to explain the necessity of the work, the controls on cost and pace, the alternatives considered, and the specific customer benefit.

Section 9. What This Means for Ameren Illinois

Our own record is the starting point, not Peoples Gas. The Commission cut our electric grid plan by roughly 75 percent in December 2024, set our electric ROE at 8.715 percent against a requested 9.27 percent, cut our gas request by 43 percent in November 2025, set our gas ROE at 9.60 percent against a requested 10.70 percent, and struck $11.2 million from our reconciliation in December 2025 while directing us to supply affordability data and cost benefit analyses in future filings.

The MYIGP. Ameren Illinois filed its second multi-year integrated grid plan covering 2028 to 2031 on January 20, 2026, aligned with CEJA and the Clean and Reliable Grid Affordability Act. The plan pairs infrastructure hardening with smarter grid technologies, faster outage response, demand-side resources, and customer programs, serving 1.2 million residential customers and 130,000 businesses.

The evidentiary burden we should assume. Based on the November 2025 orders, assume the Commission will ask three questions about every major program in the plan. Why is this needed. Why now and at this pace. What did you consider instead of replacement. The Nicor and Ameren disallowances were concentrated precisely where the utility failed to articulate need, timing, and pace.

Next year’s return on projected investment. The 9.60 percent gas figure and the 8.715 percent electric figure are the live benchmarks. The Peoples Gas outcome in Docket 26-0065, expected around December 2026, will reset the gas anchor one way or the other. Build the filing expecting to be compared.

Future of Gas. Phase 2C produced a large set of policy recommendations across accelerated depreciation, non-pipeline alternatives, integrated gas and electric planning, long-term gas planning, obligation to provide heat, and line extension reform. The Commission has already ordered Staff to report on line extension allowances, noting that multiple states have ended the practice of existing customers subsidizing new connections. The final report is expected in December 2026.

Where we are genuinely different. Lower density, slower iron pipe exposure, an active economic development and large load story, and a reliability narrative that reaches labor and business constituencies the Peoples Gas coalition does not touch. We are not Peoples Gas. But the analytical standard travels, and in one respect, the depth of the grid plan cut, we have already been treated more severely.

Plain-language script. Ameren Illinois should begin with its own regulatory record. The ICC cut our electric grid plan spending by about 75 percent, cut our gas request by about 43 percent, approved returns below what we requested, and later required more affordability data and cost-benefit analysis. That means the next MYIGP should answer three questions for every major investment: Why is it needed, why must it be done now and at this pace, and what alternatives were considered? Ameren is different from Peoples Gas because of its service territory, economic development opportunities, large-load growth, and reliability needs. But those differences will only matter if they are translated into a clear project-level record and measurable customer benefits.

Section 10. Political Implication Hypothesis and Forecast

Near term, through spring session 2027.

Base case. Recent orders suggest that the final decision in Docket 26-0065 is likely to approve an increase below the approximately $144 million request and may authorize an ROE at or below the 9.60 percent level approved for Nicor and Ameren Illinois gas in 2025. This is a forecast based on recent Commission practice, not an established outcome. Indicators to watch include the proposed order, treatment of disputed capital projects, Staff’s ROE position, and the weight given to affordability evidence.

Legislative outlook. A legislative proposal could emerge in the 2027 spring session, particularly if the final rate order or the Future of Gas report intensifies affordability concerns. Plausible approaches include gas infrastructure planning requirements, non-pipeline alternatives analysis, or a mandatory cost-benefit screen for major capital programs. Renewal of a QIP-style automatic recovery mechanism appears unlikely in the near term. Indicators to watch include bill introductions, sponsor composition, labor and business engagement, and whether the debate expands beyond Chicago.

Medium term, two to four years.

The Future of Gas final report in December 2026 becomes the policy blueprint. Integrated gas and electric planning is the most likely structural outcome. If adopted, gas capital plans must be justified against electric system alternatives, which changes the analytical burden for every gas utility in the state.

Accelerated depreciation is the sleeper issue. Requiring depreciation studies that model declining throughput shortens recovery periods, raises near-term rates, and creates a new affordability fight. Utilities should be careful what they ask for on stranded asset protection.

Line extension allowance reform is the underrated near-certainty. The Commission has already ordered the study and flagged other states ending the practice.

Longer term, five years and beyond.

Two scenarios.

Scenario A, planning-first regime. Capital approval increasingly precedes investment, alternatives screening becomes a standard expectation, and utilities earn on approved plans with performance conditions attached. This scenario becomes more likely if the Commission continues using existing authority to impose planning and reporting requirements and if the General Assembly shows limited interest in prescribing rate outcomes directly.

Scenario B, legislative constraints. Affordability pressure prompts the General Assembly to impose more prescriptive requirements, potentially including rate caps, securitization tools for stranded gas assets, or restrictions on new gas line extensions. This scenario becomes more likely if customer bills rise sharply, arrearages worsen, or a broader coalition forms around direct legislative intervention.

An underexamined risk. If the Commission continues reducing requested recovery while also requiring accelerated work, utilities may face a widening gap between mandated investment and authorized recovery. Depending on the magnitude and duration of that gap, the effects could include credit pressure, smaller capital plans, delayed projects, or litigation. Peoples Gas’s proposed Section 8-406 CPCN modification, which would require Commission action on urgent safety infrastructure within 180 days, is an early indicator of that tension.

Plain-language script. In the near term, the most likely outcome is that the ICC approves less than Peoples Gas’s current request and keeps the authorized return near recent Illinois gas benchmarks. The final Future of Gas report could then shape legislation on long-term gas planning, alternatives, cost-benefit review, depreciation, and new-line subsidies. Over time, Illinois could move toward one of two models: a planning-first system in which projects are approved in advance with performance conditions, or a more prescriptive legislative system driven by affordability pressure. The risk to watch is a widening gap between work the state requires and recovery the Commission allows, because that could affect credit, project timing, capital plans, or litigation.

Section 11. Affordability Across Illinois

The affordability problem is not primarily a commodity problem. It is a delivery charge problem driven by capital recovery.

That means relief can only come from four places: slowing capital deployment, lengthening recovery periods, lowering authorized returns, or subsidizing low income customers directly.

Illinois has deployed the fourth lever aggressively. Peoples Gas customers can receive up to an 83 percent low income discount. In November 2025 the Commission went further, directing both Nicor and Ameren to maintain a 3 percent energy burden for all customers by adjusting the low income discount, explicitly citing 3 percent as a nationally recognized affordability measure.

That 3 percent energy burden standard is a structural development that deserves more attention than it has received. It converts affordability from a rhetorical argument into a measurable compliance target, and once a number is in an order, it tends to migrate.

For every Illinois utility, the practical evidentiary burden is increasing. Demonstrating that an investment is prudent may no longer be sufficient on its own. The emerging expectation is that the utility also show why the proposed approach is preferable to reasonable alternatives and quantify the resulting customer bill impacts.

Plain-language script. The affordability problem is increasingly tied to delivery charges and long-lived capital investment, not just the price of gas or electricity. Regulators can respond by slowing investment, spreading recovery over a longer period, lowering returns, or providing direct assistance. Illinois has relied heavily on low-income discounts and has now used a 3 percent energy-burden target for Nicor and Ameren. That turns affordability into something measurable. Going forward, utilities should expect to show not only that an investment is reasonable, but also what it will do to customer bills and why the chosen approach is better than realistic alternatives.

Section 13. Glossary

Rate base. Net original cost of utility plant in service plus working capital, less accumulated depreciation and accumulated deferred income taxes. The asset base on which a utility earns a return.

Return on equity, ROE. The authorized percentage return applied to the equity portion of the capital structure. The primary driver of shareholder earnings and the most contested number in any rate case.

Equity ratio. The share of the capital structure funded by equity rather than debt. Nicor’s approved ratio in November 2025 was 50.00 percent.

Revenue requirement. The total annual revenue a utility must collect to cover operating expenses, depreciation, taxes, and a return on rate base.

Regulatory lag. The delay between when a utility spends capital and when it begins recovering that capital in rates. Functions as natural spending discipline. Rider QIP removed it.

Rider QIP, Qualifying Infrastructure Plant surcharge. Created by Public Act 98-57 in 2013 under Section 9-220.3. Allowed gas utilities to recover qualifying infrastructure investment through an automatic surcharge. Sunset at the end of 2023. Nicor’s equivalent tariff is Rider 32.

CI/DI. Cast iron and ductile iron pipe. The specific asset class the February 2025 ICC order directed Peoples Gas to retire by 2035.

PHMSA. The federal Pipeline and Hazardous Materials Safety Administration, whose 2011 Call to Action following San Bruno drove national distribution integrity programs.

Prudence standard. The legal test under the Public Utilities Act. Costs are recoverable only if the utility demonstrates they were reasonable and prudently incurred. The burden of proof rests on the utility.

Disallowance. A Commission determination that specific costs may not be recovered from customers. Frequently produces an earnings charge at the parent company level.

Multi-year integrated grid plan, MYIGP. The CEJA-required electric distribution plan that utilities file with the Commission, consolidated with a multi-year rate plan and subject to performance-based reconciliation.

Non-pipeline alternative, NPA. Any measure that defers or avoids gas infrastructure investment, including targeted efficiency, electrification, thermal networks, or enhanced monitoring in place of replacement.

Low income discount, LID. Tariffed bill reduction for qualifying customers. Peoples Gas offers up to 83 percent. The Commission adopted a 3 percent energy burden target for Nicor and Ameren in November 2025.

Energy burden. Household energy cost as a percentage of household income. Three percent is the nationally recognized affordability threshold the Commission adopted.

Plain-language script. The glossary provides the vocabulary behind the policy debate. Rate base is the pool of approved assets on which a utility can earn a return. ROE is the authorized return on the equity share of that investment. Regulatory lag is the normal delay between spending and recovery, which Rider QIP reduced. A disallowance keeps identified costs out of customer rates. The MYIGP is Ameren Illinois’s multi-year electric distribution plan. Non-pipeline alternatives are measures that can defer or avoid gas infrastructure. Energy burden measures household energy costs as a share of income, with 3 percent now serving as an important Illinois affordability reference point.

Section 15. Closing Perspective

The Peoples Gas record is not only a story about aging pipe. It shows what can happen when a capital recovery mechanism moves faster than the governance, planning, and affordability controls surrounding it.

The engineering case for retiring nineteenth century cast iron under a dense city was and remains sound. What failed was the discipline around how fast, at what cost, in what order, and with what proof of benefit.

The benchmark record supports a broader conclusion. Across multiple Illinois utility proceedings, the Commission has repeatedly asked for evidence of need, timing, pace, alternatives, and customer impact. Utilities that address those questions in the initial filing are better positioned than those that respond only after the record exposes a gap.

For the MYIGP and future return requests, Ameren Illinois should not rely on being different from Peoples Gas. The stronger posture is to demonstrate, project by project, that the company has already incorporated the standards the Commission is applying: a documented need, a defensible schedule, consideration of reasonable alternatives, measurable customer benefits, and a clear affordability analysis. Government relations should reinforce that record with consistent messages on reliability, economic development, and cost discipline and should adjust its strategy as the identified regulatory and legislative indicators develop.

Plain-language script. The ultimate lesson is not that infrastructure investment is unnecessary. The aging-pipe safety problem was real and remains real. The failure was allowing spending and recovery to move faster than planning, oversight, affordability controls, and proof of results. For Ameren Illinois, the strongest response is not simply to say that we are different from Peoples Gas. It is to show, for every major project, the need, schedule, alternatives, customer benefit, and bill impact. Government relations can then support that record with a consistent explanation of reliability, economic development, affordability, and cost discipline.
