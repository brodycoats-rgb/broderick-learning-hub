The process in one minute

  • Both chambers must pass identical text. If the second chamber changes a bill, the first must agree or the two negotiate.
  • A bill is read by title on three different days in each chamber. That sets a floor on how fast it can move.
  • Passing takes a majority of everyone elected, not just those present. That is 60 in the House and 30 in the Senate.
  • A bill must reach the Governor within 30 days of passing, and the Governor has 60 days to act. A bill not returned becomes law.
  • An override takes three fifths, which is 71 House votes and 36 Senate votes.
  • After May 31 timing changes. A bill passed later takes effect no earlier than June 1 of the next year unless three fifths vote for an earlier date.
  • The Constitution requires only reasonable public notice of committee meetings. It sets no number of hours, which is why notice can be very short.
Say it plainlyA bill gets filed, a committee hears it, and the full chamber votes. Then the other chamber does the same. If they change it, the first chamber has to agree to the changes. After that it goes to the Governor, who can sign it, veto it, or change it. The steps are written down. What is not written down is who decides when a bill actually gets called, and that is where we pay the most attention.

Who is who

The House has 118 members who serve two year terms. The Senate has 59 members, each elected from a district that holds two House districts.

Say it plainlyThink of it as a series of gates with a person at each one. The sponsor carries the bill. The committee chair decides if it gets a hearing. Rules in the House and Assignments in the Senate decide where it goes. Leadership decides whether it ever gets called, and staff often write the actual wording. When someone asks who to call, the honest answer is whoever is standing at the next gate.

The year and the four kinds of session

SessionWhat happensVote rule
Regular, springConvenes in January and normally adjourns May 31. Most bills pass here.Simple majority. After May 31 three fifths for an early effective date.
Veto session, fallA short return, usually in two parts, for vetoed bills and other measures.Three fifths for any bill that should take effect right away.
Lame duck, JanuaryAfter the election and before the new General Assembly is seated.A simple majority is enough, even for an immediate effective date.
SpecialCalled by the Governor, or by both presiding officers together.Business is limited to the stated purpose.

The Constitution says the General Assembly convenes each year on the second Wednesday of January. When someone says a deal might slide to January, they usually mean the votes are easier to find then.

Bills other than spending and revision bills must stick to one subject, and spending bills may deal only with spending. That is a common basis for challenging a bill stuffed with unrelated changes.

Behind the scenes, in practice

Shell and vehicle bills

A shell is a placeholder with little content. Its job is to be alive and in the right place. An amendment later replaces its contents. The bill number you track may never carry the final language, and a bill with an unrelated title may suddenly carry the one you care about.

The amendment is the real bill

In the final stretch the amendment matters more than the bill. Read amendments the day they appear and compare them word by word. Small changes in definitions are often where costs and duties move.

Leadership and the caucus

A bill can have a sponsor, a hearing, and good arguments and still not move because it is not a leadership priority. Watch what leaders say about timing.

Working groups and deals

Major bills often take shape among leadership, the Governor's office, and stakeholders. The public record lags the real negotiation, and membership can change.

Counting votes

Sponsors count before they call a bill. A vote that looks sudden is usually the end of a long count. When you hear a bill is moving, ask whether the votes are there.

Timing games

An election, veto session, or a January lame duck can change who votes and how many votes are needed. Many deals are timed around those moments.

What this means for us. Do not rely on the bill title or number alone. Read the wording. Know the sponsor, the chair, and the staff drafting. Build relationships before a crunch, because staff return calls from people they already know. Keep notes on past sessions.

Speed and notice

Committees must give "reasonable public notice of meetings, including a statement of subjects to be considered." No hours are set. Each chamber has its own notice rules, historically longer than the Constitution, and chambers can shorten or suspend them. In the 2026 data center push, hearings were posted with as little as one hour of notice.

Ten questions for any bill

  1. Who is the sponsor, and are they a leader or in a working group?
  2. Is this a real bill or a shell or vehicle? What was in it before?
  3. Where is it on the path, and what is the next formal step?
  4. What exactly changed since the last version?
  5. Who pays, and who carries the risk if a project falls through?
  6. Does it touch the ICC's say over rates or customer choice law?
  7. Does it treat MISO and PJM the same, and does that matter?
  8. Does it affect a customer or project we are working with?
  9. Who is for and against, and who has not spoken?
  10. How many votes does it need, and does it need three fifths?

The one page summary

Fill it for every high priority bill and refresh it when wording or status changes. Bill, sponsor, and chamber. Real bill or vehicle. What it does in two sentences. Where it is and the next date. Who it affects. Who pays and who carries the risk. Questions for Legal and the ICC. MISO and PJM effect. Who supports and opposes. Our position and how we know. Backup wording. Owner and next update.

Labels on every bill. How we know it (confirmed public, internal reporting, GR assessment, working baseline). Priority (high, medium, low). Position (support, monitor, neutral, lean oppose, oppose, or waiting on the experts).
