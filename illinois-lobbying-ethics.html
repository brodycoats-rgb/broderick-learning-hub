Illinois Lobbying Ethics and Reporting

In Illinois, a government relations director's credibility starts with compliance. The rules are detailed, the reporting is frequent, and the state's history means every interaction is judged closely.

A working guide to registration, reporting, the gift ban, campaign finance, the revolving door, and the utility-specific rules. It is a study aid, not legal advice. Confirm specifics with the Secretary of State, the State Board of Elections and your company's counsel.

The rulebook at a glance

25 ILCS 170

Lobbyist Registration Act

Who must register, what they disclose, and the semi-monthly expenditure reports. Administered by the Secretary of State.

5 ILCS 430

State Officials and Employees Ethics Act

The gift ban, revolving door limits and inspector general oversight for state officials and employees, including legislators.

10 ILCS 5/9

Election Code, Article 9

Campaign contribution limits, disclosure and the ban on fundraisers during session. Administered by the State Board of Elections.

Public Utilities Act

Utility ethics under CEJA

Annual ethics and compliance reporting to the Illinois Commerce Commission, added after the 2020 ComEd case.

Registration

WhenBefore doing any lobbying, and no later than 2 business days after being employed or retained. Renew every year by January 31.
Fee$300 a year, nonrefundable.
What you discloseContact information, each client, each consultant, the actions you will lobby on, every state agency and local government you expect to lobby, your client's business category, and any public office you hold. Individuals also provide a photo.
ConsultantsIf you retain a consultant, amend your registration before the work starts. A consultant who only advises does not register, but one who contacts an official or spends money on one must register as a lobbyist. Contingent fees are banned for consultants too.
Local lobbyingSince 2022, lobbying a county, city or township for pay requires state registration, for both the individual and the employer. Chicago keeps its own rules as well.
Grassroots lobbyingPaying to urge others to contact officials can trigger registration. A participant can avoid it by reporting its costs to the registered sponsor.
Harassment policyEvery registrant must have a sexual harassment policy, provide it within 2 business days on request, and accept inspector general jurisdiction over complaints.

Expenditure reporting

The schedule

Reports are filed twice a month with the Secretary of State.

  • The 1st through the 15th is due by the 20th of that month
  • The 16th through month-end is due by the 5th of the next month
Your next two deadlines

What goes on the report

  • Every lobbying expenditure, with no minimum dollar threshold
  • Itemized by official, client, amount, description, vendor and location, date and subject
  • Categories: travel and lodging, meals and entertainment, gifts, honoraria, anything else of value
  • Spending by your consultants for you
  • Receptions with 25 or more officials invited: total cost, date and estimated attendance only

Also required. File even when there is nothing to report. Notify each official named within 30 days after the filing deadline. Keep receipts for 2 years. Late or incomplete reports are violations.

Not included: salaries and fees, your own travel, meals and office costs, and anything reimbursed before the deadline.

The gift ban

State officials, employees and their household family members may not accept gifts from a prohibited source. That includes anyone registered or required to register as a lobbyist, anyone seeking state business, and anyone regulated by or affected by the official's duties. For a utility lobbyist, assume every legislator and agency official is covered.

Key exceptionsLimit
Food and refreshmentsUp to $75 per person in a single day, eaten where it was bought or prepared, or catered.
Low-value itemsLess than $100 in total from one source in a calendar year.
Personal friendshipAllowed unless the official has reason to believe it was given because of the office.
Political contributions and eventsLawful contributions and activities at political fundraisers.
Same as the publicBenefits offered to the public on identical terms.
Market valueAnything the official pays full price for.
Educational materials and missions, meeting travelFor state business, as defined by ethics commission rules.
Family, outside employment, government gifts, bequestsNarrow exceptions defined in the statute.
An official who receives a prohibited gift can return it, donate it to a 501(c)(3) charity, or donate its value, and should document it. The easier path is never to offer one.

Campaign finance and fundraising

Contribution limits in effect since January 1, 2025. The State Board of Elections adjusts them for inflation every odd-numbered year, so new limits arrive January 1, 2027.

Individual$7,300per election cycle to a candidate committee
Corporation, labor union or association$14,600per election cycle to a candidate committee
Political action committee$72,800per election cycle to a candidate committee

The session fundraising ban

Since 2022, legislators and their committees may not hold fundraisers anywhere in Illinois during the regular session, the fall veto session or any special session, or on the day before a session begins. Special-session fundraisers are exempt only if scheduled at least 14 days in advance.

For anyone who plans events, check the session calendar first, and check it again when leaders add days.

Keep the political separate

  • No company time, email, phones or facilities for campaign work unless counsel approves
  • No policy asks at fundraisers, and no fundraising talk in official meetings
  • Contributions follow the company's PAC rules and approvals
  • Every event is logged with compliance

Revolving door and conflicts

Legislators who leave mid-term

May not do registrable state lobbying until their term would have ended or six months after leaving, whichever comes first.

State employees on contracts

One-year ban on joining contractors where they took part in awarding or administering contracts of $25,000 or more.

Officials lobbying other governments

Legislators and statewide officers may not lobby local governments for pay on behalf of registered lobbyists. Local officials may not lobby their own body for pay.

Contingent fees

No lobbyist or consultant may be paid based on the outcome of a bill or action.

The utility-specific layer

  • In July 2020 ComEd entered a deferred prosecution agreement with federal prosecutors, paid a $200 million fine, and admitted arranging jobs and payments for allies of the former House Speaker to influence him.
  • Its commitments included tracking anything of value given to public officials, monitoring third-party lobbyists and consultants, barring lobbyists from subcontracting, and requiring a compliance and ethics officer to approve those contracts.
  • A federal judge dismissed the charge on July 17, 2023, after prosecutors told the court ComEd had fully complied with the agreement.
  • CEJA added ethics requirements for Illinois utilities, including annual ethics and compliance reports to the ICC. ComEd's reports describe updated lobbying and political activity policies, expanded disclosure of political contributions, and a supply chain compliance team.
  • Advocates continue to push further. In 2024, SB 2885 and HB 5061 proposed barring utilities from charging customers for charitable contributions, trade association dues, goodwill advertising and political contributions. Check the current status before citing them.
The lesson for any utility GR team is that the rules on paper were not enough. What changed after 2020 was systems: tracking, approvals, contract terms and reporting that make the right behavior automatic.

A director's compliance system

Seven habits that turn the rules into how the team works every day.

1

Pre-clear

Any event, meal or gift involving an official goes to compliance first. Same rule for every person on the team.

2

Log every contact

Contract lobbyists file a short contact report after every meeting: who, what, any follow-up, anything of value.

3

Own the calendar

Registration renewals by January 31, semi-monthly reports on the 5th and 20th, session-day fundraising blackouts, and training deadlines all on one shared calendar.

4

Write it into contracts

Every contract lobbyist and consultant agreement includes disclosure, no subcontracting, no contingent fees, policy compliance and audit rights.

5

Separate the political

Fundraisers and contributions run through the political program, never through official meetings, company resources or business hours.

6

Make asking easy

A one-line rule for the team: if you are unsure, stop and ask compliance. Nobody is ever criticized for asking.

7

Train and test

Annual ethics and harassment training for everyone, plus a short scenario review before each session.

Test yourself

Twelve real-world situations. Choose allowed, not allowed, or depends, then see why.

Talking about ethics in an interview

Thirty seconds, confident and specific.

I treat ethics as a system, not a judgment call. I know the rules: registration before any contact, semi-monthly reports, the $75 food limit, and the statewide session fundraising ban. More important, I build habits so the whole team follows them, like pre-clearing anything involving an official, contact reports from every contract lobbyist, and one shared compliance calendar. I've coordinated political fundraising events for years within those rules, and I keep it clean by letting the process do the work.
