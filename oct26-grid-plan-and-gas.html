Grid Plans and the Future of Gas Study Guide

Two ICC tracks that will set up the next session

Why it matters

Two long running regulatory tracks will shape the next legislative session. The multi-year grid plans decide how utilities invest and recover costs. The future of gas work decides how fast gas service could change. Both are mostly settled by the ICC rather than by lawmakers, but lawmakers will react.

Multi-year grid plans

  • The ICC asked Ameren Illinois and ComEd to file new large load tariffs, tied to the same planning discussion.
  • An administrative law judge issue with preliminary findings was reported on the Ameren side. ComEd had no immediate update.
  • Final rulings are expected by December 2026.
  • A reported guideline is that utilities hold back on formal outreach about plan impacts until final orders issue, then legislative outreach begins. One follow up conversation was unsure what a closed window meant, so confirm the actual rule with regulatory counsel.

Message discipline lesson

A planning call reviewed a handout for policymakers. Three lessons came out of it. First, if no bill is pending, asking legislators to support something in theory can sound like asking them to take a position. Second, a short fact based one pager informs better than a strategy heavy card. Third, points such as cost recovery land badly with general audiences unless they are framed around customer benefit.

Future of gas

  • Proposals to require a full gas utility phase-out by 2050 were pushed back and moderated.
  • The ICC Chairman will publish an advisory report with recommendations, not a binding mandate.
  • The working group plans to steer toward aspirational targets rather than hard statutory bans.
  • Core tension is long term decarbonization against winter heating and reliability.

Background, what a multi-year grid plan is

A multi-year integrated grid plan is a roadmap for how a utility will spend on the wires side of the system over several years. It covers things such as pole and line replacement, substations, automation, storms and resilience, connecting rooftop and community solar, batteries and electric vehicle charging, and tools that give customers more control over use. The regulator reviews whether the spending is needed, whether it is the right size, and how the money gets recovered from customers.

Why it matters to lawmakers
It is the largest single driver of future delivery charges on a bill. Legislators who care about affordability and reliability both have a stake in how it comes out.
Why timing is awkward
Final ICC orders are expected by December 2026. Until then, utilities are limited in what they can say publicly about the effect on rates, so outreach waits.
What is in dispute
Meetings mentioned an administrative law judge issue that touched one utility's filing, while the other had no immediate update. The details were not in the summary.
What happens after
Once final orders exist, the plan is to start legislative outreach and explain the investments and the rate effects in plain terms.

Reading a docket without getting lost

  1. Petition or filing. The utility files its plan and testimony.
  2. Intervenors. Consumer groups, industrial customers, environmental groups and others file their own views.
  3. Hearings. Witnesses are cross examined before an administrative law judge.
  4. Proposed order. The judge issues a proposal. Parties file exceptions to it.
  5. Final order. The Commission votes. After that, parties may seek rehearing or appeal.
  6. Implementation. The utility files tariffs and reports that put the order in effect.

The meetings referred to preliminary findings and to waiting for final orders, which puts the plans somewhere between steps 4 and 5.

Future of gas, the policy menu

The question under discussion is how a gas utility system fits a decarbonizing state. The meetings said a proposal for a full phase out of gas utilities by 2050 had been pushed back and moderated. The ICC chair is expected to publish an advisory report instead of imposing a binding phase out. The working group wants to steer any legislation toward aspirational targets, not a hard ban.

ToolBinding?StrengthWeakness
Statutory phase out dateYesClear signal to investors and customersHard to square with heating reliability, high political and legal risk
Aspirational targetNoAllows planning and flexibilityCan be ignored, critics will call it weak
Advisory report with recommendationsNoBuilds a record and keeps options openRecommendations can harden into expectations
Pilot programs such as thermal energy networks or hybrid heat pumpsDepends on rulesTests real world cost and performanceSmall scale, results take years
Rate design and cost recovery changesYes, through the ICCDirectly affects customer billsComplex, litigated, can shift costs among customers

The ask ladder, in practice

An earlier model on this page walked through the ask ladder. In short, when you meet a legislator, ask for the smallest useful step first. Reading a one pager before the December orders is a smaller ask than meeting, which is smaller than attending an event, which is smaller than supporting language. Where gas and the grid meet

Gas matters for two separate reasons. Gas utilities serve heating customers, which is the future of gas proceeding. Gas fired power plants supply peak electricity, which is the capacity topic. One speaker tied the two together, saying that a push to shut down gas raises the chance that industrial load is the first to be shed in a stressed system. Keep the two issues separate when you talk, or listeners will blend them.

One minute, on the future of gas

Many customers heat their homes with gas and they need that to keep working. The Commission chair is expected to publish recommendations that guide the discussion without setting a hard deadline. We think goals and pilots make sense, and a binding ban does not, because we need to protect reliability and keep bills in check while technology develops.

One minute, handing out the postcard

This is a short summary of why we think the state should keep investing in a reliable, modern grid. There is no bill attached to it. I would be glad to answer questions, and I have a one page fact sheet if you want the numbers.

More questions you may get

Why can't you tell me what the grid plan will do to my rates?

The proceeding is still open and the utility is limited in what it can say before final orders. Once the orders are issued, the numbers are public and we can walk through them.

Is the grid plan a done deal?

No. The Commission has not issued final orders. An administrative law judge issue affected one filing, and orders are expected by December.

Will gas be banned?

No binding ban is on the table in what was described. The ICC chair is expected to publish an advisory report, and the working group favors aspirational goals.

What is a thermal energy network?

A shared underground loop of water that buildings tap for heating and cooling with heat pumps. It was mentioned as one of the pilot options in the gas proceeding.

Why a postcard instead of a bill summary?

The postcard is meant to explain why investment matters in general, not to push a bill, which is why reviewers worried it could confuse people.

Who decides if outreach is allowed?

Each organization sets its own policy, and rules for commissioners are stricter than for legislators. The meeting left the closed window question open for follow up.
