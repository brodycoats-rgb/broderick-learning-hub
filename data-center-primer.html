The short version

Six ideas that explain most of the Illinois debate.

In plain words

A data center is a building full of computers that needs steady, very large amounts of electricity and a lot of cooling. One large campus can use as much power as a small city. For a utility like Ameren Illinois that is both an opportunity and a risk. More electricity sold spreads the fixed cost of the grid across more kilowatt hours, which can hold down bills. But the utility builds lines and substations years before the customer shows up, and if the customer never comes, or leaves, someone else pays. Almost every Illinois bill is an attempt to answer one question. Who carries the risk, and what does the state get in return?

What a data center is and how it uses power

The vocabulary and the machine, in one picture.

In plain words

Think of a data center as a factory where the product is computing. Electricity goes in, almost all of it comes out as heat, and a second system has to carry that heat away. Everything a bill cares about, power supply, water, noise, land and cost, comes from those two flows.

How power flows through the building

The gridHigh voltage lines bring power in. This is where our interconnection studies and upgrades start.
→
SubstationSteps voltage down. For the largest campuses the customer needs its own, and it can take 30 to 36 months to energize.7
→
Backup powerBatteries and generators keep the computers on during an outage. Diesel generators bring air permits into the picture.
→
Servers and chipsThe useful work. This is called the IT load. AI chips draw far more power per rack than older servers.
→
HeatAlmost all the electricity turns into heat that must be removed.
→
CoolingChillers, cooling towers or liquid cooling. This is where water use comes from.

Power usage effectiveness, or PUE, compares total power to the power used by the computers. A PUE of 1.2 means 20 percent extra goes to cooling and other overhead. Efficient modern sites are often reported near 1.1 to 1.3, though older sites run higher. Rack power figures are industry rules of thumb, and AI racks draw many times what traditional racks do.

Why AI changed the game

AI chips draw far more power per rack than traditional servers and run at high, steady loads, so campuses are larger and denser than a decade ago. Berkeley Lab found that US data center electricity use more than doubled from 2017 to 2023, largely because of AI servers, and projects it could double or triple again by 2028.1

In Illinois the data centers are concentrated in the Chicago suburbs, where fiber, land and power were already in place. Newer projects are moving outward, to DeKalb, Joliet and Sangamon County, and Ameren Illinois' territory is where the next wave may land. How big is the wave, and how real is it

National forecast, the Illinois pipeline, and the gap between requests and reality.

In plain words

Everyone agrees demand is rising fast. The disagreement is how much of the requested power will actually be built. A request in the queue is not a customer. Developers file for the same project in several places, and projects change or die. That gap is why utilities ask for deposits and minimum bills, and why the loosest bills worry us.

How big tech is buying power, and what the Clinton deal means for Illinois

The contracts behind the headlines, and the three ways power gets sold to a very large customer.

In plain words

There are three ways a data center gets power. It can sign a long term contract with a power plant, like Meta at Clinton. It can become a customer of its local utility under a large load tariff, like the contracts Ameren Missouri signed. Or it can build its own supply next to the building. Each puts cost, risk and control in different hands. Illinois bills try to steer which route a data center takes.

Why Clinton matters in Illinois

The Clinton nuclear plant in central Illinois was nearly closed in 2017 and was kept open with a state zero emission credit that customers pay through their bills. That credit expires in 2027. Meta's 20 year purchase of the plant's output, starting June 2027, is designed to replace it, and Constellation says the plant will run without ratepayer support. The plant has one reactor, about 1,062 MW, and Constellation says it preserves more than 1,000 jobs and about $13.5 million a year in tax revenue.14 15 22

Questions the deal raises

Does a contract between a data center and a plant change who serves that load locally? Does it keep supply on the Illinois grid, or move the output to one buyer? What happens to capacity prices when a plant is committed to a single customer? None of these are answered in public reports, so they are worth asking Constellation and the regulators.

What the nuclear deals can and cannot do

  • Restarts and life extensions are the nearest term option, and still need about three years of licensing and interconnection work.
  • New reactors typically take 10 to 15 years, and no US small reactor operates at commercial scale yet.
  • The AI build is much faster than nuclear, which is why gas and solar still carry most near term growth.
  • Hyperscalers prefer to buy power rather than own plants, which leaves utilities as the main builders.

Building supply next to the data center, called behind the meter or co-location, is what the bring your own energy bills are reaching toward. It has a regulatory catch. Power supply, the grid and the market

Where the electricity comes from, what it costs to be ready, and why a flat load is hard to serve with clean power.

In plain words

Ameren Illinois delivers electricity but does not own the power plants in Illinois. Power is bought through the regional market and state procurement. A data center changes two things. It raises the demand the regional grid operator must plan for, and it needs wires and substations built to reach it. When demand outruns supply, the price paid just to have power available, called the capacity price, goes up and every customer pays more.

Capacity prices, dollars per megawatt day

MISO and PJM design their auctions differently and report different periods, so compare the direction, not the levels. MISO prices fell in 2026-27 because new solar, gas, storage and imports arrived. PJM, which serves ComEd, cleared at its cap, about triple the prior cycle.4,7

Why hourly clean matching is hard (illustration)

Solar produces a bell shape. A data center draws power around the clock. Annual matching only needs the daily totals to balance. Hourly matching, as in HB5826, requires clean supply in every hour, which is why it favors nuclear and storage and penalizes shortfalls.

Environment, water and land

Emissions, water, heat, noise and what the Illinois bills try to do about them.

In plain words

Environmental questions come in two kinds. Global ones, like emissions from the power plants that serve the load, depend on where the electricity comes from. Local ones, like water, noise and heat, depend on the site and the cooling design. Local issues are what neighbors show up to meetings about, and they can stop a project that already has power.

Taxes and state incentives

What Illinois offers, what it has committed so far, and what host communities collect.

In plain words

Illinois exempts data centers from sales and use taxes on equipment, which is the big tax break, and adds a credit on construction wages in some areas. The state estimates the value at about 6.25 percent of the money a company pledges to invest. Local governments, meanwhile, collect property tax, which is where the large local benefit comes from. The state paused new incentive agreements as of July 1, 2026.2

What it means for a utility like Ameren Illinois

The upside, the exposure, and the practical limits.

In plain words

For a regulated delivery utility, a data center is a new, very large customer. The upside is sales growth that spreads fixed grid costs, investment in wires and substations that earn a regulated return, and economic development in communities we serve. The exposure is equally real. We build early, requests are speculative, tariffs and bills are politically sensitive, and a customer that leaves leaves the cost behind. That is why our SME feedback keeps coming back to the same tests.

Upside

  • More kilowatt hours to spread fixed costs, which can put downward pressure on rates.
  • New wires and substations that are part of regulated investment.
  • Jobs and tax base in communities we serve, especially downstate.
  • A large, steady customer that improves how well the grid is used.

Exposure

  • Stranded cost if a project cancels or shrinks after upgrades are built.
  • Queue congestion from low commitment requests.
  • Capacity price pressure if supply does not keep up.
  • Affordability and political risk if customers feel they are subsidizing the load.
  • Losing the load to co-ops and municipal utilities if our terms are the toughest.

Questions that cut through the noise

Use these when someone quotes a number or a promise.

On demand

  • Is this a request, a study, or a signed agreement?
  • How many megawatts, and is that peak or average?
  • What happens to the forecast if half of it never arrives?

On cost

  • Who pays if the customer leaves in year five?
  • Is the cost allocated to the data center or spread to everyone?
  • Which protections apply, and for how long?

On supply and clean energy

  • Is the supply new, or relabeled existing power?
  • Does it work in every hour, or only on an annual average?
  • What does it do to capacity prices?

On water and land

  • Is the cooling closed loop, evaporative or liquid?
  • Where does the water come from, and who else uses it?
  • What is the noise and truck traffic plan?

On taxes and jobs

  • How many permanent jobs, and at what wage?
  • What is the exemption worth, and what do local governments collect?
  • What happens when the incentive ends?

On the bill

  • What does the bill define, and what does it leave out?
  • Does it work for a 5 MW site and a 500 MW site?
  • What will the ICC or the IPA still have to decide later?
