A plain language course and working reference

September 24, 2026

Understand the system. Follow the money. Read the decision. Explain the customer impact.

This knowledge center teaches Illinois utility regulation through the work of Ameren Illinois. Eighteen learning modules connect the physical energy system, company responsibilities, ICC proceedings, utility economics, customer bills, affordability, and government affairs. Each module includes a lesson, an Ameren application or study tool, and ten multiple-choice questions with explained answers: 180 questions in total.

Begin with Modules 1–4 for the foundations. Use Modules 5–12 to understand major proceedings and technologies. Modules 13–16 connect customer needs with practical analysis and communication. Module 17 builds vocabulary, and Module 18 applies the material in a capstone.

Your learning cycle

Read → work the example → explain it aloud → answer ten questions → review the explanations → revisit missed concepts.

Set aside roughly 25–40 minutes per module, with extra time for unfamiliar topics. Answer before looking at the key. Mark each answer as confident or uncertain. A correct guess still deserves review. Use the clickable contents and Word's Navigation Pane to move between modules; bracketed source numbers link to the reading library.

Four labels to keep straight

Established fact: supported by the identified source and date. Company position: what Ameren proposes, reports, or argues. Analysis: a reasoned interpretation that depends on stated assumptions. Hypothetical example: invented numbers or circumstances used for learning, not an actual tariff, project forecast, customer quote, or official company position.

The dated facts in this edition are a September 24, 2026 learning snapshot. Current tariffs and later orders govern current applications. Examples marked hypothetical teach a method rather than predict a bill. Future milestones are described as scheduled or required actions, not as completed outcomes.

1The electricity system and the institutions around itWho does what, from plant to meter
Retrieve first. A local transformer fails while the region has plenty of generation. Which of the three reliability problems is that?

Power travels from generation over high-voltage transmission to substations, then over local distribution lines to the meter. Ameren Illinois is primarily a regulated delivery utility in MISO, while ComEd is in PJM. The General Assembly, ICC, IPA, FERC, PJM and MISO each decide different things.

Key takeawayA broken local line needs a local repair; a regional shortage needs a resource response. Name the problem, the legal entity (AIC, ATXI or Ameren Missouri) and the institution before you assign a cost or a fix.

2How an ICC proceeding becomes a decisionDocket, record, order, appeal
Retrieve first. What does "testimony filed" not establish? What does "proposed order" not establish?

A docket is the numbered case record. A matter moves from a petition through an administrative law judge, discovery, testimony, hearing and briefs to a proposed order and a final order, with compliance, rehearing or appeal possible afterward. Staff recommends; the Commissioners decide.

Key takeawayA docket number tells you where the case record is, not whether the request was approved. Highlight the verb: requested, recommended, ordered, appealed or implemented.

Read the full module in the reference

3How utility spending becomes a customer chargeCapital, annual revenue, monthly bill
Retrieve first. Hypothetical: a $100 million substation, 40-year recovery, 8 percent return on a $100 million rate base. What is year-one depreciation plus return?

A capital project is recovered over time. The annual revenue requirement covers operating expenses, depreciation, taxes and an allowed return on rate base. That revenue is then allocated among customer classes and collected through rate design, which is what finally reaches a monthly bill.

Key takeawayA $100 million construction program and a $100 million annual rate increase are different claims. Keep three boxes: capital invested, annual dollars authorized, monthly dollars for a defined customer.

Read the full module in the reference

4The laws and plans that organize the workLaw, plan, implementation chain
Retrieve first. Which plan answers "What should change on the distribution system?"

FEJA, CEJA and CRGA set obligations and authority, but each decision runs through specific sections, plans and Commission actions. Grid plans, rate cases, the statewide IRP, IPA procurement plans and gas plans answer different questions and remain distinct approvals.

Key takeawayAsk "What is the decision?" before "What is the docket number?" A plan that recommends storage still needs rules, contracts, permits, interconnection, financing, construction and testing.

Read the full module in the reference

5Multi Year Integrated Grid PlansNeeds, alternatives, timing, equity
Retrieve first. An $18 million expansion competes with $5 million of equipment plus a $600,000-a-year demand program for ten years. What would have to be true for the alternative to work?

Ameren Illinois filed its 2028-2031 grid plan petition on January 20, 2026, in Docket 26-0051. The practical question is which investments are justified, when, against what alternatives, and how benefits compare with costs. Project cards turn each investment into a testable claim.

Key takeaway"Supports reliability" is not evidence. Name the reliability problem and how success will be measured. A prior 75 percent reduction applied to the spending that release addressed, not to all Ameren investment or bills.

Read the full module in the reference

6Statewide Integrated Resource PlanningScenarios, dependable capacity
Retrieve first. A 100 MW battery holds 400 MWh of usable energy. About how long can it discharge at 100 MW?

The statewide IRP asks whether a reasonable combination of resources can serve future needs. A scenario is a conditional statement, nameplate capacity is not dependable contribution, and the 2025 Resource Adequacy Study reports conditional regional shortfalls, not scheduled blackouts. Filing starts a review; it is not an approval.

Key takeawayA model shows a need under stated assumptions. It guides action but guarantees neither a shortage nor the success of a particular project.

Read the full module in the reference

7The Future of GasLong-lived network, remaining customers
Retrieve first. Hypothetical: $10 million of fixed cost across 10,000 customers, then 2,000 leave. What does each remaining customer carry on average?

Gas networks are built to last while technology, customer choices and policy change future use. If fixed costs stay while customers leave, remaining customers can face higher charges. Non-pipeline alternatives, zonal electrification and revenue balancing are proposals to test against complete costs, not adopted policy.

Key takeawayLook at the remaining customers as carefully as the early adopters, and compare complete costs over consistent lifetimes, showing who pays each component.

Read the full module in the reference

8Large Loads and Data CentersCredible demand and protections
Retrieve first. Name the four categories of exposure a large customer can create for other customers.

A very large customer may need infrastructure before it takes service. Requested maximum MW, initial MW, average use and committed ramp are four different numbers. Application costs, dedicated facilities, broader network upgrades and regional capacity costs are four different exposures, and a deposit addresses only part of them.

Key takeawayA deposit is one protection. Its amount and terms decide what risk it actually covers. Ask what customer commitments are secured before each construction milestone.

Read the full module in the reference

9Rate Cases and Customer BillsRequested vs approved, price vs use
Retrieve first. Hypothetical: supply goes from 800 kWh at 10 cents to 1,000 kWh at 12 cents, a $40 increase. How much comes from the extra use at the old rate?

A rate case sets the authorized cost of service. A requested amount, an approved amount and a bill impact are different numbers, and a fixed charge, usage charge and demand charge affect customers differently. Read a bill in layers: delivery class, supply, riders and the supplier arrangement.

Key takeawaySeparate price from use before saying a rate rose. A revenue-neutral proposal leaves total collections unchanged but does not leave every customer unaffected.

Read the full module in the reference

10Energy Efficiency and Beneficial ElectrificationSavings, peaks, charging
Retrieve first. Hypothetical fleet: 20 vehicles at 7 kW each charge at once. What is the demand, and what if only half charge at a time?

Efficiency uses less energy for a comparable service; demand response shifts use to particular times. Gross savings are not net savings, annual energy savings are not peak savings, and electrification can raise the electric bill while lowering total household fuel spending. Managed charging links customer behavior to local grid planning.

Key takeawayA rebate count measures participation or transactions, not savings. Ask for verified savings, cost effectiveness and who was reached or turned away.

Read the full module in the reference

11Storage, Renewable Procurement and Virtual Power PlantsAwards, MW vs MWh, aggregation
Retrieve first. A 20 MW / 80 MWh battery discharges at full power for about how many hours before losses and limits?

The August 26, 2026 storage procurement targeted 1,038 MW; the ICC approved results on September 1 supporting 600 MW (520 MW in MISO, 80 MW in PJM). MW is power and MWh is stored energy. A virtual power plant aggregates many small resources, and its dependable output can be lower than the nameplate sum.

Key takeawayAn award is a contract milestone, not operating capacity. A regional storage headline is not an Ameren construction announcement.

12Regional Markets, Transmission and SitingEnergy, capacity, interconnection
Retrieve first. Why can a dollar-per-MW-day capacity price not be read as cents per kWh on a residential bill?

Energy markets pay for electricity supplied, capacity mechanisms pay for being available, and ancillary services support short-interval reliability. Label every result with region, delivery year, product and unit. An interconnection queue is not a list of built facilities, and siting approval is not grid readiness.

Key takeawayThree clocks run at once: regulatory approval, engineering and construction, and the date customers or the region need service. A project can be approved and still miss the third clock.

Read the full module in the reference

13Affordability, Equity and the Household BudgetMatch the tool to the problem
Retrieve first. A household sits just above the LIHEAP limit. Which tools do you check separately?

A high bill can come from price, usage, an inefficient home, or several at once, and ability to pay depends on income and other obligations. Bill assistance, arrearage relief, deposit protections, payment timing and efficiency work on different timelines and are funded differently.

Key takeawayMatch the help to the household problem, and name who funds each tool rather than calling every benefit "money from Ameren."

14How the Proceedings InteractSeveral channels, one customer
Retrieve first. Draw the four linked boxes for a combined campus exercise.

A campus, a neighborhood heating transition and a storage award each touch several proceedings: delivery, supply, regional resources, program charges and customer-side costs. The same project can appear in different documents with different dates or sizes, and programs can claim the same demand reduction twice.

Key takeawayOne docket never resolves every channel through which a customer pays. Name the action needed now and the evidence required before the next commitment.

30 seconds: why a bill changes83 words · about 33 sec spoken

Your bill pays for two main things: the electricity itself, called supply, and the wires and poles that bring it to you, called delivery. Ameren Illinois is primarily a delivery utility, and for customers on its supply service it passes electricity costs through without a markup.

When a number changes, the first question is which part moved and whether the household also used more. The Illinois Commerce Commission reviews delivery costs in a public case. [Confirm current tariff values before quoting any rate.]

2 minutes: from a project to a bill198 words · about 79 sec spoken

Start with what you already know. Your electric bill has more than one part. Supply is the electricity. Delivery is the local grid that carries it to your home. A customer who switches supplier still gets the same local crews to fix a downed line.

Here is how a grid project reaches a bill. A utility builds a long-lived asset, such as a substation, and recovers its cost over time, not in one month. The annual amount it may collect covers operating costs, depreciation, taxes and an allowed return. That annual amount is then divided among customer groups and turned into charges. So a hundred-million-dollar construction program and a hundred-million-dollar annual rate increase are two different claims.

A teaching example, not a real tariff: a household uses 800 kilowatt-hours and supply goes from 8 cents to 10 cents. Supply rises 25 percent, from $64 to $80. If the total bill was $140, it rises to $156, about 11 percent. Same change, two different percentages.

The Commission decides in a public case. Requested is not approved, and approved is not the same as a bill reduction. [Cite the specific docket and its latest order before using any real figure.]

5 minutes: briefing outline for a new audience319 words · about 128 sec spoken

Opening (30 seconds). Say the question you will answer: how does a utility decision become a customer charge, and who decides what? Ask the audience to guess before you give the answer.

Part 1, the physical system (45 seconds). Generation, transmission, substations, distribution, meter. Ameren Illinois is primarily a regulated delivery utility in MISO; ComEd is in PJM. Name the three reliability problems: distribution, transmission and resource adequacy. They need different evidence and different fixes.

Part 2, the money (90 seconds). Capital spending, then annual revenue requirement, then allocation to customer groups, then the monthly bill. Use the hypothetical substation: a $100 million asset, 40-year recovery and 8 percent return gives $2.5 million of depreciation plus $8 million of return in year one, before operating costs and taxes. Say clearly that this is a teaching example. ROE is the return on the equity portion, not a markup on each bill dollar.

Part 3, how to read a decision (60 seconds). A docket number locates the case, not the outcome. Petition, testimony, proposed order and final order are different statuses. Use the gas case 25-0084 as the example: the ICC order reported a $55.8 million reduction from a $128.8 million request, about $73.0 million annual revenue increase, and a 9.60 percent authorized ROE versus 10.70 percent proposed. An appeal notice was later recorded. [Confirm the current docket status with regulatory counsel before external use.]

Part 4, three live topics (60 seconds). Grid plan 26-0051 was filed January 20, 2026. The first statewide IRP is due to be submitted by November 16, 2026, and filing starts a review. The 2026 storage procurement targeted 1,038 MW and supports 600 MW of awards, which are contract milestones, not operating capacity.

Close (15 seconds). Say the decision test: which entity, which stage, which kind of number, which kind of fact. Offer to share the sources. [Clear any named company position with Government Affairs leadership.]

Skeptical Q&A drill

Answer aloud first, then open the card. Items in [brackets] need clearance.

"Ameren is asking for a big increase. Why should I believe it is justified?"

That is a fair question, and the Commission asks it too. A request is not an approval. In the gas case 25-0084 the ICC order reported a $55.8 million reduction from a $128.8 million request and a 9.60 percent ROE instead of 10.70 percent, leaving about $73.0 million as an annual revenue increase. Ameren later filed a notice of appeal, which shows a continuing dispute but not a reversal. The test is the record: what the Commission ordered and what remains contested. [Confirm current status with regulatory counsel.]

"Doesn't the utility just profit off the electricity it passes through?"

For customers on Ameren Illinois supply service, purchased electricity costs are passed through without a markup, and Ameren Illinois is primarily a delivery utility. The delivery side is what the Commission reviews in rate cases. Customers can also choose an alternative supplier, but the local wires and outage response stay with the delivery utility.

"The ICC cut the last grid plan by 75 percent. Isn't this one padded?"

I would not draw that conclusion. The corrected December 2024 release reported $83 million approved from $333 million proposed in the spending it addressed. That is not a cut to all Ameren investment or all bills, and it does not predict the next case. It shows the kind of evidence the Commission expects: a named problem, alternatives and measurable benefits. The 2028-2031 plan is in Docket 26-0051.

"Will data centers raise my bill?"

It depends on what is committed before construction. A very large customer can create four exposures: study costs, underused dedicated facilities, broader network upgrades and regional capacity costs. A deposit covers part of that, so we look at what it secures and when. The ComEd tariff discussed requests of 50 MW or more, but that threshold does not automatically apply to Ameren Illinois. [Use Ameren Illinois tariff terms only after verifying them.]

"A study says a shortfall in 2031. Are the lights going out?"

No. The Resource Adequacy Study describes conditional regional baseline shortfalls: PJM beginning in 2029 and MISO in 2031. New supply, demand changes, transmission and operating measures can change the outcome. It is a reason to evaluate timely solutions, not a forecast of blackouts.

"Why not just build more batteries?"

Batteries help, and the 2026 procurement supports 600 MW of new storage against a 1,038 MW target. But an award is a contract milestone. The project still needs site control, permits, interconnection, financing, construction and testing. A battery also has a power rating (MW) and a stored-energy rating (MWh), so a 50 MW battery with 200 MWh runs about four hours, not all day.

"Is Ameren trying to shut down the gas system?"

The Future of Gas process explores how a long-lived network may adapt. Exploring alternatives does not prohibit service. Ameren's zonal electrification concept, as described in the January 2026 workshop notes, is voluntary and incentive-based and needs economic validation and ICC approval. It is a workshop proposal, not an adopted program. A separate petition, Docket 26-0146, seeks to terminate or suspend the process, which shows it is contested.

"You say supply is a pass-through, so why did my bill go up?"

Let us find which part moved and whether your use changed. Using the teaching example, 800 kWh at 8 cents is $64 of supply; at 10 cents it is $80. Supply rose 25 percent while a $140 total rose to $156, about 11.4 percent. Weather can raise use at the same time, so we compare delivery, supply and riders on the same usage. [Use the customer's actual bill and current tariff.]

"What can a family just above the LIHEAP limit get?"

Check each tool separately, because each has its own rules and funder. Warm Neighbors Cool Friends has a 200-300 percent FPL or county-AMI pathway with account and matching-payment requirements. The Natural Gas Service Discount tiers extend through 300% FPL for qualifying gas accounts. Income Eligible Status can waive deposits and late charges. Efficiency programs may reduce future use. Do not promise approval based on self-reported income. [Confirm enrollment and current terms before referring.]

Module 1 Explain to a neighbor why switching electric suppliers does not change who repairs an Ameren Illinois distribution line. Then explain why a Missouri power plant announcement is not automatically an Illinois delivery investment.

Look for: Delivery responsibility stays with the local utility; supply is a separate arrangement. Ameren Missouri is a different utility with a generation business, so identify the legal entity and activity first.

Module 3 A $10 million asset has $250,000 annual depreciation, $150,000 operating expense and a $700,000 simplified first-year return. Explain what the annual requirement is and what it is not.

Look for: $1.10 million before taxes and adjustments. It is not the $10 million outlay nor $700,000 of guaranteed profit. Equal split across 100,000 customers would be $11 a year (about $0.92 a month), but actual charges use customer classes and billing determinants.

Module 2 In four sentences, describe gas case 25-0084 using the correct verb for each claim.

Look for: Requested $128.8M and 10.70 percent ROE; the ICC ordered a $55.8M reduction and authorized 9.60 percent on November 19, 2025 (about $73.0M annual increase); Ameren later filed a notice of appeal. Not a capital budget, not an equal charge to every household, not proof of reversal.

Module 8 A manufacturer requests 80 MW initially and 200 MW eventually. An upgrade costs $60 million and the customer secures $20 million. What do you check before calling the remaining $40 million a subsidy?

Look for: Which costs are exposed, value to other customers, minimum payments, exit obligations, recoverable salvage and approved allocation. Also do not claim $20 million protects everyone because it is a large number.

Module 15 Write a six-sentence briefing for an executive on a pending decision, labeling each sentence fact, company position, inference or recommendation.

Look for: Decision and deadline; Ameren request or position; customer problem; strongest evidence; principal challenge or uncertainty; action leadership must take. Source and status attached to each key claim.

Ten worked cases

Question 1 A spending cut and a bill reduction

A utility requests $400 million of capital investment. The Commission approves $300 million. Can you tell a legislator that customers will save $100 million on next year’s bills?

Answer: No. The $100 million difference is a capital-investment comparison. Its bill effect depends on when assets enter service, depreciation, financing costs, taxes, other adjustments, and the approved recovery mechanism. First identify the annual revenue effect and customer allocation.
Question 2 Supply prices and the total bill

A household uses 1,000 kWh monthly. Supply rises from 7 cents to 9 cents per kWh. All other charges remain $70. What changes?

Answer: Supply rises from $70 to $90, an increase of $20 or 28.6 percent. The total bill rises from $140 to $160, an increase of 14.3 percent. The supply percentage and total-bill percentage describe different denominators.
Question 3 A battery and a factory

A factory needs 50 MW continuously. Could a 50 MW battery with 200 MWh of usable energy serve it for a full day without recharging?

Answer: No. In the simplified example, the battery could provide 50 MW for four hours before losses and operating limits. The factory would need 1,200 MWh for 24 hours. The battery’s power rating matches the load, but its stored energy does not meet the full-day requirement.
Question 4 A petition and a policy

A stakeholder files a petition asking the ICC to suspend a workshop process. Is the process suspended?

Answer: The filing establishes a request. Look for a Commission order or other applicable action granting it. A petitioner’s requested relief is not the decision.
Question 5 A resource adequacy forecast

A study shows a shortfall in 2031 under its baseline assumptions. Does that prove an outage will occur in 2031?

Answer: No. It identifies a conditional gap relative to a planning standard. New supply, demand changes, transmission, operating measures, or changed assumptions can alter the outcome. It is a reason to evaluate timely solutions, not a deterministic outage prediction.
Question 6 A large customer deposit

A developer posts a $2 million deposit. The utility may need $90 million in facilities. Are existing customers fully protected?

Answer: The deposit amount alone cannot establish that. Determine what it covers, whether additional security is required before construction, what contractual revenues are committed, and what happens on cancellation. Also examine wider system effects beyond the dedicated facilities.
Question 7 An efficiency program and a rebate count

A program reports that it issued 20,000 rebates. Does that establish its energy savings?

Answer: No. Rebates measure participation or transactions. Savings depend on the equipment, baseline, operation, useful life, and evaluation method. Ask for verified savings, cost effectiveness, and distribution of benefits.
Question 8 Two apparently conflicting market headlines

PJM reports a future capacity shortfall while MISO reports sufficient capacity for the upcoming year. Is one wrong?

Answer: Not necessarily. They concern different regions, periods, and market frameworks. State the region and delivery year before comparing the findings.
Question 9 A public workshop proposal

An Ameren proposal appears in a Future of Gas presentation. Can the proposal be described as Ameren’s approved program?

Answer: No. Attribute it as a company proposal in that workshop. Determine whether a separate tariff, order, or law is needed and whether it has been adopted.
Question 10 A complete briefing

An executive asks, “What do I need to know about this docket?” What is the minimum useful answer?

Answer: Explain the customer or system problem, the decision maker and next decision, the principal disputed issue, the practical effect of plausible outcomes, and the action needed now. Include a source and date. Add detail where it changes the decision rather than substituting a list of filings for an explanation.

Module 1: The electricity system and the institutions around it

What you will learn

Trace electricity from resources to the meter; distinguish Ameren legal entities; match institutions to decisions.

Follow one unit of electricity

Imagine a home using an electric oven. A generating facility produces electricity. High-voltage transmission lines move power across a region. Substations reduce voltage, and local distribution lines carry power to the neighborhood. The meter records the home’s use. The customer receives a bill that reflects several services and obligations along this chain.

The physical system is interconnected. A customer’s electricity cannot generally be traced to one particular power plant simply because the customer bought a particular supply product. Contracts determine financial responsibility and environmental claims; the network determines actual power flows.

Ameren Illinois operates primarily as an electricity delivery utility, with a limited solar-generation exception explained below. For customers taking its supply service, it passes purchased electricity costs through without a markup. An alternative supplier may instead provide the customer’s supply arrangement. The delivery utility still maintains the local wires and responds to local outages. This distinction is fundamental when explaining a bill increase. [1]

Power and energy measure different things

Power describes the rate at which electricity is being used or produced at a moment. It is measured in kilowatts, abbreviated kW, or megawatts, abbreviated MW. One MW equals 1,000 kW. Energy describes how much electricity is used over time. It is measured in kilowatt-hours, or kWh, and megawatt-hours, or MWh.

A device drawing 2 kW for 3 hours uses 6 kWh. A facility drawing 100 MW continuously for 24 hours uses 2,400 MWh. The first number helps determine how large the equipment must be. The second helps determine how much electricity must be produced and purchased.

Peak demand is the highest level of use during a defined period. A system must be able to serve that peak even if average use is much lower. Two communities can consume the same annual energy but require different infrastructure if one uses much more electricity during a few hours.

Three kinds of reliability problems

A distribution problem occurs close to the customer: a tree falls on a line, a transformer fails, or a local circuit becomes overloaded. More generation elsewhere does not repair that broken neighborhood line.

A transmission problem limits the movement of electricity across the wider network. Enough generation may exist in the region, but a constraint can prevent it from reaching a particular area when needed. Additional local supply, transmission upgrades, or demand reductions may address different parts of this problem.

Resource adequacy asks whether enough dependable supply and demand reduction will be available during difficult conditions. It considers outages, weather, demand, imports, and the operating characteristics of resources. A region can produce plenty of energy over a year and still lack enough dependable resources during a critical evening or winter event. These three problems interact, but they require different evidence and remedies. [2, 3]

There is a small but important exception to the usual “delivery-only” description: that same fact sheet identifies two 2 MW East St. Louis solar facilities, operating since 2023 and 2024. Their combined 4 MW does not turn AIC into a vertically integrated generation utility like Ameren Missouri. Use “primarily a regulated delivery utility” when teaching the overall business model; avoid the absolute claim that it owns no generation. [39]

Module 2: How an ICC proceeding becomes a decision

What you will learn

Read a docket by legal status; distinguish requests, recommendations, orders, and appeals; explain the AIC gas-case example.

A docket is the official case record

A docket is a numbered proceeding with an official collection of filings, rulings, testimony, exhibits, and orders. For example, 26-0051 identifies Ameren Illinois’s 2026 filing for approval of a multi-year integrated grid plan. The number locates the case; it does not tell you whether the request was approved. [6]

A petition asks the Commission to do something. A tariff states rates or service terms. Testimony provides a witness’s evidence and explanations. A motion asks for a procedural or substantive ruling within the case. An order states a Commission decision. Confusing these document types is a common source of inaccurate briefings.

The usual sequence

1. A utility, another petitioner, or the Commission initiates a matter. The opening documents establish the requested action and legal basis.

2. The Commission assigns an administrative law judge, or ALJ, and establishes procedural steps. Parties may seek permission to intervene.

3. Parties exchange information through discovery. They submit testimony and exhibits supporting or challenging proposals.

4. The record may include additional rounds of testimony and an evidentiary hearing. Witnesses may be questioned about disputed evidence.

5. Parties submit briefs explaining what they believe the record supports. A proposed order may be issued for comment or exceptions.

6. The Commissioners issue an order. Subsequent steps can include compliance filings, rehearing requests, implementation, or judicial review.

The precise sequence varies by case. A workshop, procurement approval, complaint, rulemaking, and general rate case do not necessarily have the same schedule or evidentiary requirements. The case’s orders and procedural rulings determine its actual path. The ICC’s eDocket organizes the case details, docket sheet, schedule, documents, and public comments separately. [7]

Staff and the Commission are different

ICC Staff can investigate a proposal, request information, present analysis, and recommend changes. The Commissioners make the Commission’s decisions. A Staff recommendation is therefore significant evidence of an issue’s direction, but it is not itself the Commission’s final position.

An ALJ manages the proceeding and may prepare a proposed order. A proposed order is another important signal, but it must be distinguished from the final Commission order. A party’s press release is an explanation of that party’s perspective, even when it accurately describes portions of a decision.

Public participation has more than one form

A public comment can explain how a policy or proposal affects customers and communities. Formal intervention provides a different role in the case, subject to the applicable rules and rulings. A workshop submission contributes to an informal process, but it does not automatically become evidence in a later contested docket.

For government affairs work, first determine which channel fits the task. A community’s experience with repeated outages may inform a public discussion; technical evidence supporting a project’s cost and benefit belongs in the relevant regulatory process. Coordinate case-specific participation with regulatory counsel so that factual claims, procedural rules, and public messaging stay aligned.

Read an order in a useful sequence

Start with the ordered provisions near the end to identify what the Commission directed. Then read the findings and discussion on the disputed subjects. Look for the approved amount, conditions, reporting duties, effective dates, and required follow-up filings. Trace any important number to its definition and period.

Next, identify what remains unresolved. The Commission may approve one component while requiring further study of another. It may reject a proposed cost but permit a revised request. An order may also be challenged. Record each of these facts separately rather than assigning the entire case a single “win” or “loss” label.

Module 3: How utility spending becomes a customer charge

What you will learn

Separate capital spending, annual recovery, and monthly bills; explain rate base, ROE, and cost allocation.

Separate the project budget from the annual revenue requirement

A capital project creates or improves a long-lived asset, such as a substation. The utility typically recovers an approved asset’s cost over time rather than billing customers for the entire construction cost in one month. The annual amount the utility is authorized to collect for providing service is called the revenue requirement.

A simplified revenue requirement includes operating expenses, depreciation, taxes, and an allowed return on the investment recognized for ratemaking. Actual calculations also include other adjustments, credits, and legal requirements. The simplified structure is useful because it explains why a $100 million construction program and a $100 million annual rate increase are different claims.

Rate base is the investment balance recognized for calculating a return. It is not necessarily identical to the original construction cost. Accumulated depreciation and other adjustments affect it. Depreciation is the recovery of an asset’s cost across its estimated service life. An allowed return compensates the capital supporting the investment; it is distinct from returning the original investment through depreciation.

A hypothetical substation example

Assume a utility places a $100 million substation into service. For this teaching example, assume a 40-year recovery period, no salvage value, and an 8 percent overall allowed return applied to a simplified $100 million rate base in the first year.

Annual depreciation would be $2.5 million. The return component would be $8 million. That produces $10.5 million before operating expenses, taxes, and other adjustments. This is not the utility’s actual rate formula, and the return calculation would change as the recognized investment balance changes.

The lesson is that construction spending has a multiyear customer impact. A reduction in an investment proposal cannot be translated dollar for dollar into an immediate annual bill reduction. Ask whether a cited number is capital investment, rate base, annual revenue, cumulative revenue over several years, or a customer bill estimate.

Return on equity is one part of the calculation

Utilities finance investment with a combination of debt and equity. Debt is borrowed money. Equity is capital supplied by shareholders. Return on equity, or ROE, is the allowed return on the equity portion recognized in the capital structure. It is not the return applied to every dollar on a customer’s bill.

Hypothetical example: assume a $100 million rate base financed equally with debt and equity. A 5 percent debt cost contributes $2.5 million, and a 9 percent equity return contributes $4.5 million, for a combined $7 million return before associated tax treatment and other adjustments. Calling the 9 percent equity allowance a 9 percent profit margin on customer bills would be incorrect.

An allowed return is also not a guarantee of achieved earnings. Actual outcomes can depend on expenses, sales, disallowances, performance provisions, timing, and the specific regulatory mechanism. When discussing a rate case, separate the utility’s requested ROE, Staff’s recommendation, and the Commission’s authorized ROE.

Cost recovery and cost allocation answer different questions

Cost recovery asks whether and how an expense or investment may be collected from customers. Cost allocation asks which customer groups should bear it. Rate design determines the actual structure of the charges: for example, a monthly customer charge, a per-kWh charge, or a demand charge based on kW.

A rider is a tariff mechanism used to calculate or adjust a particular category of charges or credits. Its purpose and operation depend on its approved terms. A reconciliation compares specified actual amounts with amounts previously collected or authorized and makes the adjustment required by the mechanism. Neither term means that every expense automatically passes through without review.

Connect the mechanism to the bill

Suppose a household uses 800 kWh. A hypothetical supply price of 8 cents per kWh produces a $64 supply charge. If that price rises to 10 cents with use unchanged, supply becomes $80. The increase is $16. If the original total bill was $140 and all other components remain unchanged, the new total is $156, an 11.4 percent increase. Supply rose 25 percent; the total bill did not.

A different household may use 1,200 kWh and experience a $24 increase from the same supply-price change. If weather also increases use, the total bill rises for two reasons. Always state the assumed usage, customer class, bill components, and comparison period when communicating a projected impact. Ameren’s bill materials distinguish delivery, supply, and other charges; the specific tariff and supplier arrangement determine the actual bill. [1, 8]

Ameren Illinois in focus: three different financial questions

For an AIC electric project, ask whether the investment belongs in the grid plan, which costs can enter the authorized revenue requirement, and how the resulting charges are allocated and collected. These are linked questions with different records. Ameren's tariff library separately identifies performance-based reconciliation, performance metrics, delivery service, and purchased electricity recovery. The existence of several mechanisms is a reason to reconcile the bill carefully, not to assume every line is a second recovery of the same cost. [43]

Hypothetical teaching calculation: assume a $10 million asset, $250,000 annual depreciation, $150,000 operating expense, and a simplified first-year allowed return of $700,000. Before taxes and other adjustments, the annual requirement is $1.10 million. It is neither the $10 million construction outlay nor $700,000 of guaranteed shareholder profit. Debt costs are included in the overall financing return, while actual earnings depend on the applicable rules and performance.

If that simplified requirement were allocated equally to 100,000 customers, it would average $11 a year, or about $0.92 a month. Actual AIC charges use approved customer classes and billing determinants, not this equal division. A class with different demand characteristics may receive a different allocation.

Module 5: Multi Year Integrated Grid Plans

What you will learn

Evaluate AIC grid investments using needs, alternatives, timing, reliability, and equity.

What the proceeding is trying to accomplish

A grid plan explains how a utility proposes to develop its distribution system over a planning period. That system must continue serving existing customers while accommodating changing demand, distributed generation, storage, electric vehicles, and other needs. The plan should connect proposed work to identifiable system conditions and customer benefits.

Ameren Illinois filed its current grid-plan petition on January 20, 2026, in Docket 26-0051. ComEd’s corresponding 2028–2031 proceeding is Docket 26-0047. The official records identify the plan filings and testimony; the actual procedural schedule and orders govern the review. [6, 13]

The practical question is not simply whether the grid should be modernized. It is which investments are justified, when they are needed, what alternatives exist, and how their benefits compare with their costs. Different projects can have different answers.

What proposed investments can look like

A utility might replace aging equipment, install automated switches, expand a substation, add monitoring, improve the ability to connect customer generation, or strengthen circuits exposed to severe weather. These investments address different problems and should be evaluated with different measures.

For an aging transformer, relevant evidence might include condition, failure consequences, repair history, and replacement timing. For an overloaded circuit, the evidence might include measured loading, customer commitments, and demand forecasts. For automation, the analysis might estimate how much more quickly unaffected customers could be restored after a fault.

A general statement that a project “supports reliability” is insufficient for learning or evaluation. Ask which reliability problem it addresses and how success will be measured. Reducing the number of outages and shortening the duration of outages are related but different outcomes.

A hypothetical circuit decision

A circuit is forecast to exceed its practical capacity in three years. The utility proposes an $18 million expansion. An alternative combines $5 million of targeted equipment work with a customer demand-management program costing $600,000 annually for ten years.

The alternative totals $11 million before discounting and other differences. That arithmetic alone does not prove it is better. The analysis must ask whether the demand reduction is available during the right hours, whether customers remain enrolled, how long the arrangement lasts, and whether the circuit still needs replacement later.

The expansion also needs scrutiny. What happens if forecast demand does not materialize? Can construction be staged? Does the project solve several problems at once? Would a smaller investment provide enough flexibility? These questions explain why an investment review needs engineering and economic evidence together.

Forecasts and equity belong in the project analysis

A large proposed customer can change an area’s forecast. A signed service commitment carries different information from an early inquiry. A credible plan shows how uncertain projects are treated and how the utility would respond if growth is faster or slower than expected.

Equity analysis asks how costs, service quality, access to programs, and benefits are distributed. An investment located in a particular community does not automatically establish that the community receives the main benefit. Examine who experiences fewer outages, who can connect distributed resources, who faces barriers to participation, and who pays.

How to interpret prior reductions

The ICC’s corrected December 20, 2024, release reports that the prior Ameren grid-plan decision approved $83 million from a proposed $333 million in the spending addressed by that release, approximately a 75 percent reduction. That historical figure should not be described as a 75 percent cut to all Ameren Illinois electric investment or to all customer bills. A detailed comparison must reconcile the amount to the underlying order and investment categories. [14]

Prior decisions can reveal the type of evidence the Commission expects. They do not automatically determine the next case’s outcome. Avoid assuming that a previous percentage reduction will recur or that a historical allowed ROE is the authorized return for a different future period.

What government affairs should take from the docket

Prepare a concise explanation of the customer problem, the proposed solution, the principal disputed assumptions, and the consequences of different outcomes. Ask the regulatory team to identify which projects are central to economic development, reliability, or statutory obligations and which are more discretionary in timing.

Useful meeting questions include: Which forecast changes matter most? What evidence connects spending to customer benefits? What alternatives were tested? Which communities experience the costs and benefits? What decision would change the construction schedule? Keep the investment proposal, the requested rate recovery, and the ultimate customer-bill effect in separate fields.

Build a project card for each material investment: service problem; affected customers; condition of existing equipment; forecast demand; proposed solution; alternatives; cost and timing; measurable benefit; consequence of delay; and recovery mechanism. For a rural circuit, line length, vegetation exposure, switching options, and customers served may matter differently than for a dense urban circuit. Learning tool: compare three remedies for one constraint

Hypothetical AIC-area feeder: expected peak is 12 MW, while the relevant operating limit is 10 MW. A conventional upgrade may add capacity for many years. A 2 MW battery may cover a short peak but needs sufficient stored energy and recharge opportunities. The teacher's question is: “What would have to be true for each option to work?” Compare duration, availability, customer participation, failure conditions, total cost, and the date the problem must be solved. A low bid for an option that cannot serve the needed hours is not the least-cost feasible solution.

For reliability, distinguish frequency of interruptions from their duration, and ordinary conditions from major storms. Ask whether published measures include or exclude major events and whether averages hide persistently poor circuits. A companywide improvement can coexist with a neighborhood that still needs attention.

These are company planning priorities in the filed plan. DERMS is the software and control capability used to coordinate distributed resources; its value depends on what resources it can actually observe and control. Ask which capability is needed now, which depends on later investment, and how results will be measured. [60]

Module 6: Statewide Integrated Resource Planning

What you will learn

Read resource scenarios as conditional findings; test dependable contribution, location, and implementation.

What the IRP adds

The statewide integrated resource plan, or IRP, examines whether future electricity needs can be served with a reasonable combination of resources. Its scope reaches beyond local utility poles and wires. It considers generation, storage, demand-side resources, regional conditions, and the practical ability to develop solutions.

The statutory framework calls for analysis over at least 5-, 10-, 15-, and 20-year horizons, with multiple demand scenarios. ICC Staff coordinates the agencies’ work. The initial statutory submission date is November 15, 2026; the ICC explains that filing occurs no later than Monday, November 16, because November 15 is a Sunday. The second plan is due September 30, 2029, followed by four-year cycles. [10, 15]

Filing starts the Commission’s review. Section 16-202 provides 180 days for approval or approval with modifications, with a possible extension of up to another 180 days. The statute sets criteria involving adequacy, affordability, emissions, and other objectives. A November filing therefore is not a November final approval. [11]

Understand a resource portfolio

A portfolio is a combination of resources selected to work together. Solar may produce strongly during daylight hours. Storage may move some of that energy into later hours. Demand response may reduce use during stressed conditions. Transmission may make outside resources more accessible. Dispatchable generation may provide output when instructed, subject to fuel, maintenance, and other limits.

Each resource has different costs, capabilities, and development times. Comparing technologies solely on annual energy cost can miss whether they are available when the system needs them most. Comparing solely on nameplate MW can miss the same issue.

Nameplate capacity is different from dependable contribution

Nameplate capacity is an equipment rating. Accredited capacity is the amount a planning or market process recognizes toward reliability requirements under its rules. Weather-dependent output, forced outages, duration limits, and system conditions can affect that contribution.

Hypothetical example: a 100 MW battery with 400 MWh of usable stored energy could discharge at 100 MW for about four hours before considering losses and operational limits. It cannot sustain 100 MW indefinitely. A 100 MW solar project also does not produce 100 MW at every hour. Neither should automatically be described as a one-for-one replacement for another 100 MW resource.

Read scenarios as conditional statements

A scenario says what may happen if a set of assumptions holds. A sensitivity changes one or more assumptions to see how results respond. Neither is a prediction with certainty.

Suppose one scenario assumes three large industrial projects arrive on schedule, while another assumes only one does. The preferred resource mix could change. A third case might test construction delays even if demand remains high. Reading only the central scenario hides the conditions that create the largest exposure.

Useful questions concern the date of the demand forecast, the probability of proposed projects, the treatment of retirements, the completion rate of new resources, fuel-price assumptions, extreme weather, and the ability to import electricity. A forecast should also distinguish energy growth from peak growth.

How the resource adequacy study informs the IRP

The 2025 Resource Adequacy Study was released December 15, 2025. E3, which performed the modeling, describes baseline regional shortfalls beginning in PJM in 2029 and MISO in 2031. These are conditional regional findings, not a schedule of certain Illinois blackouts. The study also tests resource-development delays; those assumptions can bring risks forward. [5, 16]

The statutory IRP process requires the first plan to take that study into account and explain divergences from its analysis and conclusions. A difference between the study and a later plan may reflect updated demand, resources, or assumptions rather than an error. The analyst’s job is to explain the change. [10]

Follow the recommendation into an executable action

If the IRP identifies a need, ask what authority and process can address it. Possibilities include changes to procurement, program design, resource development, or other implementation measures within applicable law. The IPA has begun work on a potential long-term clean energy procurement framework associated with identified reliability needs. [17]

For Ameren Illinois government affairs, the useful questions are where MISO conditions create exposure for customers, which solutions can arrive in time, what customer costs they imply, and what actions require coordination beyond the utility. A recommendation without an owner, delivery date, or implementation pathway is incomplete as a practical response.

Ameren Illinois in focus: translating statewide scenarios into local questions

Ameren Illinois customers are exposed to MISO conditions, while much of northern Illinois is in PJM. Statewide totals can hide these regional differences. A resource assumed in a statewide model must still be deliverable where it is needed; transmission constraints and resource availability matter alongside the statewide MW count. [5, 16]

Use a three-column worksheet: “model assumption,” “Ameren-area implication,” and “action needed.” For example, an assumption of rapid electric heating growth implies a need to examine cold-weather peaks and distribution upgrades. A storage assumption requires checking duration, location, charging energy, and a credible in-service date. A demand-response assumption requires a program capable of enrolling and retaining participants.

Learning tool: test the assumption, not just the result

Hypothetical model: 1,000 MW of new load arrives by 2030. A sensitivity case moves half of that load to 2033. Compare the resources and costs selected in each case. If one expensive project is needed only under the first schedule, examine staged commitments and alternatives. This does not mean the high-growth case is wrong; it identifies the decision's exposure to timing.

Teach it back: “The model shows a need under stated assumptions. It helps us choose actions, but it does not guarantee either a shortage or the success of a particular project.”

The central problem is how to plan a long-lived network

Gas utilities operate networks designed to last for many years. At the same time, technology, customer choices, building practices, and climate policy can change future gas use. The Future of Gas process examines how the distribution system may adapt and what regulatory or legislative changes may be appropriate.

The ICC initiated the statewide process in March 2024. Its materials describe workshops and recommendations, along with long-term gas infrastructure planning requirements. Workshop proposals should be understood as proposals unless a subsequent order or law adopts them. [18]

An exploration of gas-system alternatives does not itself prohibit service or shut down the network. Likewise, continuing to provide safe service does not resolve every question about the least-cost pattern of future investment. The policy challenge is to connect present service needs with a realistic long-term pathway.

Why declining use can create an affordability problem

Many network costs do not fall in direct proportion to gas sales. Pipes still need maintenance, emergency response remains necessary, and previously approved investment may still be recovered. If fewer customers or fewer therms support those costs, charges to remaining customers can rise, depending on rate design and cost changes.

Hypothetical example: a network has $10 million in annual fixed costs allocated across 10,000 customers. That averages $1,000 per customer before other costs. If 2,000 customers leave and fixed costs remain $10 million, the average becomes $1,250 for each remaining customer. This is a simplified illustration, not a forecast. Actual outcomes depend on avoided costs, remaining usage, financing, and Commission decisions.

The example explains why an orderly transition must consider households unable to replace equipment or change fuels. A customer may face financial barriers, rental restrictions, unsuitable building conditions, or other limits. Policy analysis should examine the remaining customers as carefully as the early participants.

Stranded assets and alternatives

A stranded asset is an investment whose remaining cost may not be recoverable or economically useful as originally expected. Early retirement can create disagreement over how much customers, shareholders, or another funding source should bear. The relevant amount is generally tied to the unrecovered investment and applicable treatment, not simply the original project budget.

A non-pipeline alternative is an approach that may avoid or defer a particular gas infrastructure investment. It could involve efficiency, targeted electrification, or another solution. It must be compared with the specific project it would replace, including safety, timing, service quality, participant costs, and impacts on nonparticipants.

Hypothetical example: replacing a deteriorated main serving one neighborhood costs $12 million. A proposed alternative would weatherize buildings and convert heating systems. A fair comparison includes equipment, wiring, electric upgrades, customer contributions, ongoing bills, maintenance, and backup needs. It also asks whether the gas segment can actually be retired. Converting some homes while retaining most of the pipe obligations may yield very different savings from retiring an entire segment.

Understand the technology discussions

Electrification replaces a fossil-fuel end use with electric equipment. A heat pump moves heat rather than producing all of it through resistance heating. Its performance depends on equipment, building conditions, and weather. A serious analysis should examine cold-weather demand and any additional electric infrastructure needs.

Renewable natural gas is gas derived from sources such as organic waste after processing. Its economics and emissions depend on feedstock, leakage, treatment, and the counterfactual use of that feedstock. Hydrogen proposals require attention to production methods, equipment compatibility, leakage, and safety. A thermal energy network shares heating or cooling through a network, with a different design and cost structure from conventional gas delivery.

These are options to evaluate, not interchangeable solutions. The ICC’s pathways study compares technology and policy pathways using cost, reliability, equity, and infrastructure measures. Its published 2026 schedule and working-group materials provide a route into the technical assumptions. [19]

Module 8: Large Loads and Data Centers

What you will learn

Evaluate large-customer demand, staging, financial protections, and Ameren-specific service terms.

Why a large customer creates a different planning problem

A very large customer may require substantial infrastructure before it begins taking service. The utility must decide when to commit capital, how much demand is credible, and how to protect other customers if the project is delayed, downsized, or canceled. The regional system must also have enough resources to serve the added demand.

A data center’s proposed maximum demand is not necessarily its initial load, average use, or dependable long-term commitment. A pipeline of applications can include projects at very different stages. Adding all requested MW together and treating the sum as certain demand can overstate near-term needs.

Hypothetical example: a developer requests 300 MW at full buildout, but plans 75 MW initially. The forecast should specify the timing and confidence of each stage. Infrastructure sized for 300 MW may be economically reasonable if the commitment is credible; it creates different risk if the later stages are optional.

The ComEd proceedings

The ICC’s March 20, 2026, announcement describes approved ComEd tariff changes providing deposit protection against canceled or abandoned large-demand projects. It also describes a follow-up investigation into broader cost and consumer-protection issues. The underlying consolidated order is in Dockets 25-0677 and 25-0679; the follow-up reference is 26-0364. [22, 23, 24]

The acronym LDPAC refers to Large Demand Project Applicants or Customers. The announcement discusses requests of 50 MW or more. A threshold in a ComEd tariff should not be treated as a universal Illinois definition or automatically applied to Ameren Illinois. Service obligations and financial protections depend on the applicable utility’s tariffs, agreements, and jurisdiction.

Four categories of exposure

First, application and study work costs money even if the project never proceeds. Second, dedicated facilities can become underused if the customer cancels. Third, broader network upgrades may serve both the new load and existing customers. Fourth, additional demand can affect regional supply and capacity costs beyond the immediate connection.

A deposit may address part of the first or second exposure without resolving the third or fourth. Therefore, “the customer paid a deposit” is not enough to conclude that all other customers are protected. Read what the payment secures, when it can be drawn, how it changes as spending grows, and when it is returned.

A hypothetical cost allocation example

A sound analysis examines the actual facilities and contractual obligations rather than applying a single slogan about who should pay.

Economic development and customer protection can be evaluated together

A large customer can bring investment, employment, tax revenue, and electricity sales that help support shared costs. Those benefits should be compared with the incremental infrastructure, operating, and reliability burdens. The right analysis asks about the net effect under realistic scenarios.

For government affairs, identify the enforceable commitment behind the economic-development announcement. Ask what happens if the promised demand, investment, or jobs arrive later than expected. Also identify who bears risks the local agreement cannot resolve, including regional resource needs and federal transmission issues.

A useful government-affairs question is: “What customer commitments must be secured before each construction milestone?” This connects support for investment to an observable protection, rather than an unsupported promise that all growth will lower rates.

Module 9: Rate Cases and Customer Bills

What you will learn

Read an AIC bill by component; separate price from usage; compare supplier offers on consistent terms.

A rate case determines the authorized cost of service

A general rate case examines the revenue needed to provide service and the rates or service terms used to collect it. Disputes can concern investment, operating costs, depreciation, capital structure, ROE, cost allocation, and customer protections. An electric supply-price change is a different matter from a delivery revenue increase.

For gas service, commodity gas costs and delivery-system costs are likewise distinct. The Future of Gas can influence the planning questions surrounding gas investment, while a rate case addresses the particular costs and rates in its record. Do not collapse these into one approval.

The ICC’s Future of Gas overview explains that gas rate-case review examines whether costs are reasonable and prudent and describes the general 11-month process. Particular deadlines and issues must still be taken from the specific proceeding. [18]

Requested amounts and approved amounts need context

Suppose a company requests a $120 million annual increase and receives $75 million. It is accurate to describe the approved increase as lower than the request. It is inaccurate to call that a $45 million bill reduction relative to existing rates unless the baseline supports that conclusion.

Also ask whether a quoted amount is gross or net of existing charges, whether it concerns one year or several, and whether it includes offsets. A rate-case announcement can use a company-wide annual figure while a customer-facing example uses an average monthly bill. Those numbers need a bridge, not a direct comparison.

Fixed charges and usage charges create different effects

A monthly customer charge remains even when usage falls. A usage charge changes with consumption. A demand charge can reflect the customer’s highest measured demand during a specified interval. The mix affects customers differently.

Hypothetical example: shifting $5 per month from a usage charge to a fixed charge can leave one illustrative average bill unchanged while raising costs for a low-use customer and lowering them for a high-use customer. A statement that a proposal is “revenue neutral” means total expected collections are unchanged; it does not mean every customer is unaffected.

What to read alongside a rate case

Review the company’s proposed tariff sheets, bill-impact exhibits, Staff and intervenor testimony on disputed costs, the order, and compliance tariffs. Check the date the revised rates become effective. An order date and a bill-change date can differ.

The Ameren gas-case example below shows the distinction among requested revenue, approved revenue, effective tariffs, and total bill impact. Later proceedings can change the forward-looking context while a historical decision remains part of the record.

BGS refers to basic generation service, PER to purchased electricity recovery, and PSP to Power Smart Pricing. These labels identify different functions; an acronym alone does not establish a charge's current value. [43]

Learning tool: separate price from use

Hypothetical electric bill comparison, excluding taxes and other adjustments: at 800 kWh, a 10-cent supply rate produces $80 of supply cost. At 1,000 kWh and 12 cents, supply cost is $120. Of the $40 increase, $20 comes from using 200 additional kWh at the old rate, and $20 from the 2-cent price increase applied to the new 1,000 kWh. The allocation of the interaction depends on the decomposition method; this example explicitly applies usage first. A claim that all $40 was a rate increase would be wrong.

Now add hypothetical delivery of $55 in the first month and $58 in the second. The total becomes $135 and $178: a $43 increase. The supply portion increased 50 percent; the simplified total bill increased about 31.9 percent. Neither figure is the delivery rate change.

For gas, a therm measures energy use. Separate the purchased gas component from delivery charges and other adjustments. The November 2025 gas order's allowed ROE is a financing input; it is not a percentage markup on each therm or a promise of a uniform customer bill increase. [41, 43]

Module 10: Energy Efficiency and Beneficial Electrification

What you will learn

Explain Ameren efficiency and charging programs; distinguish annual energy savings from peak-demand benefits.

Efficiency reduces the energy needed for a service

Efficiency means using less energy to provide a comparable service, such as lighting, cooling, or industrial output. Weatherization, improved controls, and more efficient equipment can reduce bills and system needs. Demand response changes or reduces use at particular times, which can be valuable even when the effect on annual energy consumption is smaller.

An efficiency plan should explain the target savings, program budget, customer eligibility, delivery methods, and measurement approach. The relevant question is whether verified benefits justify costs under the applicable framework, including which customers can actually participate.

The ICC record for ComEd Docket 25-0213 describes a 2026–2029 efficiency plan and explains cumulative persisting annual savings: savings in a year can come from measures installed that year and from earlier measures still operating. Ameren’s entity case list identifies Docket 26-0445 as its energy efficiency and demand-response plan proceeding. Nicor’s Docket 26-0458 concerns an amended efficiency plan. [25, 26, 27]

Understand gross and net savings

Gross savings estimate the change associated with installed measures. Net savings attempt to account for what would have happened without the program and other attribution effects. These concepts matter because a program should not receive full credit for savings that would have occurred anyway.

Hypothetical example: a program pays incentives for 1,000 efficient appliances. If many customers would have bought the same models without assistance, the program’s additional savings may be lower than the sum of every appliance’s improvement. Conversely, a program may influence other purchases or practices. The evaluator’s method determines how those effects are handled.

Participation barriers change program results

A rebate paid after installation may be unusable for a household that cannot afford the initial purchase. A renter may not control the heating system. An older home may need wiring or structural repairs before an efficiency measure can be installed. A program can have attractive theoretical economics and still miss the people with the greatest need.

For government affairs, ask about completed projects, participant out-of-pocket costs, geographic distribution, and households turned away. Distinguish a program’s budget from its delivered savings and from its participation results.

Beneficial electrification changes the fuel or end use

Electrification may increase electricity consumption while reducing gasoline or gas use. That can still produce overall benefits, depending on equipment, use patterns, energy prices, emissions, and infrastructure costs. Looking only at the electric bill can miss the household’s total energy and transportation budget.

Hypothetical example: an electric vehicle adds $45 to a household’s monthly electric bill but reduces gasoline spending by $120. The household’s combined fuel spending falls $75 before vehicle purchase costs and other differences. The local grid impact still depends on when the vehicle charges and whether nearby equipment has enough capacity.

Ameren’s beneficial electrification record includes consolidated Dockets 24-0494 and 24-0578. It is a separate proceeding from the general grid plan and statewide IRP. [28]

Managed charging links customer behavior to system planning

If many vehicles begin charging when people arrive home, they may add to a local evening peak. Managed charging shifts some use to another time, subject to customer needs and program terms. A fleet depot also needs enough charging capacity to meet its departure schedule.

Ask who controls charging, whether participation is voluntary, how customers are compensated, and what happens during an operational emergency. An incentive program, a tariff, and a physical infrastructure upgrade may all be needed, but each solves a different part of the problem.

The solar as efficiency question

Docket 25-1066 is ComEd’s petition to include solar as an energy efficiency measure in the Illinois Technical Reference Manual. That is a question about treatment under a program and measurement framework. It should not be reported as already adopted simply because the petition exists. [29]

The broader learning issue is accounting: a reduction in electricity purchased from the grid can result from using less energy or producing energy on site. Program rules determine how each is credited. Analysts should watch for double counting across efficiency, renewable, and other programs.

Ameren Illinois in focus: moving from an offer to a result

Ameren Illinois' efficiency program includes residential and business pathways, contractors called Program Allies, and a Market Development Initiative that addresses participation and workforce development. Its business FAQ describes ratepayer funding through the energy-efficiency rider and offers help identifying eligible equipment improvements. Public-sector facilities are included among the nonresidential customers the FAQ discusses. Equipment eligibility and application requirements still govern an individual project. [48, 49]

A school district should move from an energy consultation to a defined project, eligibility and timing checks, installation, documentation, and verification. Some offers may require preapproval; the current application controls. A rebate announcement is not enough to place a project in a budget as guaranteed funding.

AIC also has an Optional Electric Vehicle Charging Program, Rider EVCP. Its January 1, 2026 specifications require qualifying residential rebate equipment to be networked, ENERGY STAR-certified, and safety-certified by a recognized testing laboratory. A vehicle purchase rebate from Illinois EPA is a separate program from utility charging infrastructure assistance. [50]

Learning tool: energy savings and peak savings are different

Hypothetical school replaces inefficient equipment and saves 100,000 kWh annually. At an assumed avoided variable cost of 12 cents per kWh, the simplified energy saving is $12,000 annually. Fixed charges need not disappear. If most savings occur outside the system peak, the peak-capacity benefit may be small. Measure when savings occur as well as their annual amount.

Hypothetical fleet can charge 20 vehicles at 7 kW each. Simultaneous charging adds 140 kW. If operations allow half to charge at a time, charging demand can fall to 70 kW during each group’s charging period, while the total energy needed remains approximately the same. Equipment limits, departure times, and the applicable rate determine whether that schedule is useful.

Module 11: Storage Renewable Procurement and Virtual Power Plants

What you will learn

Distinguish solar and storage transactions; calculate battery duration; evaluate aggregated resource performance.

Procurement buys a defined product

A procurement is a structured process to acquire a product or contractual commitment. Depending on the program, that product could involve electricity, capacity, renewable energy credits, storage credits, or another attribute. The product matters because each contract supports a different objective and payment stream.

A renewable energy credit, or REC, represents specified environmental attributes associated with renewable generation. Purchasing RECs is not identical to purchasing a firm supply of electricity at every hour. The IPA’s Long-Term Renewable Resources Procurement Plan guides renewable procurement and programs, including Illinois Shines and Illinois Solar for All. Its 2026 materials include a June 1 modified plan. [12]

What happened in the initial storage procurement

The August 26, 2026, storage procurement targeted 1,038 MW. The ICC approved results on September 1, supporting 600 MW of new storage projects: 520 MW in MISO territory and 80 MW in PJM territory. The total was 438 MW below the target. These are contract awards supporting development, not a statement that 600 MW was already operating. [30]

CRGA’s initial storage goal is 3,000 MW committed to commercial operation by December 31, 2030. The initial procurement uses 20-year indexed storage credit contracts. Later procurements and program development have their own requirements. [31]

Follow a battery from award to service

After award, a project still faces contractual milestones, site control, permitting, interconnection, equipment procurement, financing, construction, and testing. A delay in any critical step can change the date on which the system receives the resource’s benefit.

The MW rating describes power output. The MWh rating describes stored energy. A battery also needs a source of charging electricity and loses some energy during charging and discharging. Its contribution depends on when it charges, when it discharges, its duration, and the reliability rules applied to it.

Storage can help shift energy, respond quickly, and support reliability. It does not create primary energy and cannot automatically replace the same nameplate amount of thermal generation under all conditions. A serious comparison evaluates dependable output during the relevant hours.

Understand an indexed credit in principle

An indexed contract adjusts payment using a defined reference calculation. The purpose is to connect support to specified market conditions rather than assume a single fixed market-revenue stream. The actual Illinois storage contract determines the formula, settlement treatment, obligations, and risk allocation.

When evaluating such a contract, ask what happens when market revenues rise or fall, whether the reference accurately represents project earnings, what performance is required, and who bears development risk. Do not estimate customer charges by multiplying awarded MW by a guessed price.

The next storage milestones

The IPA issued a request for feedback on September 23, 2026, covering the summer procurement and future procurement design. Its announcement sets an October 28, 2026, 5 p.m. Central deadline. The agency also identifies a storage procurement plan for public comment no later than June 1, 2027. [30, 32]

Government affairs can use the 2026 results to ask why the geographic outcome differed from the target, what barriers affected participation, and whether proposed changes preserve competition and customer value. The existence of an award shortfall alone does not establish its cause.

A virtual power plant coordinates many smaller resources

A virtual power plant, or VPP, aggregates distributed resources so they can provide a coordinated service. Examples can include batteries, controllable equipment, and other eligible customer resources. Instead of relying on one large facility, an operator coordinates many smaller contributions.

Hypothetical example: 10,000 enrolled batteries each provide 3 kW during an event. Their combined output is 30 MW if every battery performs as assumed. Actual dependable capability may be lower because of enrollment, state of charge, customer overrides, equipment failures, or program rules.

The important questions concern dispatch authority, customer compensation, availability, cybersecurity, measurement, and avoiding payment twice for the same service. The state IRP statute expressly includes consideration of distributed storage, VPP deployment, and demand response in its analysis. A planning reference does not by itself establish a customer’s eligibility or compensation under a live program. [10]

Ameren Illinois in focus: four different solar and storage transactions

A customer installing solar may encounter interconnection, a utility rebate, bill credits under an applicable tariff, and a state REC incentive. Each has a separate purpose and eligibility test. Interconnection permission concerns safe connection; a rebate supports eligible equipment; bill crediting addresses exported energy; a REC arrangement transfers renewable attributes. Completion of one does not automatically complete the others. Illinois Shines' consumer FAQ explicitly distinguishes its incentive from utility distributed-generation rebates. [51]

An IPA procurement for grid-scale storage is another transaction. A MISO allocation is a regional procurement category, not a finding that every awarded project is in AIC's retail territory or owned by Ameren. Avoid converting a regional storage headline into an Ameren construction announcement. [30, 31]

Learning tool: power, duration, and delivery

Hypothetical 20 MW / 80 MWh battery can discharge at 20 MW for four hours before allowing for operating limits and losses. A factory needing 20 MW around the clock uses 480 MWh per day. The battery can shift energy and support selected hours; it still needs charging energy and cannot supply that daily requirement indefinitely on its own.

For aggregated customer devices, draw a chain: eligible device → enrolled customer → available device during the event → measured performance → settlement. Counting devices at the first step overstates dependable delivery if later steps lose participants or capability. Customer override rights and compensation should be explained before treating the program as a firm planning resource.

Module 12: Regional Markets Transmission and Siting

What you will learn

Distinguish energy, capacity, interconnection, siting, and operational readiness in Ameren-area projects.

Capacity payments support readiness

Energy markets compensate electricity supplied. Capacity mechanisms compensate qualifying resources for being available under defined rules. Ancillary services help the system operate reliably over shorter intervals. These products serve related but different purposes. FERC’s market materials explain the distinctions and its oversight of regional operators. [3, 33]

A capacity auction can show a tightening balance between committed resources and reliability needs. Its price is not a residential cents-per-kWh rate. Translating it into a retail bill requires knowing procurement arrangements, obligations, usage, and timing.

Read auction results with their delivery years

PJM reported a 6,831 MW shortfall against its reliability requirement for the 2028/2029 delivery year. That is a regional auction result and should be labeled with the full delivery year. It is different from the earlier 2027/2028 auction and different from a local distribution reliability measure. [34]

MISO’s announcement for the June 2026–May 2027 planning year reported sufficient regional capacity. That finding applies to that planning year and the stated regional assessment. It does not eliminate future risks or prove that every local constraint has been resolved. [35]

PJM and MISO have different structures and rules. Even where prices are expressed in similar units, check whether the comparison uses the same season, region, delivery period, and product. A headline comparison without those details can mislead.

Interconnection is the engineering and contractual connection process

A resource seeking to connect must establish that its connection can be accommodated and identify required upgrades and responsibilities. A queue is a list of projects in a process. It is not a list of completed facilities.

Projects may withdraw, change size, face higher costs, or miss target dates. Counting every queued MW as certain supply can therefore overstate future capability. MISO describes its generator interconnection process as leading to an agreement allowing a customer to connect generation; construction and operation still require further steps. [36]

For a large load, the connection process concerns taking electricity from the system. For a generator, it concerns injecting electricity. Storage may do both. The applicable process depends on facility characteristics and jurisdiction, so the word “interconnection” alone does not identify the responsible authority.

Siting approval and grid readiness are different

Siting concerns where a project can be located and the conditions under which it can be built. It may involve local land-use requirements, state standards, environmental review, safety, and other permits. Grid readiness concerns the electrical ability to connect and operate the facility.

A site can be approved while the necessary electrical upgrades remain years away. Conversely, a promising grid location can face a siting obstacle. A practical project schedule should identify both pathways and their dependencies.

For a specific proposal, identify the precise statutory section and implementing rule before describing a universal statewide permission or appeal right. CRGA is extensive, and provisions can differ by technology and type of approval. [37]

Nuclear policy and project timing

Changes to state restrictions on nuclear development can expand long-term options. They do not eliminate federal licensing, site development, financing, construction, or operating requirements. A planning exercise can examine nuclear as a future resource without treating an uncommitted project as near-term available capacity.

For government affairs, the key distinction is between legal eligibility to pursue a project and credible delivery of the project. State statutory changes should be evaluated alongside the federal Nuclear Regulatory Commission’s licensing requirements and a project-specific development schedule. [37, 38]

Ameren Illinois in focus: who owns the line and who approves what?

Ameren Illinois owns electric transmission assets, while ATXI is a separate Ameren company operating a FERC-regulated transmission business. Ameren Missouri has a different generation and delivery business. A transmission proposal can involve regional planning, federal rate regulation, state siting, land rights, and construction milestones. Identify the responsible entity and approval for each step. [40]

The McLean County Reliability Project provides a historical AIC example: Ameren's project page reports ICC authorization on October 10, 2018 for approximately 10 miles of 138 kV line and associated facilities, linking the McLean County and Normal East substations. The page illustrates siting and a certificate process; its projected schedule should not be used alone as current confirmation of operational status. [52]

Learning tool: three clocks

Clock 1 is the regulatory approval schedule. Clock 2 is the engineering, equipment, and construction schedule. Clock 3 is the date customers or the regional system need service. A project can be legally approved and still miss Clock 3 because of equipment lead times. A credible mitigation plan identifies what can operate before the permanent project is ready.

When discussing MISO auction results, preserve the season, planning year, zone, product, and unit. A dollar-per-MW-day capacity price cannot be read as cents per kWh on an Ameren residential bill. The translation requires the relevant capacity obligation, procurement treatment, allocation, and billed energy.

Module 13: Affordability Equity and the Household Budget

What you will learn

Match Ameren customer needs with assistance pathways; distinguish funding, eligibility, debt relief, and payment timing.

Affordability depends on both bills and resources

A bill can be high because prices are high, usage is high, the home is inefficient, or several of these factors occur together. A household’s ability to pay also depends on income, other necessary expenses, debt, and the predictability of earnings. Average bills alone do not capture these differences.

Energy burden is energy spending expressed as a share of income over a defined period. Hypothetical example: $2,400 in annual home energy costs equals 8 percent of a $30,000 annual income, but 3 percent of an $80,000 income. The same bill produces a different burden.

When comparing households, use consistent definitions. Does the calculation include electricity and gas, or only one? Is income gross or disposable? Is the period a year or a winter month? Are transportation fuels included? A statistic without those choices is difficult to interpret.

Separate immediate assistance from structural savings

Bill assistance provides money or credits to help pay current charges or arrears. Efficiency improvements can reduce future consumption. Rate design changes can redistribute charges. Procurement or infrastructure decisions can affect future system costs. These approaches can complement one another, but they operate on different timelines.

A family facing disconnection needs a near-term response. A resource portfolio expected to lower costs years from now does not pay today’s overdue balance. Conversely, a one-time grant does not repair a poorly insulated home or resolve recurring cost drivers.

Consider households above assistance thresholds

Moderate-income households can experience substantial pressure even when they do not qualify for income-targeted support. Any proposal aimed at this group should state eligibility, benefit size, expected reach, and funding source. “Helping customers” is not a complete program design.

Distinguish taxpayer-funded assistance, charitable donations, shareholder funding, and charges collected from other utility customers. If customers fund a benefit through rates, evaluate the burden on those who pay but do not receive it. If a utility administers a program, that does not by itself tell you who funds it.

A hypothetical program comparison

Program A provides a $400 grant to 10,000 households, requiring $4 million before administration. Program B pays for upgrades expected to save each of 2,000 households $250 annually for ten years, at an upfront cost of $3,000 per home. Program B requires $6 million upfront and may provide lasting savings, but reaches fewer households immediately.

These programs answer different needs. Their comparison should consider urgency, participation, administrative costs, savings persistence, and who remains unserved. It should also consider whether upgrades require landlord consent or additional repairs.

Avoid unsupported percentage claims

A claim that “rates rose 94 percent” needs the tariff or dataset, starting and ending dates, customer class, usage assumption, and definition of rates. A supply rate, all-in price per kWh, average revenue per kWh, and typical customer bill are different measures.

For policy conversations, use a bill comparison tied to a defined customer profile and verified source. Explain separately the effects of supply prices, delivery charges, riders, taxes, and changing usage.

Module 14: How the Proceedings Interact

What you will learn

Combine commercial commitments, local grid needs, regional resources, and customer effects in one analysis.

A hypothetical new industrial campus

Assume a proposed campus expects to reach 200 MW over five years. The first question is how credible that schedule is. The utility assesses local connection needs and may include related investment in its grid planning. Service terms determine deposits, financial commitments, and other obligations. Regional planners consider the added demand when assessing resource needs.

The statewide IRP may evaluate how this and other growth changes resource requirements. Procurement may support additional resources, but those projects must still obtain permits and interconnect. Efficiency, flexible operations, or on-site resources may reduce the peak contribution, depending on actual performance and program rules.

Customers experience the results through several possible channels: directly assigned connection costs, delivery rates, regional transmission charges, supply costs, and program charges or credits. One docket does not necessarily resolve all of them.

A hypothetical neighborhood heating transition

Assume an older gas main requires major work. The gas planning process compares replacement with alternatives. If electrification is considered, the electric utility assesses local capacity. Customers need equipment, suitable buildings, and a workable financing arrangement. Regional planning must account for any additional winter demand.

Efficiency could reduce the required heating load. A targeted approach might permit retirement of an entire gas segment, but only if the necessary participation, legal authority, and service arrangements exist. A partial conversion could leave most gas-network costs in place while adding electric-system needs.

The comparison must therefore include both networks and the household. A low cost shown in one utility’s analysis could simply shift costs to the other utility or directly to the customer. This is why coordination matters.

A hypothetical storage award

A developer wins a contract for a 100 MW battery. The procurement process establishes a contractual commitment. Interconnection determines required grid upgrades. Local and other approvals affect construction. Financing and equipment delivery affect timing. The regional reliability process determines the capacity contribution recognized under applicable rules.

A press release announcing the award describes only one milestone. A government affairs update should show the expected in-service date, major remaining dependencies, and the evidence supporting any claimed reliability or customer-cost benefit.

Look for mismatched assumptions

The same large-load project may appear in a utility plan, a regional forecast, and an economic-development announcement with different dates or sizes. Different versions can create overbuilding or underplanning if the discrepancies are not understood.

Likewise, several programs may each assume credit for the same demand reduction. An efficiency forecast, VPP estimate, and customer solar projection need consistent accounting. Ask the technical team how overlap is removed and how the combined forecast is reconciled across proceedings.

The purpose is to see why an isolated “approve or reject” description is too thin. Sequencing, safeguards, staging, and contingency plans can change the quality of an otherwise similar proposal. Your briefing should name the action needed now and the evidence required before the next commitment.

Module 15: Turning Regulatory Analysis into Government Affairs Advice

What you will learn

Translate regulatory evidence into clear, audience-appropriate government-affairs advice.

Start with the decision that matters

A useful update identifies the next consequential decision, the decision maker, and the range of plausible outcomes. It then explains the effect on customers, company operations, communities, and policy commitments. A long list of filings without this connection creates activity reporting rather than advice.

Use five sentences as a starting structure: The proceeding addresses a particular problem. The next decision will determine a specific issue. The principal dispute concerns a named assumption or proposal. The customer or operational consequence is a defined effect. The recommended action is a concrete step tied to the decision window.

Separate company interests from broader effects

A proposal can affect utility earnings, customer costs, reliability, employment, and emissions in different ways. State each effect plainly. Do not assume that an outcome favorable to one dimension is automatically favorable to every other dimension.

For example, an investment may improve service but raise near-term bills. Delaying it may reduce immediate spending while increasing outage exposure or later construction costs. The briefing should explain that tradeoff, the evidence supporting each side, and which uncertainty changes the decision.

Understand stakeholder interests without assigning motives

Consumer advocates may focus on bills, risk allocation, and protections. Industrial customers may emphasize price competitiveness and service dependability. Environmental organizations may focus on emissions, resource choices, and community impacts. Labor organizations may emphasize safety, job quality, and workforce continuity. Local governments may focus on development, tax base, land use, and constituent impacts.

These are possible areas of concern, not a substitute for reading actual positions. Organizations can differ within the same category and can support one element while opposing another. Record what each party filed and why that issue matters to the decision.

A practical meeting preparation checklist

Before a meeting, confirm the docket and latest relevant order; the decision being sought; the requested versus approved amounts; the legal authority; the main evidence disputes; and the customer-impact assumptions. Identify which questions need regulatory, engineering, finance, customer-service, or legal input.

During the meeting, ask what would change the participant’s position. That answer may reveal a missing analysis, a customer protection, a schedule concern, or a genuine policy disagreement. Afterward, record the commitment, responsible person, and next date. Avoid attributing an organizational position based only on an informal comment from one participant.

Plain language explanations for common conversations

For a legislator asking why grid spending does not solve a capacity shortfall: “The local grid moves electricity to customers. The resource question is whether enough dependable electricity can be supplied when demand is high. We need to examine both, because a stronger local network cannot deliver power that is unavailable.”

For a customer asking why a supply increase appears on the utility bill: “The bill collects charges for several services. The supply portion pays for electricity. The delivery portion pays for the system that brings it to your property. We need to identify which portion changed and whether your usage also changed.”

For an economic-development partner asking whether a large customer protects other ratepayers: “We need to examine the service commitment, the facilities required, the financial security, and the broader system effects. A deposit is one protection; its amount and terms determine what risk it actually covers.”

Ameren Illinois in focus: a reusable briefing structure

Use six sentences: (1) the decision and deadline; (2) Ameren's request or position; (3) the customer problem; (4) the strongest supporting evidence; (5) the principal challenge or uncertainty; and (6) the action leadership must take. Attach the source and status to the key claim. This format is an analytical tool, not an official company position.

Hypothetical example: “The pending decision concerns a proposed substation serving existing customers and a prospective manufacturer. Ameren seeks approval for the investment and applicable recovery. The forecast shows a constraint if the manufacturer reaches its planned load. The case should demonstrate that constraint and the expected reliability benefits. The largest uncertainty is the customer's ramp date and the cost exposure if it slips. Leadership needs a recommendation on staging and financial protections before making commitments.”

Learning tool: switch audiences without changing facts

For a customer, start with service and bill effects. For an elected official, connect community needs, statutory authority, and implementation. For an executive, identify options, timing, risk, and the requested decision. Technical depth changes; the underlying fact and status do not.

Practice aloud in 60 seconds. First explain the decision without acronyms. Second add one number with its unit and time period. Third say what remains uncertain. Evaluate only one criterion on the first attempt: could the listener state the decision back to you? Repeat after correcting the most confusing sentence.

Module 16: Keeping the Record Current

What you will learn

Maintain a dated claim record; refresh changing facts; use retrieval practice and an error log.

Use a source hierarchy

Start with enacted law for legal requirements, final orders for Commission decisions, effective tariffs for service terms and rates, and official procurement results for awards. Use agency reports for their analysis and assumptions. Use party filings for the positions they present. News coverage is useful for context, but an important legal or financial claim should lead back to the underlying record.

When two sources seem inconsistent, compare their dates and the question each answers. An initial plan, a modified plan, and a final compliance filing may all exist. A hearing schedule may change. A published summary may simplify a number that has a narrower definition in the order.

Record the information needed to reproduce a claim

For every material fact, keep the document title, author or issuing body, date, docket, relevant page or section, and a link. For numerical claims, add units, geography, period, and baseline. For projected customer impacts, retain the usage and rate assumptions.

This practice is especially valuable when a number moves into a speech or executive deck. A short phrase can lose the conditions that made the original claim accurate. The source record lets the team restore those conditions quickly.
